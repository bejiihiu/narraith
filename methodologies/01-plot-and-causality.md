## E1 — Premise & Dramatic Question

### 1. Purpose

Convert material into a pressure that generates events, and a question only the final decision can answer. A premise is neither a subject nor a situation: it is a proposition the story tests by making someone pay for acting on it `[theory]`. The output is five lines, not a synopsis. If the five lines cannot be written, drafting will not supply them — it will only produce prose that circles the missing decision.

### 2. When to use and when not

Use it at the start of anything larger than a single scene; when a draft reads as a mood or a tour of a place; when the ending arrives and the story has been *about* something without testing it; when two ideas are competing for the same pages and both are losing.

Do not use it on a fragment under roughly 300 words — a fragment can be a pure situation and be good. Do not use it on a scene whose premise is already fixed by a larger work; run E8 instead. Do not use it on a deliberate mood piece or prose poem, where the pressure is atmospheric and the question is not a decision. And do not retrofit a premise onto a finished draft you are attached to: a premise discovered after the fact usually describes the draft you wrote, not the one you meant.

### 3. Inputs

- The material: a situation, an image, a character, a request.
- The intended length. Length sets how many pressures the premise can carry: one, for anything short `[hypothesis]`.
- The ending you are drawn to, if any. With an ending and no premise, work backward — the ending is the consequence, so ask what pressure would make it the only defensible outcome.
- Fixed constraints from the user: genre, POV, period, an event that must occur.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Write the topic and put it aside.** One noun phrase — grief, inheritance, a bad marriage. A topic can be illustrated; it cannot be tested.
2. **Name the pressure in one sentence.** Who is pushed, by what, toward what act they would not otherwise perform. The pressure needs a deadline: a hearing, a harvest, a debt due, a visit ending. Without a clock, the pressure is weather.
3. **Justify the pressure from inside itself.** One sentence in the opposing force's own voice, explaining why it is right by its own account (SKILL.md, decision 3). A pressure without this produces a villain, and a villain produces melodrama.
4. **Write the premise as a causal proposition: "X leads to Y because Z."** X is an act or a condition under pressure, with an agent. Y is a consequence someone pays for. Z is the mechanism. Test Z by reading it alone, without "because": if it is an instruction ("people must be honest"), it is a moral, not a mechanism. Z must name what transfers, what is spent, or what becomes visible.
5. **Write the dramatic question as a decision, not a discovery.** "Will X [specific act] before [specific condition]?" Both answers must stay live until the final scene. A question answered by a discovery ("will she learn the truth?") is a promise, and stories keep those promises by default.
6. **Run the premise-answers-the-question test.** If Z already implies the answer, the question is rhetorical. *Loyalty to a liar makes you an accomplice* answers *will loyalty make her complicit?* Move the question from the thesis to the act: not whether loyalty corrupts her, but whether she signs the statement that protects him.
7. **Run the three-scene test.** Without consulting the setting, write three scene premises the question forces: one approach, one evasion, one payment. All three must be different kinds of attempt. Fewer than three means the question is thin; three versions of the same attempt mean it is narrow.
8. **Run the relocation test.** Move the story to another place and another decade. If the chain survives intact, the premise is a setting. The setting must supply the mechanism in Z: the town's gossip is the enforcement, the mill's shift pattern is the alibi, the inheritance law is the trap.
9. **Run the substitution test.** Replace the protagonist with someone who shares the goal. If the chain survives, the protagonist is a vehicle. The premise must require this person's particular flaw, debt, or history.
10. **Declare the premise–ending relation before drafting:** confirms, refutes, or complicates (narrative-theory §8). "Indifferent" is not a fourth option; it is the failure this step exists to catch.
11. **Record five lines and stop.** Pressure, premise, question, three forced scenes, relation. Anything longer is a plan that will compete with the draft.

#### The three-scene test, worked

Take the Mira premise above. Approach: she asks the solicitor to delay probate until her father can be assessed, and the solicitor asks her to sign a statement that she is unfit to manage anything. Evasion: she agrees to run the shop for six months so the deed cannot be changed while she is there. Payment: she signs the deed to keep the peace, and the peace lasts eleven days. Three different moves, all forced by the same question, none of them requiring the lighthouse.

#### Both answers

Before drafting, write the last scene twice: once as if the answer is yes, once as if it is no. Both must be possible from the material and both must cost something. This is not a demand for two endings in the text; it is a check that the question is live. If the no-version is unthinkable, you have written a wish with obstacles. If the yes-version is free, you have written a punishment with a delay. *Will Mira sign?* Yes: she keeps the shop and loses the town's belief that she was wronged. No: she keeps her name and loses the only roof her mother died under. Both pay.

#### On a finished draft

Read the first scene and the last scene. Write the proposition each one assumes. If the two propositions differ, the draft has no premise, only a direction of travel — and revision cannot fix that at the level of sentences. Then read the middle: if it contains a scene the question does not force, that scene is either a subplot needing its own question or material from another story.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- X has no agent: it is a condition, an era, or a mood.
- Z is a moral, an evaluation ("because it is wrong"), or a tautology ("because that is what happens").
- The question can be answered at the midpoint, or from the premise alone.
- Fewer than three forced scenes, or three scenes that are the same move.
- The relocation test passes: the story survives a change of place intact.
- The substitution test passes: any protagonist would do.
- The premise–ending relation is indifferent.
- The premise reads as a thesis for an essay with characters attached. That is an L4 problem arriving early.

One failure is survivable at draft stage. Two mean the premise is not yet a premise, and the pages written on it are evidence about the material, not about the story.

### 6. Typical errors

- **Topic trap.** *Grief becomes violence.* No agent, no mechanism, no cost. It can be illustrated for fifty pages and never tested. The topic is what the story is about; the premise is what it asserts about it.
- **Setting trap.** *A lighthouse keeper's last winter* is a place and a season. It becomes a premise only when the keeper must choose something the winter makes impossible — and the winter is what makes it impossible.
- **Thesis trap.** *Power corrupts* is a moral. A story built on it illustrates; a story built on a mechanism tests. The difference is whether the ending can come out the other way and still be true.
- **Question as plot summary.** *Will she discover the truth about her father?* In a story that poses it, yes. Convert the discovery into a decision: what she does with it, and what it costs to do it.
- **Double premise.** Two pressures of equal weight; the story serves neither because it keeps switching. Choose one, or make the second the price of the first.
- **Premise that needs the ending to make sense.** If the proposition only becomes true once the last scene is known, the story is a delivery mechanism for a twist rather than a test of a claim.
- **The premise that is a wish.** *If she tells the truth, everything will be fine* is a hope, not a proposition; it has no mechanism because nothing resists it. Premises need a Z that bites.

### 7. Output format

```
Pressure:        <who, pushed by what, toward what act, by when>
Premise:         X leads to Y because Z
Question:        Will <agent> <act> before <condition>?
Forced scenes:   1 <approach> / 2 <evasion> / 3 <payment>
Ending relation: confirms | refutes | complicates
Verdict:         pass | fail at <test number> — <one-line reason>
```

Keep it to these six lines. The verdict line is not decoration: naming the failed test is what prevents a rewrite of the same premise with new nouns.

### 8. Micro-example

**Failed:** *In a lighthouse town, a woman returns to care for her father.* Relocate it to a farm and nothing breaks. That is a setting, and the question ("will she stay?") is answered by the material's own mood of reconciliation.

**Repaired:** *Mira stays to nurse the father who wrote her out of the will, because leaving would confirm the version of her he has been telling the town for nine years.* Now the question is a decision — will she sign the deed that leaves the house in his name before he dies? The town is no longer the premise; it is the audience for the lie, and the lie is the mechanism.

## E2 — Causal Spine / Plot Causality

### 1. Purpose

Build a chain in which each event is caused by a choice, and locate the link that carries the ending. The chain is not the plot's outline; it is the argument that the ending was earned. A spine that only sequences events produces a synopsis. A spine that shows why each event could not have been otherwise produces a story.

### 2. When to use and when not

Use it whenever events feel arranged; when a beta reader says "convenient"; when a climax depends on an arrival, a letter, a death, or a stranger; when a subplot needs to justify its pages; when an outline has nine beats and no reason for the order.

Do not use it on a story whose interest is deliberately episodic — a picaresque, a fragment sequence, a mood piece — where causal tightness would falsify the form. Do not use it to police a first draft's digressions; the spine is a diagnostic on structure, not a licence to delete everything that does not advance. And do not use it as a beat template: a filled sequence of functions is the formulaic artifact this skill exists to prevent (narrative-theory §12).

### 3. Inputs

- The fabula: events in chronological order, as bare sentences.
- The premise and question from E1.
- Each protagonist's want and the cost they will accept (E3 if the motive is the problem).
- The ending, stated as an outcome, not a scene.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Write 5–9 sentences, chronological, active, named agents.** No style, no interiority. *Nadia agrees to interpret her brother's interview.* If the story will not compress to nine sentences, it is not yet understood — compress again before diagnosing.
2. **Tag every event by its source.** **C** — a choice by an agent who pays for it. **A** — an accident. **E** — an external arrival: a message, a person, an authority, weather.
3. **Apply the road rule.** An accident or arrival may close a road; it may never open one. Any A or E that removes an obstacle is either converted into a choice (someone decides to exploit it) or converted into a cost (it makes the situation worse). Write the replacement sentence, not a note.
4. **Insert connectives between every pair.** Only *therefore* and *but* are allowed. *And then* means you have a sequence; convert it or delete the event.
5. **Read the chain aloud and mark the disguised *and then*.** This is the node where a *therefore* is doing grammatical work but no causal work — the reader must supply a motive you have not given. That is the break.
6. **Run the counterfactual test on every node.** Remove it. Does the ending change? If not, the event is texture: cut it, merge it, or give it a consequence. A node that survives removal but occupies a scene is a scene with no job.
7. **Run the substitution test.** Replace the protagonist with someone who shares the goal. If the chain survives, the agency is decorative: the events were going to happen anyway and the protagonist was standing nearby.
8. **Name the cost at every C.** *What did this choice cost, and to whom?* A C without a cost is a preference, not a decision.
9. **Mark the load-bearing node.** Usually one, sometimes two. State what every later event inherits from it. If nothing inherits, you have found a scene, not a spine.
10. **Write the repaired chain and re-read it once.** Do not proceed to prose until every pair reads as *therefore* or *but*.

#### Worked spine — seven nodes

| # | Event | Tag | Cost paid | Remove it and… |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Nadia agrees to interpret her brother's asylum interview | C | her professional neutrality; the licence forbids kin | no interview; the story ends at the door |
| 2 | **but** he testifies to a version that protects their cousin | C (brother) | exposes the family to a perjury finding | the interview is routine; nothing is at stake |
| 3 | **therefore** she translates it faithfully, in front of an officer writing it down | C | her name is now on a false record | she protects him and the story becomes a favour, not a test |
| 4 | **therefore** the officer asks the follow-up only the true story answers — who drove them to the border | E (complicates) | — it closes the easy exit | node 5 has no trigger |
| 5 | **but** she moves the date one year, putting the crossing outside the window under review | C | her licence, if the recording is checked; her integrity, now | the case fails and nodes 6–7 cannot happen |
| 6 | **therefore** the interview passes, and the caseworker assigns her to the wife's interview on Thursday | consequence + E | she is now the family's instrument | the terminal choice disappears |
| 7 | **but** the wife was never rehearsed, so she will answer the date truthfully unless Nadia coaches her | C (terminal) | either her brother's permit or the record | — |

Read the connectives: 1→2 *but*, 2→3 *therefore*, 3→4 *therefore*, 4→5 *but*, 5→6 *therefore*, 6→7 *but*. Node 4 is an arrival, and it is legal because it *complicates*: the officer's question closes an exit and hands the choice back to Nadia. Nodes 3, 5 and 7 are the choices that carry the ending; remove any one and the outcome changes. Node 1 is load-bearing in the weaker sense: it is the condition that makes the rest possible.

#### Broken spine — exact repair

**Broken:** *The detective finds the ledger because a neighbour brings it round.* Tag E, solving. The plot needed the ledger; the neighbour supplied it; nobody paid.

**Repair, exact sentence:** *The neighbour called the number on the card the detective had left in every letterbox on the second floor.* The card was planted in scene 2 and cost the detective an afternoon he did not have. The arrival is now a return on a prior choice, so it complicates rather than solves — and the call can carry a price: she wants to know whether the card means her son is a suspect.

Two alternatives, for other shapes of the same problem. **Convert to cost:** she brings the ledger and asks for money, so the detective must choose between the evidence and the bribe. **Convert to concealment:** the ledger turns up because the detective broke in earlier, which means using it now exposes him. Each keeps the arrival and moves the agency back to the protagonist.

#### Attaching a second line

A subplot attaches to the spine at a node, not to the story in general. Name the node where it joins, and the cost it imposes on the main line there. If the second line never changes what the protagonist must choose, it is a parallel story rather than a subplot, and it belongs to a different book. Two lines that both resolve in the same scene are one line told twice; stagger the resolutions so that the first one raises the price of the second.

#### The spine at three resolutions

The same spine runs at different grain. In a short story, seven nodes is already too many: five is the working number, and the load-bearing node is usually second or third `[hypothesis]`. In a chapter, the spine is local — the chapter's own therefore/but chain, with the novel's spine as its given. In a novel, keep one spine of nine to twelve nodes for the whole and a separate five-node spine per act; if an act's spine does not change the whole's load-bearing node or its cost, the act is a detour. The test is the same at every scale: remove the node and ask whether the ending at that scale changes.

#### The one-sentence spine

Before writing the numbered chain, compress it to one sentence with three clauses: *Because Nadia cannot refuse her mother, she interprets for her brother; therefore she must lie on a record; but the record is the only thing that can save him.* If the sentence cannot be built, the spine has no through-line and the numbered list will be a sequence of scenes. Keep the sentence and use it as the test for every new event: an event that cannot be attached to one of the three clauses is a subplot or a cut.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- Any A or E that removes an obstacle rather than creating one.
- More than one surviving *and then*.
- A break where the reader must invent the motive.
- An event whose removal leaves the ending unchanged while the event still occupies a scene.
- A protagonist who can be swapped without consequence.
- A C with no named cost.
- A climax whose decisive act is performed by someone introduced after the midpoint.
- A chain that requires a character to miss something obvious so the plot can proceed.

### 6. Typical errors

- **The convenient arrival.** A letter, a death, a stranger, a phone call at the exact moment the plot needs it. The repair is never to delete the arrival; it is to make someone's earlier choice the reason it arrives now.
- **Retrospective causation.** The reason for an event is supplied two scenes later in dialogue. Explanation after the fact can supply *motive*; it can never supply *cause*. If a character must explain why something happened, an agent's choice did not produce it.
- **Agency in the interior only.** The character decides something and the plot proceeds as though the decision had force, while nothing in the world changes because of it. Interior decision without a changed external state is a wish.
- **The clean single chain.** `[data: StoryScope]` 79% of model-written stories carry no subplot — and 57% of human ones do not either — so the single tidy line is the measured default, not a defect. That does not make a subplot obligatory. A second line earns its place when it applies the same mechanism in Z to a different person and collides with the main line at a cost.
- **Node inflation.** Nine nodes where five carry the story; the other four exist because the outline had slots. Beats are not evidence of structure.
- **The chain that requires stupidity.** A character fails to take an obvious action so the plot can continue. The repair is not to make them stupid sooner; it is to make the obvious action unavailable, expensive, or forbidden by something they have already committed to.

### 7. Output format

```
1. C  <event>            cause: <...>   cost: <...>   remove → <what changes>
   therefore
2. C  <event>            ...
   but
3. E  <event>            complicates only: <what it closes>
   therefore
...
Break:        node <n> — <the motive the reader must invent>
Repair:       <exact replacement sentence>
Load-bearing: node <n> — inherited by <nodes>
Verdict:      pass | fail — <count> solving accidents, <count> and-thens
```

### 8. Micro-example

**Failed:** *The van breaks down, so Des misses the buyer and keeps the money.* The breakdown does the work of an ending, and Des does nothing to deserve the outcome.

**Repaired:** the breakdown stays, but it only removes the option. Des then walks the last four miles with the cash inside his coat, because the buyer's signature is the only thing standing between his daughter and the fee deadline. That walk is the choice that costs him the job: he arrives sweating, and the buyer reads desperation as leverage. An accident closed the road; a decision walked through it, and the price was the deal.

## E6 — Information Flow & Revelation

### 1. Purpose

Decide who knows what, when, and at what price — then place each revelation where its cost can be paid. Information is not a reward for reading; it is a change in the balance between people. A revelation that rearranges facts without rearranging relationships is bookkeeping, and readers feel the difference even when they cannot name it.

### 2. When to use and when not

Use it for any story with a secret, a twist, a misunderstanding, a trial, a betrayal, a hidden parent, a concealed debt; whenever a draft's middle sags because the reader is waiting; whenever a reveal lands flat; whenever a character acts on knowledge they were never given (that is also E13's knowledge matrix, and the two checks should agree).

Do not use it on a story with nothing withheld — a straight situational piece, a procedural whose interest is competence, a scene whose whole point is transparency. Do not use it to add mystery to a draft that lacks pressure; withheld information on top of no stake produces fog, not suspense. And do not use it to justify a narrator who lies: that is E15, and it has stricter requirements than a ledger.

### 3. Inputs

- The fact list: identity, cause, debt, betrayal, paternity, diagnosis, the thing in the box.
- The scene order, or the spine from E2.
- Each scene's POV character.
- The intended reader experience at each act: what should the reader suspect, dread, want?

### 4. Procedure

1. **List the facts the story depends on and give each an ID.** F1, F2, F3. Five to nine is usual for a short story. A fact that no one acts on is not a fact for this purpose — it is detail.
2. **Build the three-column ledger, one row per fact.** *Reader knows* / *protagonist knows* / *other characters know*. Fill it at the start, at the midpoint, and at the end; three snapshots catch more than a continuous table and take a third of the time.
3. **Mark each gap by type.**
   - *Dramatic irony* — reader knows more than the character. Produces dread and double meaning. Requires the reader to have the fact before the scene uses it.
   - *Mystery* — the reader knows a question exists and does not know the answer. Requires the question to be posed explicitly; an unposed question is not a mystery, it is missing information.
   - *Suspense* — reader knows more than the character about an imminent threat. Requires the threat to be concrete and time-bound, or it decays into vague unease.
   - *Surprise* — the reader knows less than everyone, briefly. Works on events. A withheld fact about the past that reinterprets everything is not a surprise; it is a revelation and needs a plant.
4. **Write the cost of each revelation in one sentence.** *Learning F3 costs Mira her only remaining ally.* Sort the revelations by that cost, descending. Cheap revelations go early; the most expensive goes last, or next to last if the final beat is the consequence of paying it.
5. **Apply the cost test to each reveal.** Does it change a relationship or force a decision inside the scene where it lands? If it only adds information, attach it to a person who must now act on it, or demote it to a beat in a scene that turns on something else.
6. **Audit the withholding.** Information should be withheld from a *character*, not from the reader without cause. When the POV narrator knows a fact and the reader does not, name the reason inside the narration: the narrator is writing for someone, is ashamed, does not yet understand its importance, or finds it too ordinary to mention. Choose one and keep it consistent. No reason means the withholding is a trick, and re-readers feel cheated even when first-time readers do not.
7. **Plant every revelation about the past.** One concrete marker earlier: an object, an anomaly someone notices, a phrase repeated without explanation, a date that does not fit (narrative-theory §13). List the plant and its location next to the reveal. An unplanted past fact reads as authorial convenience regardless of how well it is written.
8. **Design the re-reading effect in one sentence.** *Scene 2's birthday card read as affection; it now reads as a signature he practised.* If you cannot write that sentence, the reveal is an addition, not a revelation. The strongest version makes an earlier kindness look like a bribe or an earlier joke look like a threat.
9. **Check the reveal against the dramatic question.** If a revelation answers the question, the story is over — so either the question must be different from the one the reveal answers, or the reveal must arrive at the final decision.
10. **Check the holder.** Every fact needs someone whose behaviour depends on it. A fact that no character's conduct reflects does not need to be in the story, however interesting it is.
11. **Place each reveal on a scene boundary, not mid-beat.** A reveal needs the next thirty seconds of reaction on the page, or it arrives and the scene moves on, which teaches the reader that revelations do not matter.

#### A worked ledger, four facts

| Fact | Reader knows @ | Protagonist knows @ | Others know @ | Gap type | Cost of learning | Plant | Who must now act |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 the deed is forged | 7 | 7 | solicitor all along | mystery → irony | her inheritance, her name on a false document | F1b | Mira: expose or sign |
| F2 the uncle signed it | 9 | 9 | uncle | mystery | the only relative who defended her | card, scene 2 | Mira: confront before the funeral |
| F3 the uncle paid the debt | 3 | 12 | uncle, solicitor | irony (reader early) | nothing yet — held for the cost at 9 | bank slip, scene 5 | the uncle: keep it hidden |
| F4 the mother knew | never on page | 4 | mother | — | — | — | cut: no one acts on it |

The table does three jobs at once. F4 shows a fact with no holder — cut it. F3 shows a fact the reader gets early, which converts the uncle's kindness into bribery before anyone says so, and that is what makes F2 cost something when it lands. F1 and F2 are two facts two scenes apart, which is the right density for a short story: one revelation per turn, not one per page.

#### Partial payments

A mystery that runs longer than a scene must pay something on the way, or the reader stops holding the question. The payment is not the answer; it is a narrower question, a false answer that costs someone, or a fact that changes what the question means. Schedule three: one in the first quarter, one at the midpoint, one before the final revelation. The midpoint payment should be wrong in a way the reader can later see was wrong — that is what makes the final answer feel earned rather than supplied.

#### After the reveal

The scene after a revelation is where most drafts lose the gain: the reveal lands, everyone reacts, and the next scene returns to the previous business. Instead, the next scene must show the new state operating — someone acts on what they now know, or refuses to. One scene of aftermath, then the changed relationship has to be visible in ordinary behaviour. If the aftermath scene could have been written before the reveal, the reveal did not change the story, only the reader's notes.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- A revelation that changes only the reader's knowledge, while no character's state changes in the scene.
- A reveal about the past with no earlier plant.
- The most expensive revelation placed early because it was vivid.
- Information withheld from the reader with no in-narration cause.
- No re-reading sentence available.
- Two or more mysteries opened and neither partially answered for longer than a scene.
- A revelation delivered by a character with nothing at stake in telling it — the confession with no cost.
- A fact whose holder never acts on it.
- Knowledge in the ledger that contradicts what a character does in the draft.

### 6. Typical errors

- **The costless revelation.** *I'm your sister* delivered as a fact rather than as a claim on behaviour. Nothing is demanded, so nothing changes; the reader files it and moves on.
- **The withheld memory.** The first-person narrator conceals their own trauma to preserve a twist. On re-read, the narrator lied for the author's benefit, and the voice is retroactively untrustworthy in the wrong way — not bent by character, bent by craft.
- **The explain-twice reveal.** The scene shows it, a character summarises it, the narration comments. One channel. The second and third are the author distrusting the first.
- **The revelation that reorganises facts but not relationships.** *The will was forged* changes who owns what. Unless someone must now act differently toward someone else, it is paperwork.
- **Fog mistaken for mystery.** Three unanswered questions with no partial payments read as confusion, not suspense. Answer something small on the way to answering something large.
- **Withholding the question itself.** The reader cannot want an answer to a question they have not been asked. Withhold the answer, never the question.

### 7. Output format

```
Fact | Reader knows @ | Protagonist knows @ | Others know @ | Gap type | Cost of learning | Plant | Who must now act | Re-read line
```

Below the table, two lines:

```
Withholding reason (if reader < POV): <one of the four, named>
Question check: reveal <n> answers <question?> — position relative to final decision: <...>
```

The table is allowed to be ugly. It is a working instrument, not a deliverable, and it should be deleted from the reply unless the user asked for planning artifacts.

### 8. Micro-example

**Failed:** *It turned out her uncle had signed the papers years ago, which explained everything.* The reader learns a fact; nobody pays; the phrase "explained everything" does the work that a scene should have done.

**Repaired:** plant the uncle's handwriting on a birthday card in scene two. In scene seven, the niece recognises it on the deed. The recognition costs her the only relative who defended her, and it forces a choice before the funeral: expose him or accept the house. The card read as affection; it now reads as a signature he practised until it looked like one. That sentence is the test — if no earlier scene means something else afterward, you wrote an addition, not a revelation.

## How these engines combine

E1 → E2 → E6 is the default order and the only one that reliably holds. E1 fixes the proposition and the decision; E2 builds the chain that makes the decision necessary and finds which node carries it; E6 decides when the reader is allowed to know what, which is the same chain viewed from the reader's side. Run them in that order and each one has an input: E2's ending must answer E1's question, and E6's final revelation must land at or after the decision E1 named.

Two loops run backward. If E2 cannot produce a chain, the premise is usually a setting (E1 step 8) — return to E1 rather than inventing events. If E6's ledger shows that a revelation costs nothing, the missing thing is usually a relationship under pressure, which is E5's territory, not E6's; do not patch it by adding a second secret.

E2 and E6 share one object: the *plant*. In E2 a plant is a choice made early that constrains a later choice; in E6 it is a concrete detail that makes a later revelation legible. The same scene often does both, which is why a spine with no plants usually has a revelation with no setup, and a revelation with no setup is usually sitting on a spine whose early nodes were texture.

E6 also constrains E7. Order is the cheapest revelation mechanism available: telling the reader a fact before or after a scene is a decision about what the scene means. If the information plan is settled, the temporal plan mostly writes itself; if it is not, no amount of reordering will produce suspense, only confusion. E8 takes both as given: a scene's turn is usually a revelation or a decision, and its cost is the cost E6 priced.

Do not run all three at full resolution on a short story. Under about 2,000 words, E1 plus one pass of E6 is enough `[hypothesis]`; E2's counterfactual test on every node is a long-form instrument and produces outline-shaped prose when applied to a single scene.

On an existing draft the order inverts. Run E6 first: the ledger is cheap to fill from scenes that already exist, and it exposes the revelations that cost nothing, which are usually why a draft feels flat without any visible structural fault. Then E2, because a spine assembled from written scenes is easier to test than one invented in the abstract. Run E1 last, as a verdict: state the proposition the finished draft actually tests and compare it with the one the opening promised.

| Symptom | Engine | First move |
|---|---|---|
| The story is "about" something and stops | E1 | write the proposition of the first and last scene; compare |
| A turn feels convenient | E2 | tag the node; convert the accident into a cost |
| The middle sags while the reader waits | E6 | find the mystery with no partial payment |
| A reveal lands and nothing changes | E6 | write the cost sentence; if it has none, cut the reveal |
| The ending answers a question nobody asked | E1 then E2 | check the load-bearing node against the final decision |

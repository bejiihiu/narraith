## E7 — Temporal Architecture

### 1. Purpose

Choose the order in which the reader receives the story, then make every jump in time legible and load-bearing. Order is not decoration on a chronology; it is a claim about what the reader is allowed to ask, and when. Two stories with the same events, delivered in different orders, are different stories — one is a mystery, the other an elegy — and the difference is a decision, not a mood.

### 2. When to use and when not

Use it for any structure with more than one time: a withheld past, a frame, a braid, a reverse, an opening on the consequence. Use it when a flashback feels like an interruption rather than a turn; when readers disagree about when things happened; when the first draft is chronological because you never examined the alternative; when a scene is strong but the sequence is flat.

Do not use it on a story under roughly 800 words where the chronology is the whole point `[hypothesis]` — a short anecdote usually spends more than it earns on a second timeline. Do not use it when the real problem is causality: reordering a chain of *and then* produces a more confusing sequence, not a structure (that is E2). Do not reach for reverse chronology as a bid for seriousness; reverse works when the ending is worth knowing and the question is *how*, not *what*. And do not use it to hide a weak scene — moving a scene in time changes its meaning only if it had one.

### 3. Inputs

- The fabula from E2, numbered: F1, F2, F3. Refer to beats by number, never as "the scene where".
- The dramatic question from E1.
- The information plan from E6: which facts the reader holds at each point.
- Length, format (spoken narration, epistolary, frame), and the reader's patience for reconstruction, which genre sets.
- The narrating situation: who speaks, from when, to whom (narrative-theory §3). This is not the tense.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Number the fabula.** Every later decision refers to numbers. If you cannot number it, the story is not yet sequential enough to reorder.
2. **Write three candidate deliveries as beat lists, not prose.** Chronological; present-with-withheld-past; braid of two lines. Add reverse only if the ending is already strong. For each candidate write one sentence: *this order makes the reader ask ___ before ___*.
3. **Choose by the question.** Which candidate poses the dramatic question earliest and lets it be answered latest? If two tie, choose the simpler — complexity must earn its reconstruction cost.
4. **Fix the entry point.** Take the latest fabula moment that still contains the pressure. Test later: which pressure disappears? Test earlier: what does the reader learn too soon?
5. **Place every shift against a trigger.** A shift arrives because the present line has just raised the question it answers (narrative-theory §2). Write the trigger question in the map. No trigger means the shift is an interruption, however good the material.
6. **Give each shift two anchors.** One object or sensory repeat that exists in both times, and one time marker: a date, an age, a season, a technology, a form of address. Two is the minimum because one can be read as coincidence; three is shouting.
7. **Run the reconstruction check.** At each shift, write one sentence stating what the reader now believes about the chronology. If two readers could write incompatible sentences, the shift is under-signed — add an anchor or move the shift.
8. **Decide what the order hides and what it exposes.** List two facts the order conceals and one it reveals early, with the reason for each. If you cannot name a concealed fact, you have reordered for texture.
9. **Run the chronological counterfactual.** Rewrite the delivery in fabula order and state exactly what is lost: a suspicion, an irony, a question, a rereading. If nothing is lost, use chronology.
10. **Test each flashback: argument or excuse.** Delete it and replace it with one sentence of present behaviour. If the scene still works, the flashback was an excuse — it supplied motive the present scene failed to dramatise. If it collapses, the flashback was an argument: it changed the meaning of something the reader had already seen.
11. **Budget the timelines.** Each line must contain at least one scene that could not exist in another line `[hypothesis]`. A second timeline with no exclusive scene is a flashback wearing a structure.

#### The four common orders

| Order | Shape | Reader asks | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chronological | F1…Fn | what happens next? | no irony; the past cannot reframe the present |
| Withheld past | present line, one or few analepses | what happened that night? | the present must keep raising the question or the past feels like an insert |
| Braid | two lines alternating | how do these connect? | the connection must be causal, not thematic, or it is a collage |
| Reverse | Fn…F1 | how did it come to this? | suspense dies; only irony and inevitability remain, so the ending must be worth arriving at from behind |

#### Reverse order: what it requires

Reverse chronology turns the reader's question from *what happens* to *how it came to this*, and it pays only when the ending is worth arriving at from behind. Three requirements. The final state must be legible in the first scene without explanation. Each earlier scene must gain irony from what the reader already knows — a kindness becomes a warning. And the earliest scene must still contain a surprise, or the form is a countdown to a fact handed over at the door. A reverse that merely withholds the cause is a mystery told backward; a reverse that makes every earlier scene mean something else is a structure.

#### When the order carries the theme

Sometimes the order is the argument: a braid in which the past explains the present's compromise, a frame in which the telling is the narrator's last act. Then the order must be defended as a claim, not as a device. State the sentence it asserts — *the son repeats his father's choice because he never learned what it cost* — and test it. If the sentence holds only in the non-linear order, the structure is doing thematic work. If it holds in chronological order too, the structure is decoration.

#### A worked shift pair

Present line: Mira folds her mother's coat for the charity bag, three days after the funeral. Past line: the same coat on a kitchen chair in 1989, a cigarette burn on the cuff. Trigger: the solicitor has just asked whether her father ever raised a hand to her, and the coat is what she was wearing that night.

Anchors: the burn on the cuff (object, both times) and the shipping forecast on the kitchen radio — the same broadcast, a corded set in 1989 and a digital one in 1998 (marker). Reconstruction sentence: *the coat is from the year her mother left, nine years before the funeral.* Without the burn, the shift reads as a wardrobe coincidence; without the radio's changing hardware, the reader cannot date the kitchen.

#### A worked order map

Use the Mira material. Present line, 1998: the funeral, the solicitor, the deed (F7, F9, F12). Past line, 1989: the coat, the departure, the letter (F2, F3, F5). Entry on F7, the solicitor's question about violence. Shift 1 to F3 at the coat, triggered by that question; shift 2 to F5 at the letter, triggered by the date on the deed.

Concealed by the order: that her mother left without the coat, and that her father kept the letter. Exposed early: that the deed exists. Reason: the reader must know there is a document to fear before caring who signed it. The chronological counterfactual loses the irony that the coat Mira is folding is the one her mother chose not to take — a fact the present line can use without ever explaining it.

#### Two timelines, one clock

Braided timelines drift. The past line runs at its own pace, the present at another, and halfway through the book the reader cannot tell whether the past has caught up. Fix it with one clock: a dated event in the present that the past is moving toward — a hearing, a birth, a move — and a stated interval between the lines. Then the reader can always place how far behind they are, and a gap in the past line reads as significance rather than as a mistake. When the lines meet, the meeting should be an event the present has been waiting for, not a coincidence of chapter numbering.

#### Order is not tense

Tense is grammar; order is architecture. A present-tense narration can be a subsequent narration imitating immediacy, and the reader will feel the seam wherever the imitation breaks (narrative-theory §3). Decide the narrating situation first — who is speaking, from when, to whom — then let it constrain knowledge. A narrator speaking after the events may foreshadow. A narrator speaking during them may not know what the next hour holds, and a sentence that does is a break the reader will forgive once.

#### Speed: assigning duration

Every beat has a speed, and a draft that has only two speeds has two gears where it needs five (narrative-theory §2 names them: ellipsis, summary, scene, stretch, pause). Assign the speed before writing the beat, by function:

| Function of the beat | Speed | What goes wrong otherwise |
|---|---|---|
| a period holds no decision | ellipsis or summary | scene-speed on transport reads as padding |
| a decision under pressure | scene, or stretch at the instant of choice | summary on a decision steals the story's only scene |
| the object being decided about | a brief pause | pause before the decision is atmosphere; after it, consequence |
| routine that is about to break | iterative | singulative routine reads as a list of days |

The rule: the slowest speed in a section sits on the decision, the fastest on the transport between decisions. A section at scene speed throughout has no shape. A section in summary throughout has no scene.

#### Frequency as a signal

Frequency is the cheapest structural instrument available. Run a stretch in the iterative — *most evenings he ate at the counter and said nothing* — then break into the singulative at the moment routine fails: *on the last evening he asked her name.* The switch alone tells the reader this is the day it changed, without a word of explanation. The second use is repetition: the same event told twice is not redundancy when the second telling knows something the first did not. Same event, different knowledge, is the mechanism of revelation (E6). Repetition that knows nothing new is a continuity error wearing style.

#### Frames

A frame is a second narrating situation wrapped around the first: someone tells the story, writes it, or remembers it. It costs the reader an entry and an exit, so it must buy something the inner story cannot buy alone — a reason the story is told now, a listener who changes the telling, or an outcome the narrator already knows and cannot avoid. If the frame's narrator has nothing at stake in the telling, delete the frame and start inside. If the listener could be removed without changing a word, they were never a listener.

#### Flashback or summary

A flashback and a summary can carry the same information; choose by what the reader must feel. Summary reports; flashback dramatises, and dramatising costs pages and attention. If the texture of the past matters — a voice, a room, a gesture the present repeats — dramatise it. If only the fact matters, summarise it in the present line's voice. The commonest error is dramatising backstory the reader needs only as a fact, which produces a well-written flashback that stops the story.

#### Short transitions

A shift does not always need a section break. A white space, a tense change, a repeated noun, or a change in the time marker can carry the reader across years in one line. What a line-level transition cannot do is change the scene's pressure: if the reader needs to know how much time passed, say so; if they need to feel it, do not. At the join, can the reader write the reconstruction sentence? If yes, the transition is doing its work. If not, the join needs a break, an anchor, or both.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- A shift with no trigger question in the line it interrupts.
- Fewer than two anchors per shift, or three or more competing ones.
- Incompatible reconstructions available at the same shift.
- No fact concealed by the order.
- The chronological counterfactual loses nothing.
- A flashback that supplies motive the present scene failed to dramatise.
- A second timeline with no exclusive scene.
- The order answers the dramatic question before the final decision.
- Reverse chronology used where the ending is not worth knowing.
- A frame whose narrator has nothing at stake in telling the story.

### 6. Typical errors

- **The vivid opening.** Start on the most dramatic scene, then cut to "three weeks earlier". The flashback becomes a waiting room: the reader has seen the fire and now wants the cause, not the marriage. The vivid scene must raise the question the past answers, and it must be a question about meaning rather than sequence.
- **The informative flashback.** Backstory delivered because the reader "needs to know". The tell is the absence of a trigger: nothing in the present asked. If the information is genuinely needed, make the present scene need it first.
- **The braid that is a collage.** Two timelines joined by theme — both about mothers, both about water — rather than by causation. The reader waits for a connection that never becomes one, and the ending arrives without the two lines having touched.
- **The reverse that kills suspense.** If the reader knows the ending, the middle must offer irony or inevitability. Otherwise it is a summary in slow motion, and the reader's only remaining question is how long it will take.
- **Anchor inflation.** Three dates, two songs, a newspaper headline in the first paragraph of every flashback. The reader is being shouted at, and the prose reads as a set of labels.
- **The frame that frames nothing.** A narrator telling the story to someone who never matters. A frame costs the narrator something or it is a preface; if the listener can be deleted without consequence, delete them.
- **Chronology by default.** Deliberately linear is a legitimate and often superior choice. Linear because you never examined the alternative is the modal answer, and it usually shows as a first act that exists to deliver information.

### 7. Output format

```
Line A (present, <year>):  F7 → F9 → F12
Line B (past, <year>):     F2 → F3 → F5
Shift 1: A(F9) → B(F3)   trigger: <question raised in F9>
                         anchors: <object> / <marker>
                         reconstruction: <one sentence>
Shift 2: B(F5) → A(F12)  trigger: <...>
Hidden by order: <two facts>    Exposed early: <one fact>    Why: <...>
Chronological counterfactual loses: <...>

```

The map is a working instrument. Do not paste it into the fiction, and do not show it unless the user asked for planning artifacts.

### 8. Micro-example

**Failed:** a story opens with a funeral, cuts to "three weeks earlier" because the funeral is vivid, and the reader spends the first flashback waiting to return. The past answers a question nobody asked.

**Repaired:** the present line raises a question first — why the sister was not invited — and the flashback answers that question rather than the writer's need for a strong opening. Two anchors carry the shift: the same coat, and a hymn still audible in the present scene. The reconstruction sentence is one line: *the funeral is after the affair and before the will.* At any shift where you cannot write that sentence, the order is hiding the story from its reader.

## E8 — Scene Construction

### 1. Purpose

Build a scene as a unit of change under pressure, then test it before the prose is written. A scene is not a unit of length. It is a decision made by someone at a cost, and everything else — description, dialogue, interiority — is the texture of that decision. The output is a scene card and a pass or fail on the six gates in `SKILL.md`, with cost and last line as sub-tests inside gates 2 and 3.

### 2. When to use and when not

Use it on every scene you write, and on every scene you suspect: the one that feels flat, the one that is 2,000 words for a 200-word decision, the one whose ending you keep rewriting, the chapter that is a series of conversations in which nobody wants anything.

Do not use it on summary. A paragraph that transports the reader across a month is not a failed scene; it is summary doing its job, and the rule for when to use which is below. Do not use it on a fragment of description inside a scene that turns — texture is allowed to have no agenda. Do not use it to justify a sequel: a sequel has a different shape, and if it produces no decision it is not a sequel but a pause.

### 3. Inputs

- The scene's job in the spine (E2 node) and its question's place in the information plan (E6).
- What the POV character wants from a specific person, in this encounter.
- The entering state: knowledge, trust, power, possibility, and the value's charge.
- The value pair the scene will move (narrative-theory §6).
- A length budget in words, not pages.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Name the scene's question in one line.** *Will Ilias get the hire's signature before the shift bell?* Concrete, present, this encounter. If it cannot be phrased with two live answers, the scene has a topic instead of a question.
2. **Name the entering value and its charge.** A pair — trust/betrayal, honesty/complicity, hope/despair — and `+` or `−`. If no pair applies, the scene has no stake yet.
3. **Name who wants what from whom.** Two agendas, each a verb, an object, and an obstacle: *Ilias wants the signature without explaining the date; the hire wants to know why the report says Tuesday.* If one person wants nothing, the scene is a request or a report. Add an agenda or cut the scene.
4. **Write the turn as a decision, or a discovery that forces one.** If the turn is "she realised", attach the realisation to the line or the event that produced it. The turn must be produced by someone in the room, not by an arrival (E2's road rule applies inside scenes too).
5. **Name the cost.** What the turn costs the person who turns, and to whom. One sentence with a loser in it. *He tells the truth about the date and loses the easy signature, because now the hire can refuse knowingly.*
6. **Write the exit state.** What is different: knowledge, trust, power, possibility. Then the value's charge at exit. Same charge as entry means the scene must be marked as tested-and-refused, or cut.
7. **Run the last-line test and the first-paragraph test.** The last line must land on the change. Read the final three sentences: if the change happens in the first and the other two comment, cut them. Then delete the opening paragraph: if nothing is lost, it was throat-clearing.
8. **Check the mode — scene or sequel.** Scene: goal → conflict → setback. Sequel: reaction → dilemma → decision. A sequel earns its pages only by producing a decision that changes the next scene's goal; otherwise compress it to a paragraph.
9. **Run the modal test on the turn.** Given this person, this pressure, this obstacle — would a competent re-writer produce this turn? If yes, the turn is the default. Change who wants what, or change the cost, and re-run (E11 owns the test; run it here, before the prose exists).
10. **Fill the card and stop.** Six lines, two annotations. A card longer than the scene it describes is a plan competing with the draft.

#### Whose scene is it

Give the scene to whoever has the most to lose in it. That is usually not the book's protagonist and not the person holding the information. If the POV character cannot be changed by the outcome, the scene is being watched rather than lived, and the reader feels the distance as flatness. The exception is deliberate: an observer scene can be the point when the observer's failure to act is the subject — mark it, and give the observer a cost for not acting.

#### Two people, one value

When both characters are moved by the same value in opposite directions, the scene gets its shape for free: one rises as the other falls. This is the cleanest engine for a scene with no plot function — a negotiation, a farewell, a test of loyalty — because the value carries the change even when nothing external happens. The failure is a scene where both move the same way: two people reassured, or two people humiliated. That is a chorus, not a scene.

#### The scene card

```
Question:        Will <who> get <what> from <whom> before <limit>?
Entering value:  <pair>  <+ / −>
Agendas:         A wants <...> from B; B wants <...> from A
Turn:            <decision> — produced by <who>, on <what line/event>
Cost:            <what it costs, to whom>
Exit state:      <what changed>  <charge>
Mode:            scene | sequel        Last line: <quote or paraphrase>
```

#### Worked card — a 300-word scene

Setting: a loading bay at the end of a night shift. Ilias, a supervisor, needs a new hire's signature on a safety report whose date has been changed.

- **Question:** will Ilias get the signature before the shift bell?
- **Entering value:** his idea of himself as a man who does not lie for the company — `+`.
- **Agendas:** Ilias wants the signature without explaining the date; the hire wants to know why the report says Tuesday when the accident was Thursday.
- **Turn:** Ilias tells him the date was changed. It is a decision, and it costs him the easy signature: the hire can now refuse knowingly.
- **Cost:** the hire signs, and says, "Next time ask me first." Ilias has made him an accomplice and can never be straightforward with him again.
- **Exit:** honesty `−`, trust `−`; mode: scene; last line: the hire's "next time", which lands on the change rather than after it.

Three beats of dialogue, one line of action with the clipboard, and the bell. At 300 words the scene has no room for the loading bay's history, and does not need it.

#### Word budget inside the scene

Allocate words by pressure, not by chronology. The approach gets the least, the turn gets the most, the exit gets one line. A scene that spends half its words on arrival — who came in, what the room looked like, how they greeted each other — has spent its budget before the decision. Enter after the greeting and leave before the farewell. If the turn needs forty words and the approach has two hundred, the scene is describing the wrong moment.

#### The gates, run on the worked scene

State change: pass — Ilias's standing with the hire moves from `+` to `−`. Agency: pass — the turn is his own decision to tell the truth. Cost: pass — "next time ask me first" converts a subordinate into an accomplice. Last line: pass — it lands on the new relation, not after it. Modal: borderline — a supervisor confessing a falsified date is the expected move for this setup. The non-modal version has Ilias blame the office: *the date was changed upstairs, I just need it signed.* The hire signs, Ilias keeps his self-image, and the scene is competent and forgettable. The modal gate does not condemn the scene; it sends you back to the agenda.

#### A scene that is only a turn

A very short scene — two lines of dialogue, one gesture — can pass every gate if the turn and its cost both sit inside it. Length is not the criterion. What a short scene cannot carry is a change of agenda: with no room for the approach, the reader must already know what each person wants when the scene opens, which means the setup belongs to the scene before. If it does not, the short scene reads as a fragment of a scene that was cut.

#### Diagnosis of a failed scene

The failed version: two colleagues meet in a car park after a funeral, talk about the dead woman, she cries, he offers his coat, the scene ends on the car park lights.

- **Agendas:** none. Both want comfort, which is the same object, so there is nothing to negotiate.
- **Turn:** a mood. She cries; crying happens to her, and nothing is decided.
- **Cost:** unnamed. The coat costs him nothing and means nothing.
- **Exit:** unchanged. Both are sad before and after, and the reader has learned no fact and no relationship.
- **Last line:** lands after any possible change, on weather.

Repair: he wants her to take the dead woman's dog; she wants him to admit he was there the night she died. Now the coat is a bribe, the crying is a move in a negotiation, the dog is the thing on the table, and the last line can land on her refusal.

#### Revising the card, not the prose

When a scene fails, change the card first and rewrite from the changed card. The commonest repair is not a better turn but a different cost: keep the turn, raise what it costs, and the prose usually rewrites itself around the new price. The second commonest is to change the other character's agenda — the scene was a request because both people wanted the same thing. The third is to move the turn earlier and end on its consequence. Rewriting prose against an unchanged card produces the same scene in better sentences, which is a polish that does not land.

#### Last lines: three ways to land after the change

The failure is a last line that comments on what just happened. Three repairs. Cut back to the line where the change occurs. Replace commentary with an action that carries the new state — *he wrote down the collection time and did not look up.* Or hand the last line to the other person, whose reply measures the change from outside. None of the three explains the change. An explained ending tells the reader what they just felt, and the feeling arrives after the explanation has closed the door.

#### Summary as a scene substitute

Use summary when nothing is decided and the reader needs transport, time, or a fact. Use a scene when a decision is made, a relationship shifts, or force is applied and resisted. A summary that contains the story's only decision has stolen the scene, and no later scene can pay it back. The inverse error is just as common: staging a scene for a decision the reader has already been told about, which is a re-enactment and reads as padding.

#### Scene and sequel pairing

A scene ends in a setback; the sequel processes it: reaction, dilemma, decision. The sequel's output is a decision that changes the next scene's goal. If it produces only feeling, compress it into the next scene's opening. Two scenes with no sequel read breathless — things happen, nothing lands. Two sequels in a row read static — the character almost decides, twice. The sequel is also where agency is repaired: without a decision between scenes, the next scene begins because the plot needs it to.

#### Auditing a chapter's scenes

List every scene in the chapter with two columns: its question and its exit state. Run three checks. Two scenes with the same exit are one scene told twice — merge or cut. A scene whose exit equals its entry is a pause. A scene that shares its question with the next one is a scene split in two, usually because the writer wanted a chapter break, which is not a reason. The audit takes ten minutes and finds a chapter's dead weight faster than rereading it.

#### A chapter is not a scene

A chapter is two to four scenes plus the sequel that connects them, or one scene and its consequence, or a single long scene if the pressure holds. It is a unit of reading, not a unit of change, and its break should land on a question rather than an answer. If the last line resolves the scene's question completely, the reader's only reason to continue is habit — legitimate in some genres, weak in most.

#### Choosing the emotion channel

`[data: StoryScope]` model-written stories render emotion through the body in 81% of cases against 38% for human writers, and name it directly in 8% against 29%. The finding does not say to name emotion. It says the somatic channel is the model's default, so at the moment a scene turns, the default is probably doing the work that a choice should do. Choose by function: a body response when the character cannot afford to know what they feel; a named feeling when the character is precise about it and the precision is the point; an action when the feeling is instrumental. The failure is not telling — it is using the default channel at the turn.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- No state change, or a change the scene does not name.
- A turn produced by an arrival from outside the scene: a call, a messenger, a discovery in a drawer.
- A turn that is interior only, with no event or line to produce it.
- No cost, or a cost the scene asserts and never shows.
- A last line that comments after the change instead of landing on it.
- Two agendas that are the same agenda.
- A sequel with no decision, or a scene whose only decision was already summarised.
- The scene's question answered by the setup rather than by the turn.
- A scene much longer than the decision it contains — if the decision fits in a third of the words, the rest is atmosphere or repetition.
- The default emotion channel used at the turn.
- A scene whose value moves the same way for both participants — a chorus, not a conflict.

### 6. Typical errors

- **The atmospheric scene.** Beautiful, changes nothing, defended with "but it establishes mood". Mood is established by the scene that turns; this one is a pause with weather.
- **The meeting that transmits plot.** Two people exchange information the reader needs; nobody wants anything. Convert one of them into someone who needs the information not to be true, and the same information becomes a threat.
- **The argument where both say what they mean.** The turn becomes a statement instead of a concession. Subtext is E9's work; the scene-level symptom is that nothing is at risk in saying it.
- **Scene inflation.** A 2,000-word scene for a 200-word decision, usually because the writer enjoyed the room. The room is not the scene.
- **The reveal with no loser.** Someone learns something, nobody loses anything by knowing, and the scene ends. E6 prices this; E8 shows it.
- **The re-enactment.** A scene staged for a decision the reader was already told about in summary. The reader skips it and feels justified.
- **The turn by telegram.** A phone call or a letter arrives with exactly the thing that changes the scene. Same failure as a solving accident, one scale down.

### 7. Output format

Return the scene card, then one verdict line per gate:

```
Gates: state change <pass/fail> | agency+the turn is a costly choice <pass/fail> | entry/exit+last line <pass/fail> | information <pass/fail> | voice <pass/fail> | modal <pass/fail>
Verdict: <rewrite the turn | cut the scene | merge into X | keep>
```

The verdict must name an action. "Needs work" is not a verdict, and neither is a list of adjectives.

### 8. Micro-example

**Failed:** *They sorted their mother's things and talked about the funeral. Outside, the light went down over the lot.* Nothing is wanted from anyone, and the turn is a mood.

**Repaired:** the older brother wants the younger to take the piano; the younger wants an admission about the house sale. The younger says he will take it, then adds that he flies on Thursday — acceptance as a weapon. Cost: the older brother loses his last reason to keep him. Exit: trust `−`. The last line lands on the change: *He wrote down the collection time and did not look up.*

## How these engines combine

E7 sets the frame; E8 fills it. The dependency runs the other way more often than expected: a scene with no change cannot be rescued by moving it in time, since reordering only changes which scene it fails to affect. Diagnose scene failure before reaching for non-linear structure. When the scenes are strong and the sequence is flat, E7 is the missing layer — the same scene placed before or after another becomes a different scene, and that difference is the whole point of order.

E8 depends on E6 for the entering charge. A value charge is a knowledge state: a scene that opens with the character already knowing what the scene is supposed to teach them is usually two scenes collapsed into one, and separating them is a cut, not a rewrite. E8 depends on E2 for the scene's job: if the scene's turn does not move the spine's load-bearing node or its cost, the scene is a subplot or a cut, and no amount of scene craft will make it necessary.

E7 depends on E2 for the same reason at the level of discourse. A shift is a *therefore* or a *but* in the telling. If the fabula's connectives are *and then*, reordering produces a more confusing sequence rather than a structure, and the reader's reconstruction work has nothing to reward it.

E7 and E11 have a working relationship worth naming: the modal test is run on the *turn*, not on the sentences, and it is run before the prose exists. A scene whose turn is the modal continuation of its setup will be rewritten in adjectives forever. Change the agenda or the cost, then re-run.

Do not stack E7 and E8 at full resolution on the same first draft. Fix the scene first; decide the order second; write. Braided structure built from scenes that do not turn is a more expensive way to be flat.

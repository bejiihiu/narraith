## Engine index

| ID | Engine | Pull it when | It produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| E3 | Character Agency & Motivation | the turn arrives by accident, message or new arrival; the protagonist reacts for pages; you cannot say what they want in one clause | a priced fork, a named chooser, and a verdict on whether the character is load-bearing |
| E4 | Contradiction & Moral Ambiguity | two characters argue and one is obviously right; the conflict is carried by a villain's backstory; the ending delivers a verdict | two defensible arguments, one irreconcilable moment, a visible cost, and no adjudication |
| E5 | Relationship Dynamics | the scene is two people talking and nothing moves; a relationship changes offstage | a five-row ledger and one row moved through a physical detail |
| E9 | Dialogue & Subtext | characters say what they mean; every line answers the previous line's content; the scene ends on a summary | two agendas, a surface subject, a failed bid, and a balance that has shifted |
| E15 | Unreliable Narration | the narrator has a stake in the version they tell; the reader's revision of the past is the point | a story-fact sheet, a type, three recoverable discrepancies, an ending mode |

Three warnings before you start. These engines are **diagnostic first, generative second**: run them on a scene you have drafted, not on a blank page, or you will build characters out of procedure. They are **not cumulative** — a scene that runs all five is a scene with no centre, and the reader feels the machinery. And each has a **legitimate off-switch** stated under "when not to use"; the off-switch is part of the engine, not an exception to it.

## E3 — Character Agency & Motivation

### 1. Purpose

Make the scene's turn happen because someone chose it, and make the choice cost something the story has been building. E3 answers one question: is this character load-bearing, or is the plot carrying them?

### 2. When to use, and when not

**Use** when the turn of a scene is produced by an accident, a message, a new arrival, or a realisation with no event behind it. Use when the protagonist spends a scene reacting. Use when the ending is a decision to accept something. Use when you cannot state the protagonist's want in one clause, or when a reader says "things happen to her".

**Do not use** on a scene whose subject is the impossibility of choice — but then run E3 anyway and record the passivity as a *chosen* refusal with a price, because a passive protagonist is a claim that needs defending. Do not use it on a scene whose only job is to deliver information; that is E6's territory. And do not run it on every scene at draft stage: characters who choose in every scene read as busy, and a story needs units where nothing is decided and the consequences are absorbed.

### 3. Inputs

- The scene, drafted or planned, with its turn already identifiable.
- The character's three axes, one sentence each: want, need, lie.
- The scene card if one exists: goal, obstacle, turn, cost, exit.
- What the story needs to be true after this scene, so the counterfactual test has a target to break.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Locate the turn.** Mark the sentence after which the scene's state is different. If you cannot point at one sentence, the scene has no turn and E3 cannot repair it — go to E8.
2. **Name the chooser.** Who acted in the sentence immediately before the turn? Write a name. Not "fate", not "the situation", not "it became clear that". If the actor is the opponent, decide whether that displacement is deliberate and will be paid for later.
3. **Separate want from need in this scene.** One clause each: what they are trying to get *here*, and what would actually help them. If the two are identical, the scene has no internal resistance and the obstacle is doing all the work.
4. **Classify the obstacle.** *External convenience* blocks them and could be removed without changing them: a locked door, a late train, an unhelpful clerk. *Internal cost* means acting requires giving up something they value or violating the lie. Count both. Three external obstacles and no internal one is a chase, not a scene.
5. **Write the fork.** Two options, both available in the room, both priced in a concrete currency: money, an hour, a person's respect, a promise, a piece of information, a place to sleep. If one option is free, the fork is fake — price it or delete it.
6. **Check the fork against the lie.** The option that obeys the lie should feel *necessary* to the character; the option that breaks it should be the one that would repair them. If both options obey the lie, the scene cannot move them. If both break it, the lie is already dead and the scene is an epilogue.
7. **Run the counterfactual.** Write the next scene in one sentence assuming the other branch. If it works equally well, the character is not load-bearing — the plot is. Repair by making the branch irreversible (a signature, a disclosure, a departure, a debt incurred) rather than by adding talk about stakes.
8. **Pay on the page.** Name the moment the cost is charged, in this scene or the next. A cost that exists only in the synopsis was never paid, and the reader will feel the missing bill even if they cannot locate it.

`[data: StoryScope]` records protagonist-driven resolution in 69% of AI-written stories against 46% of human ones, as a binary choice between protagonist choice and external fate. The feature cannot distinguish a costly choice from a free one, so treat the figure as a warning about defaults, not a target. `[hypothesis]`

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- No chooser: the turn has no agent.
- Unpriced options: the fork is a preference, not a decision.
- The price is invented in the scene that charges it, rather than built earlier.
- The choice is what any plausible character in that position would do.
- The plot survives either branch, so the character is scenery.
- Someone else pays the cost — a sacrifice by proxy, usually a secondary character dying or leaving so the protagonist can feel something safely.
- The choice is an acceptance of something the character could have refused, with no refusal priced.

### 6. Typical errors

- **Making the obstacle harder instead of more expensive.** More guards, worse weather, a tighter deadline. Difficulty is not cost.
- **Motivating with backstory.** The orphan paragraph explains the want and removes the need to price it.
- **Confusing want with need.** "She wants to be happy" is a need in a want's clothes and cannot be blocked by a person in a room.
- **Giving the choice to the most articulate character** rather than the one with something at stake. Eloquence is not agency.
- **Two choices in one scene**, so neither is charged.
- **Delegating the costly choice to a secondary character** so the protagonist stays sympathetic. This is the commonest form of puppetry in literary drafts, and it is invisible in synopsis.

Three fake forks to check for, each of which passes a casual reading:

- *The obvious fork.* One option is right and the other is stupid, so the character's choice was never in question. Repair by making the wrong option the one that keeps a promise.
- *The feeling fork.* Both options are the same action, differing only in how the character feels about it. That is a mood, not a decision; the reader cannot see it from outside.
- *The free fork.* One option costs nothing, so the character's virtue is the absence of a price. Give it a price or remove it — an unpriced alternative makes the chosen one meaningless.

A fourth check, since it is easy to confuse with the others: a choice can be *correct* and still costly. The test is not whether the character chose well but whether they can name what they gave up.

### 7. Output format

```
Turn:          <the sentence>
Chooser:       <name>
Fork:          A) <option> — costs <currency>   B) <option> — costs <currency>
Lie check:     <which option obeys the lie; which would repair them>
Counterfactual: if B, <one sentence> — and the story breaks because <reason>
Cost paid:     <where, in what currency, charged by whom>
Verdict:       load-bearing / passenger
```

Keep it to seven lines. If a field will not fill, that field is the defect.

### 8. Micro-example

> The form was two pages and the second page was the one that mattered. Ifeoma read the first page twice, which was how she had learned to make the second page feel decided. The pen was the clerk's, chained to the desk. Behind her the queue had stopped pretending not to listen. She thought about the flat, the rent, and the fact that Ngozi had said the word *finally* on the phone as though it were a kindness. She filled in the first page, turned it, and put the pen down on the second.

Fork: sign the settlement, and lose the only role she has in the family — the one who handles things; refuse, and lose the flat. The pen put down is the choice, made by not writing. The cost is charged in the queue's silence: she has to turn round and walk past eight people who heard her decide.

## E4 — Contradiction & Moral Ambiguity

### 1. Purpose

Build a situation in which two goods the character actually holds cannot both be honoured, so the choice costs and the text does not tell the reader what to think. E4 is not about making a villain human. It is about making the reader's judgement necessary and insufficient.

### 2. When to use, and when not

**Use** when a character is consistent to the point of being a function. Use when the moral weight of the story is currently carried by a villain's unhappy childhood. Use when two characters argue and one of them is obviously right, or when the draft's last page delivers a verdict the reader did not reach. Use when a character's principles have never been tested by a scene.

**Do not use** when the conflict is material rather than moral — who gets the money, who gets the job — because that is a scarcity problem and belongs to E2 and E3. Do not use it on a fable, a satire, or a parable that needs a verdict to work; blurring those produces a text that has lost its instrument. And do not use it if no character in the scene can lose anything: ambiguity requires stakes, and stakes require someone who can be deprived.

### 3. Inputs

- The values at stake, written as positive pairs: loyalty/honesty, mercy/justice, the crew's wages/the log's truth.
- Each involved character's lie, since the lie decides which good they will betray first.
- Every character's stated position, in their own words.
- What must be true after the scene for the plot to continue. This is the constraint that stops the ambiguity from dissolving the story.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Name the conflict as two goods.** Write both sides as things a decent person could want. If one side is a vice, split the vice until you find the good it serves — cruelty serving order, cowardice serving peace — and use that.
2. **Give each side a coherent argument.** Three sentences, in the voice of someone who holds it, with no self-justification and no concession. Write the side you disagree with first, and make it the stronger of the two on the page.
3. **Swap the arguments.** Put each argument in the other character's mouth. Each must be able to state the other's case without distortion. If it cannot, you have written a straw position and the scene will read as a rigged trial.
4. **Locate the moment both cannot be honoured.** It must be an available action with a deadline: a signature, a testimony, a departure, a name given to someone. If no such moment exists inside a scene, you have a theme, not a structure.
5. **Make the character pay in a visible currency.** Decide which good loses, and what physical or social fact records the loss: the returned keys, the chair that stays empty in the photograph, the name struck off a list, the shift nobody offers again.
6. **Ensure the text does not adjudicate.** Remove the narrator's commentary, the closing image that resolves the argument into weather, the wise third character who pronounces sentence, and any ending in which the losing side is punished by events in a way that reads as authorial judgement rather than consequence.
7. **Run the anti-collapse check.** Three questions: does each side win something the reader wants? is the cost shown concretely on the side that loses? has one side been given a checkable factual advantage? Three noes mean you have made everyone bad, which is not ambiguity but fatigue.
8. **Price the repetition.** Decide where the same conflict recurs at higher stakes. One occurrence is an incident; the second is what turns it into a structure the reader can see coming and dread.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- One side is a vice dressed as a good, so the ending was never in doubt.
- Both arguments are weak, and the scene is two people being wrong at each other.
- The conflict is stated by a character instead of staged by a situation.
- The cost is emotional and unnamed — "she felt terrible" — with no concrete fact carrying it.
- The text adjudicates in its last paragraph, usually through an image that means "and yet, life".
- The ambiguity removes all grounds for judgement, so the reader can neither convict nor acquit. That is nihilism, and it is a different failure from the one this engine is for.

### 6. Typical errors

- **Villain backstory as ambiguity.** Explaining how someone became cruel does not make their position good; it makes them pitiable, which is cheaper and safer.
- **Distributing blame evenly.** Even-handedness is not the same as equal guilt; a text that apportions fault symmetrically reads as cowardice, and readers punish it.
- **Giving one side all the good lines** while claiming to be balanced.
- **Resolving through a third party's wisdom** — the priest, the grandmother, the colleague who says the sensible thing.
- **Ambiguity about facts** (who did it) instead of **values** (what should be done). The first is a mystery; the second is this engine.
- **Using ambiguity to avoid committing** to what the story is about. The story must still take a position on *which goods are in conflict*; it may not take one on who was right.

One distinction worth keeping sharp, because it decides whether a scene reads as ambiguity or as excuse: **explanation is not justification.** A childhood that explains why a man beats his wife does not make beating her a competing good. Competing goods require that the character could defend the action to someone they respect without lying. If the only available defence is a cause, the material is a case history, and the scene will read as special pleading no matter how well it is written.

### 7. Output format

```
Pair:            <good A> vs <good B>
A's case:        "<three sentences, strongest form>"
B's case:        "<three sentences, strongest form>"
Irreconcilable:  <the action available now that honours only one>
Currency:        <what the losing side loses, as a concrete fact>
Recurs at:       <scene / act>
Adjudication:    none — <what was removed>
```

Two arguments in quotation marks, both defensible, is the whole test. If you cannot bear to write the second one well, the scene is not ready.

### 8. Micro-example

> The list lay on the table between them: eight names, and the company had agreed to lose three. "You pick," Halloran said, "and I'll sign it, and in the morning I'll tell them it was me." He said it the way a man says a thing he has practised. She looked at the names and found her own third from the bottom, in her own handwriting, because she had written the rota. "If I pick," she said, "they'll know I picked." "That's the job," he said. "The job is I pick and you carry it," she said, "and you've got that backwards."

Two goods: the crew's survival and the fairness of the process, and each survives only by sacrificing the other. The currency is authorship — who is seen to have chosen — which is the only thing either of them still owns.

## E5 — Relationship Dynamics

### 1. Purpose

Make a relationship a trackable pattern of exchange rather than an atmosphere, and make each relationship scene move exactly one row of that pattern, so the relationship changes without anyone announcing it.

### 2. When to use, and when not

**Use** when a scene between two people reads as a status report — pleasant, accurate, and inert. Use when a relationship changes offstage ("they grew closer over the winter"). Use when you cannot tell who has power in a pair, or when a reconciliation or rupture happens in a single line and does not convince. Use when a character's behaviour toward one person contradicts their behaviour toward everyone else and you need to know why.

**Do not use** on a first meeting, where there is no pattern yet: establish the ledger instead of moving it, and let the pattern be visible in the first three exchanges. Do not use it on a scene that must carry a heavy plot function and has no room for a row change — fold that scene into one that has room. Do not use it on crowds or on a pair whose relationship is deliberately flat, because a cast where every relationship is finely tracked has no background.

### 3. Inputs

- The pair, and what the scene needs to accomplish for the plot.
- The ledger if it exists; if not, the material to build one.
- The current state of each row, in the present tense.
- Who else is present, since a third party changes which rows can move.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Build or read the five rows.** Initiative (who starts, calls, proposes, arrives), permission (who may touch, ask, refuse without explaining, enter without knocking), debt (what is owed, by whom, in what currency, whether it is mentionable), taboo (what cannot be raised, and what happens when it is), physical grammar (distance, touch, doorway, who handles whose things).
2. **Find the row under pressure.** Which row does the scene's subject keep touching? It is usually the taboo, or the debt, and it is usually the one nobody names.
3. **Decide the new state of that one row**, as a single sentence in the present tense: "June may now mention the money. Ada may no longer walk in."
4. **Choose the vehicle.** A doorway, a chair, a set of keys, a form of address, who drives, who pays, who waits, who carries what. The vehicle must be able to carry the change with no dialogue at all.
5. **Write the scene with everything else held constant.** Same room, same time of day, same third parties, same subject. If a second row starts to move, delete that movement — two rows in one scene blurs the causality and the reader cannot tell which change caused which.
6. **Show the change in the last beat, through the vehicle, and stop.** Do not add a line in which somebody notices that things are different. The reader's noticing is the payoff; a character's noticing steals it.
7. **Update the ledger.** Record the row, the old state, the new state, and where in the story it moved. An unrecorded change is a relationship that will silently reset two chapters later (E13).

A scene with three people contains three ledgers, and only one is on the table. Decide which pair's row is moving in front of the third, and what the third's presence forbids. This is where most ensemble scenes earn their keep: the same sentence spoken alone would land differently, and the third person is the reason it cannot be said. A three-hander in which every pair behaves identically in and out of company has no triangulation — it is a two-hander with a witness who might as well be furniture.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- No row changes: the scene is a status report and should be folded into one that moves something.
- The change is stated rather than shown — "something between them had shifted".
- Two rows move, so the scene has no single cause.
- The change is reversible at no cost, so it is a mood.
- The physical grammar contradicts the ledger with no reason: a character who may not touch keeps touching, and the blocking is now a continuity error.
- Power is asserted in dialogue while permission says the opposite, and the scene believes the dialogue.

### 6. Typical errors

- **Writing the feeling and skipping the exchange.** The reader is told the relationship is strained and shown two people being polite.
- **Making every scene about the taboo**, so the relationship becomes one note and the reader learns to predict the avoidance.
- **Using a quarrel as the only mechanism.** Quarrels move rows loudly and cheaply; most real movement happens in logistics.
- **Letting a third character narrate the relationship** — "they had always been close, in the way of sisters who".
- **Resetting the ledger between chapters** because nobody wrote it down, so a permission granted in act one is forgotten in act two.

### 7. Output format

```
Pair: <A / B>
Initiative:      <who, how often, in what form>
Permission:      <who may do what without asking>
Debt:            <what is owed, currency, mentionable?>
Taboo:           <subject, and the room's behaviour when it is raised>
Physical grammar: <distance, touch, doorway, objects>
Row changed:     <row> from <old> to <new>, shown by <vehicle>
```

The ledger is five lines; the change is one. Anything longer is a character study, not a scene tool.

### 8. Micro-example

> Tomas carried the boxes and Radek held the doors, which was how it had always gone. At the third box Radek put his hand out for it, and Tomas gave it to him, and then stood with his arms empty while Radek worked out where it went in the van. It took him a while. Tomas did not take it back. When the van was full Radek wiped his hands on his trousers and said, "I'll drive," and Tomas went round to the passenger side without a word, and they sat there for a minute with the engine off.

The initiative row moves: the one who has always carried now holds doors, and the one who has always held doors now drives. Nothing is said about the change. The minute with the engine off is the cost of it.

## E9 — Dialogue & Subtext

### 1. Purpose

Make an exchange do work the words do not state: two agendas, one literal surface subject, and a balance that has shifted by the last line. Subtext is not secrecy. It is a second layer produced by a first layer that is genuinely about something.

### 2. When to use, and when not

**Use** when characters say what they mean and the scene reads as an exchange of information. Use when every line replies to the previous line's content. Use when a scene ends on a summary of what it meant. Use when two characters are interchangeable once you delete the dialogue tags (see the tag-strip test, `references/narrative-theory.md` §10).

**Do not use** where transparency is the effect: a confession, a briefing, a dying declaration, a formal interview. Directness there is a choice, and blurring it produces characters who cannot say the one thing the scene exists for. Do not use it for comedy built on literal misunderstanding, which requires characters to answer the sentence rather than the act. And do not attempt it with four or more speakers in one scene: agendas multiply past legibility, and the fix is to split the scene, not to add more subtext.

### 3. Inputs

- Each speaker's **agenda-in-the-scene**: what they want from the other person in this room, phrased as a request they will not make.
- Each speaker's **taboo**: the sentence they will not say, which should be the most obvious sentence available.
- The **surface subject**: something concrete, literal, and genuinely worth a few minutes of anyone's attention.
- The **entry balance**: who holds the upper hand, and in what currency — information, permission, money, affection, the ability to leave.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Write both agendas as refusals to ask.** "He wants her to take their mother for the weekend and will not ask." If both agendas can be satisfied at once, you have a negotiation; if they cannot, you have a scene.
2. **Name both taboos.** Each should be the sentence a stranger would say first. The scene is the management of that sentence, and the reader must be able to feel it being managed.
3. **Choose the surface subject** and check it can hold the whole scene without strain. It must be literal and concrete: the parking fee, the hangers, the ramp, the roof. If the subject is a metaphor for the real subject, the subtext collapses into allegory and the reader stops believing the people.
4. **Write the first exchange as an answer to the act, not the line.** If A accuses, B answers the accusation by answering something adjacent, or by answering it too precisely. The literal content of B's reply should be true and slightly off.
5. **Cut the greeting and the farewell.** Enter after the ritual and leave before it. If the ritual is the point, cut everything else and let the ritual carry the scene.
6. **Insert one failed bid for connection.** A line that reaches, is not taken up, and is not repeated. One per scene, placed before the last third. It is the reader's evidence that something was wanted, and its failure is what makes the ending land.
7. **End on the shift in the balance**, not on a summary and not on a line that names the theme. Who now holds what; who has stopped asking; what can no longer be said.
8. **Run the tag-strip test.** Delete every tag and beat. If you cannot tell who is speaking, fix idiolect — one lexical field and one syntactic habit per speaker — rather than adding accents or catchphrases.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- Both speakers state their meaning, so the scene is a transcript.
- No agenda: the characters are present because the plot needs them in a room.
- The surface subject is a pretext, and it collapses the moment anyone asks a direct question about it.
- No failed bid, so nothing was wanted and the scene has no cost.
- The scene ends on a statement of what it meant.
- Every line replies to the previous line's content, so the exchange is a rally rather than a negotiation.
- The balance is identical at entry and exit.
- The subtext depends on knowledge the reader does not have, which is not subtext but a private joke.

### 6. Typical errors

- **The briefing in disguise.** Two characters tell each other things they both know so the reader can hear them.
- **Therapy dialogue.** Characters accurately name each other's feelings, which is rarer in life than in drafts and always sounds like the author.
- **Symmetrical wit.** Every line equally clever, so no one has more at stake than anyone else.
- **Overloaded silence.** A scene where nobody speaks and nothing happens; silence is a move, not a mode.
- **Accent as characterisation.** Phonetic spelling marks origin and flattens everything else.
- **The obliging interlocutor** who asks exactly the question that resolves the scene and accepts the answer.

### 7. Output format

```
A — agenda: <what they want, unasked> / taboo: <the unsaid sentence>
B — agenda: <what they want, unasked> / taboo: <the unsaid sentence>
Surface:  <the literal subject>
Balance:  <who holds what> → <who holds what>
Failed bid: "<the line>"
```

Then the exchange. If the block takes longer to write than the scene, the scene is not yet about anything.

### 8. Micro-example

> "You'll keep the flat, then," Priya said.
> "The lease is up in June."
> "I only ask because Marcus said you'd been looking at Highbury."
> "Marcus says a lot of things."
> "He does." She stirred her drink. "The cake's from Denning, the one that does the lemon. They've started doing a coffee one, but I thought — lemon. Because of the thing."
> "Lemon's fine."

Priya's agenda is to be told she is still needed; her taboo is the word *leaving*. The cake is over-specificity and a near-miss at naming the thing, and "Lemon's fine" answers the cake while closing the bid. The balance moves in the last two words: Priya has stopped asking.

### Subtext moves

| Move | What it looks like | What it reveals | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deflection** | The reply addresses a nearby topic instead of the one offered | The topic is loaded and the speaker knows it | If the reader cannot see what was evaded, it reads as a non-sequitur |
| **Over-specificity** | An unnecessary exact number, date, or procedure | Anxiety managed as precision; sometimes an alibi being built | If the detail serves no emotional function, it reads as research |
| **Category shift** | Answering in a different category than asked — feeling answered with logistics, a person answered as an object | Refusal to enter the frame the other proposed | If the shift is not legible as refusal, it reads as a mistake |
| **Question answered with a question** | The burden reversed | The speaker will not be the one who commits first | Once per scene; twice is a tic |
| **Sudden logistics** | A practical detail arrives at an emotional peak | Time being bought, or a decision to leave while appearing to stay | If the logistics are dull, the scene's heat dies |
| **Silence** | A beat with no reply, marked by a physical action | The line landed and there is no safe answer | Silence after every line flattens the scene; silence with no action reads as a formatting error |
| **Misnaming** | Wrong name, wrong title, wrong kinship word — "your mother" instead of "Mum" | A claim about distance, or a refusal of intimacy, or a preoccupation leaking | Reads as an error unless the reader knows the correct form |
| **Reply to the act** | The words answer what the other person is doing — testing, retreating, claiming innocence — rather than the content of the line | The speaker has read the manoeuvre and is answering it, which is a claim of superior knowledge | If the act is not legible from the line, the reply looks like a non-sequitur |

### Worked exchange

Two siblings in a car park. He wants her to take their mother; she has decided not to, and has not said so.

> "You took the long way," Tomas said.
> "There's a thing at the top of the ramp now. You have to pay before you come down."
> "I know. I paid."
> She held out the keys. He looked at them, and then at the boot of her car, which was full of boxes.
> "Those are hers," he said.
> "Those are the ones from the wardrobe. I thought the charity place on Mill Street."
> "They'd want the hangers off."
> "I'll do it Sunday."
> "You said Sunday about the boiler."
> "The boiler's done."
> He took the keys. He did not move toward his car. "She asked about the garden. Whether you'd come and look at the garden."
> "Tell her it needs cutting back."
> "You could tell her."
> "I'm on at seven."
> He turned the key over in his hand twice. "Right," he said. "Well. Mind the ramp on the way up."

### Annotation

1. **"You took the long way."** An accusation in the shape of an observation. He is tracking her movements, which means he has been thinking about her arrival. The agenda is already running.
2. **"There's a thing at the top of the ramp now."** Deflection: she answers the route, not the accusation. The information is true, which is what makes the deflection survive. She does not deny taking the long way, and the omission is the tell.
3. **"I know. I paid."** He answers the implied debt instead of the stated fact. Within three lines the ledger is visible: who pays, who notices, who is owed.
4. **She holds out the keys.** The transaction is offered before the conversation. Physical grammar: she is the one who hands over, so she is the one leaving.
5. **He looks at the boot.** He does not take the keys first. The look is the question, and it is asked with an object.
6. **"Those are hers."** Category shift: he names the boxes by ownership, which is the emotional category, not the practical one.
7. **"Those are the ones from the wardrobe."** She shifts the category back — from *hers* to *which ones* — and adds a destination, which converts a mother's clothes into an errand. The refusal is in the grammar.
8. **"They'd want the hangers off."** Over-specificity. The hangers are the small labour that stands for the large one, and he is testing whether she will do it. This is also the first bid.
9. **"I'll do it Sunday." / "You said Sunday about the boiler."** Her promise is called in as bad credit. The debt row is now on the table without either of them naming it.
10. **"The boiler's done."** She wins the fact and loses the argument, and neither of them says so.
11. **He takes the keys. He does not move toward his car.** The transaction is complete; the request is not. The blocking carries the whole subtext: he cannot leave because he has not asked.
12. **"She asked about the garden."** The real subject arrives, displaced onto a third person and a task. This is the second bid, and it is stronger because it comes from the mother's mouth.
13. **"Tell her it needs cutting back."** She answers the task, not the request. Over-specificity as refusal, again — and the repetition of the move is what shows it is a policy, not a mood.
14. **"You could tell her."** The failed bid proper: a rebuke soft enough to be deniable, offering her a way back into the family through a garden. It is not taken up.
15. **"I'm on at seven."** Logistics as a wall. The shift is real, and it is also the perfect excuse, and the reader cannot tell how much of each.
16. **"He turned the key over in his hand twice."** The only somatic tell, and it is small. This is where the refusal lands — in the pause, not in a line.
17. **"Right. Well. Mind the ramp on the way up."** He withdraws, and the withdrawal is both a kindness and a closing. The balance has shifted: he has stopped asking. She has won, and the win has cost her the thing she was protecting, which the scene does not name and does not need to.

## E15 — Unreliable Narration

### 1. Purpose

Build a narration whose account is bent in a way the reader can detect and adjudicate from evidence inside the text, without the narrator confessing. The technique lives entirely in the gap between what the narrator says and what the reader can reconstruct.

### 2. When to use, and when not

**Use** when the narrator has an interest in the version they tell. Use when the story is about memory, shame, ideology, or self-justification, and the reader's revision of the past is the point. Use when a first-person account is suspiciously coherent — trauma and guilt rarely narrate in clean arcs.

**Do not use** it as a twist. Do not use it when you cannot supply a second track of evidence, because unreliability the reader cannot detect is indistinguishable from error (see the warning in §6). Do not confuse it with voice: a narrator with strong opinions is not unreliable, only opinionated. And do not put a bent narrator in front of complex material the reader needs to learn — a procedural, a tutorial narrative, a story whose pleasure is the solution. There, fog replaces irony.

### 3. Inputs

- The story-facts: what actually happened, written plainly and chronologically, before any narration exists.
- The narrator's stake: what the bent version buys them.
- The chosen type, and the tell that goes with it.
- The ending's job: what the story needs the reader to be able to do at the last page.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Write the story-fact sheet first.** Seven to ten plain sentences, chronological, third person, named agents, no interpretation, no adjectives of judgement. This is the ground truth the reader will reconstruct. If you cannot write it, you have no story to be unreliable about — you have an atmosphere.
2. **Choose the type**, one dominant: *self-interest* (the narrator benefits from the version), *trauma* (the account routes around an event and returns to its edges), *ideology* (the frame distorts what is seen and what is valued), *incompetence* (the narrator cannot understand what they accurately report), *deliberate deception* (the narrator lies to a specific audience inside the text). Mixed types are legal, but one must dominate or the reader cannot model the bend.
3. **Name the tell.** State what this type does to a sentence: inflation, omission, euphemism, misplaced precision, moralising, sudden vagueness, a shift into the passive at the moment of action. One tell, repeated with variation, is enough. A narrator who is unreliable in a different way on every page is not a narrator but a fog.

   **In third person, the bend lives in the free indirect discourse.** There is no confessor to catch, so the discrepancy sits between the narration's evaluative language and the facts it reports: the character's judgement enters the narrative voice unmarked, and the surrounding detail refuses it. The reader's evidence is the gap between what a sentence approves of and what the scene shows. Same mechanism as first person, one surface fewer — no "I", so no admissions to discount, and every skew has to be caught in the diction.
4. **Plant three recoverable discrepancies of different kinds.** One **factual** (a detail contradicts another detail: a date, an object, an order of events). One **temporal** (an event placed at two times, or a duration that cannot be right, or a season that changes twice). One **tonal** (the narrator's feeling about a scene is out of proportion to what they have just reported, or changes without a cause in the scene).
5. **Test recoverability.** For each discrepancy, ask whether a careful reader can find it on a second pass without the narrator pointing at it. The evidence must be inside the text — not in your notes, not in an afterword, not in a detail you meant to add.
6. **Test adjudication.** The reader must be able to construct the likely truth, or at least rank the possibilities. If both readings are equally supported with no tie-breaker anywhere, you have written an ambiguity or a puzzle. Both are legitimate; neither is this engine.
7. **Decide the ending's mode.** *Confirm*: the text delivers a fact that settles the account — late, small, and preferably from an unremarkable source. *Refuse to confirm*: the discrepancies stay open, but their existence is undeniable and the narrator's composure is now visible as effort. *Reframe*: the reader learns why the narrator needed the version, and re-reads the account rather than correcting it.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- No story-fact sheet, so there is nothing for the narration to be wrong about.
- Discrepancies that exist only in the author's intention.
- The narrator confesses in the final chapter, which converts the technique into a plot device and spends the reader's work for them.
- The reader cannot adjudicate, and the text offers no tie-breaker.
- Unreliability used to excuse continuity errors: two incompatible facts, and the author's defence is "he's unreliable".
- A tonal tell that is actually inconsistent register, which is a craft error wearing the technique's clothes.
- The narrator is unreliable about everything, including the weather and the furniture, so the evidence base disappears.

### 6. Typical errors

- **The confession ending.** "And then I understood that I had been lying to myself all along." The reader is handed the interpretation they were supposed to build.
- **Withholding the story-facts entirely**, so the reader has no material to test and experiences the text as confusion rather than duplicity.
- **Global rather than local unreliability.** A narrator is reliable about the weather and unreliable about their brother. Unreliability is a local bend; if everything bends, nothing can be measured.
- **One kind of discrepancy only.** All factual discrepancies make a puzzle; all tonal ones make a mood. The three kinds are what let the reader triangulate.
- **Amnesia as a type.** It is a device, and it removes the narrator's stake, which is the engine.
- **Confusing sympathy with reliability.** A narrator the reader likes is not thereby honest, and a narrator the reader dislikes is not thereby lying.
- **Missing the sincere narrator who is simply wrong.** Deception and self-interest are the easy types; the ideology type requires that the narrator report accurately and interpret catastrophically. Such a narrator never lies, cannot be caught in a factual contradiction, and is the hardest to build — and the most common shape of unreliability outside genre fiction.

**The warning.** Unreliability without recoverable evidence is just a lie the reader cannot detect. That is not a technique; it is a defect with a theory attached. The reader's pleasure is not being fooled — it is noticing, late, that they have been assembling a different story from the one they were told, and being able to prove it from the text.

### 7. Output format

```
Story-facts:   <7-10 plain sentences>
Type:          <self-interest | trauma | ideology | incompetence | deception>
Tell:          <what it does to a sentence>
Discrepancies: factual   — planted <where> vs <where>; lets the reader infer <x>
               temporal  — planted <where> vs <where>; lets the reader infer <y>
               tonal     — planted <where>; lets the reader infer <z>
Ending mode:   confirm / refuse / reframe — <the fact or refusal that does it>
```

### 8. Micro-example

> My brother met me at the station. He was fifteen minutes late, which was not like him, and he said the traffic, and there is no traffic in that town at four in the afternoon. He took my bag without being asked and carried it the whole way to the car, and I let him. He looked well. We drove past the school and the new estate, which I had only seen in photographs, and he told me about the roof, and I told him about the thing at work, and it was like the four years hadn't happened. Then he said, "She's not coming, by the way," and I said, "I know," though I hadn't.

Three discrepancies, all recoverable. Factual: the narrator refutes the traffic excuse in the same breath he accepts it. Temporal: he recognises the new estate only from photographs, which contradicts the four years narrated as continuity. Tonal: "He looked well" is the sentence of a man performing a report, and it sits where grief would go.

## How these engines combine

Run them in the order the defect appears, not in numerical order. Some pairings do real work; others produce the machinery the reader can hear.

**E3 + E6 — a revelation that costs.** The strongest use of an information engine is to deliver the new fact *while* a choice is being made, so the fork is re-priced in front of the reader. The reveal does not arrive as news; it arrives as a new price on the option the character was about to take. Sequence: E6 schedules the reveal, E3 prices the fork, and the scene is written once with both live.

**E4 + E5 — the loyalty trap is a ledger.** A moral conflict between two people is almost always a debt row and a taboo row under pressure at the same time. E4 supplies the two goods; E5 supplies the currency and the vehicle. The request scene is the meeting point: someone asks for something that would honour one good, and the asking is itself the row change.

**E9 + E3 — dialogue is the want in a room.** When an exchange stalls, the usual cause is that neither speaker has an agenda, which means E3 was skipped upstream. Run E3 first to establish what each wants *here* and what it costs; then E9 has something to withhold. Reversing the order produces characters who speak in subtext about nothing.

**E15 + E6 — the bent filter on an information schedule.** Unreliability is an information-flow problem with a distorted narrator. E6 decides what the reader learns and when; E15 decides how the report of it bends. E15 without E6 produces fog, because there is no schedule for the reader to be ahead of or behind.

**E5 + E13 — the ledger as continuity.** A relationship row that changes and is not recorded will silently reset. E5 produces the change; E13 stores it. This is the cheapest continuity win in the whole system and the one most often skipped, because relationship state feels like it belongs to the scene rather than to the book.

**E4 + E14 — ambiguity versus muddle.** When a draft's moral material reads as confused rather than unresolved, E14's diagnosis must separate *unresolved by design* from *unresolved because two scenes were merged*. The test is whether each side's argument can be stated in three sentences. If it cannot, the scene is not ambiguous; it is unfinished.

**Two pairings to avoid.** E4 + E9 in the same pass at draft stage produces characters who debate their moral positions in dialogue, which converts subtext into position papers — run E4 first, then E9, and delete any line where a character states the conflict. E3 + E15 during planning produces a narrator who carefully explains their own agency, which is the opposite of the effect; save E15 for revision, when the story-facts exist and the bend can be measured against them.

Three engines per story is usually enough. A short story might run E3 once and E9 twice. A novel runs E3 and E5 continuously, E4 at the two or three places where the premise is tested, and E15 only if the narration itself is a subject. If you find yourself running all five on one scene, the scene is probably doing two jobs and should be split.

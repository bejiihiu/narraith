## E10 — Prose Rhythm & Voice

### 1. Purpose

Two jobs that are usually taught separately and are the same job. **Rhythm** is the distribution of sentence shapes across a passage, and it exists to make the reader's attention track the scene's movement. **Voice** is the set of habits that make the narration belong to one teller, and it exists so that the reader can hear a person deciding. Both are [L2] and [L3] work, and both fail in the same way: the prose is smooth, correct, and interchangeable.

The output is a scene whose sentence lengths follow its emotional movement, plus a voice contract specific enough that a neutral rewrite of one paragraph visibly loses something.

The six voice slots in step 7 are the working content of the canonical Voice Contract. POV, distance, and tense are settled before E10 begins; attention, syntax, lexicon, and figuration supply the register; attitude supplies the narrator's stance; refusals supply the three prohibitions, and the three obligations are their positive form. Nothing here reopens a decision that belongs to the ledger.

### 2. When to use and when not

Use E10 when the draft is competent and flat; when every sentence is the same length; when a reader says "I couldn't hear anyone"; when a scene's turn lands with no change in pressure; when you are writing in a voice you have not used before and need to fix its rules before page thirty rather than after.

Do not use it on a first draft before the scene's decisions are settled — polishing prose that is executing the wrong decision is the most expensive way to waste a revision. Do not use it to make a passage "more literary": that instruction has no function attached and produces ornament. Do not use it on a scene whose flatness is the point, an exhausted POV, a bureaucratic voice, a procedural passage — there the flatness is the instrument, and the correct move is to record why rather than to fix it. And do not apply the voice contract to dialogue: dialogue has its own idiolect rules (E9), and a narrator who sounds like their characters has no distance from them.

### 3. Inputs

- The scene, with its emotional movement stated: what the reader should feel at the entry and at the exit, and the beat where it changes.
- The POV, distance, and tense — already decided, not to be renegotiated here.
- Two or three pages of prose by another writer whose rhythm you want to learn from, if you have them. Not to imitate; to calibrate your ear against something that is not your own default.
- The list of things this narrator would never say. If the list is empty, the voice is not yet a voice.

### 4. Procedure

1. **State the emotional movement in one line, with a turn.** *Enters braced, ends wanting to be caught*, or *enters bored, ends frightened of being bored*. A scene with no movement has no rhythm to serve, and this step will tell you before you spend an hour on commas.
2. **Mark the beats.** Divide the scene into 4–8 beats, each a unit of action or attention: she enters; she sees the jar; she counts; she hears the door; she decides. Name what each beat does to the movement — *pressure up*, *pressure held*, *pressure released*.
3. **Assign a sentence function to each beat before writing.** A scene usually needs two or three of these, not six:

| Function | Shape | What it does | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Land** | Short, terminal, no subordination | Ends a movement; gives the reader a place to stop | Used twice in a row, it becomes a drumbeat and stops landing |
| **Carry** | Long, subordinated, one main verb | Holds a complex thought or a sustained image | Used without a landing nearby, it reads as the writer not finishing |
| **Qualify** | Mid-length, parenthetical, turns on a concessive | Complicates what was just said; prevents a clean idea | Overused, it makes the narrator sound like a lawyer |
| **Refuse** | Fragment, broken syntax, an unfinished clause | Performs the thought the character will not complete | Without an established interruptible narrator, it reads as an error |
| **Accelerate** | Parataxis, short clauses, no subordination | Makes the reader's eye move at the speed of the event | Applied to reflection, it produces breathlessness without pressure |
| **Suspend** | A period that delays its verb | Holds the reader in uncertainty for the length of a clause | Two in a page is a mannerism; three is unreadable |
4. **Draft the scene with the functions in mind, not in hand.** Do not write to the plan sentence by sentence; write the scene, then check it. The plan exists so the check has something to check against.
5. **Measure the distribution.** Read the scene aloud, or count. Two things to look for: a run of three or more sentences within a few words of each other in length, and a passage where every sentence has the same shape (subject-verb-object, or a participle opening every paragraph). Either one flattens the reader's attention regardless of the content.
6. **Repair only where the rhythm fights the meaning.** This is the discipline that separates E10 from line-editing. A run of same-length sentences is not automatically a defect — it can be the correct instrument for a monotone of dread, a list, a bureaucratic voice, a character who cannot vary. Repair where the flatness contradicts the beat's function: a *land* beat that arrives inside a run of *carry* sentences, an *accelerate* passage made of subordinate clauses. Use the table below.
7. **Build the voice contract.** Six slots, each answered in a phrase, not a paragraph:
   - **Attention** — what this narrator notices first, and what they never notice. A nurse notices hands; a builder notices load paths; a liar notices exits.
   - **Syntax** — the habitual sentence shape, and the shape they avoid. *Short declaratives, no semicolons, verbs before subjects when frightened.*
   - **Lexicon** — the small set of words they return to, and the register they cannot enter. Three to six words is enough; a whole diction is a costume.
   - **Figuration** — what they compare things to, drawn from one domain. A mechanic compares people to engines, not to weather. One domain, consistently, is voice; four domains is decoration.
   - **Attitude** — the stance toward the material: amused, exact, aggrieved, proprietary, ashamed. Attitude is what makes the same fact sound different in two mouths.
   - **Refusals** — three things this narrator will not say, and what they say instead. These become the three prohibitions of the Voice Contract; the three obligations are the positive form of the same rules (*must name a physical detail before an emotion*, *must never explain another character's motive*).
8. **Test the voice by neutralising it.** Take one paragraph and rewrite it in a flat, competent, unmarked register — no attention bias, no figuration, average syntax. Read both. Name what was lost, in one line per loss. If nothing is lost, the paragraph was not in the voice, and the voice contract is either wrong or not yet being executed.
9. **Record the contract and the two or three repairs you made.** The contract goes where the project keeps its decision artifacts; the repairs go in your own notes. Neither goes in the reply unless the user asked for craft documentation.

**Rhythm repairs.**

| Repair | Symptom | What it changes | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Split** | A long sentence carrying two movements, so the turn is buried mid-clause | Separates the two movements; the second gets its own stress | Loses the sense of a single continuous thought |
| **Merge** | Three short sentences in a row, each landing, so nothing accumulates | Builds one complex movement; subordinates the minor beats | Buries the beat that deserved to land alone |
| **Invert** | The important element arrives first and the sentence dies after it | Moves the stress to the end, where English puts weight | Can read as straining for effect if used more than once a page |
| **Interrupt** | A smooth sentence where the character's thought would break | Inserts a dash, a parenthesis, or a self-correction; the syntax performs the hesitation | Breaks immersion if the narrator has not been established as interruptible |
| **Extend** | A short sentence that should carry weight but reads as clipped and thin | Adds a clause that earns the length by carrying a second idea | Turns a landing into a mumble if the added clause is decoration |
| **Isolate** | A beat that needs to stand alone but is inside a paragraph | Gives it its own paragraph, or its own line | Costs vertical space and can read as pretension when overused |

Two rules for using the table. Fix the sentence that carries the beat, not the sentence that annoys you. And make at most one repair per paragraph on a first pass — consecutive repairs produce prose that reads as though it is being performed.

**Paragraphs are rhythm units too.** Rhythm is not only sentence length: it is also how much air sits between blocks of text. A page of uniform four-line paragraphs is as flat as a page of uniform sentences, and the same repair table applies at the larger scale — split a paragraph that carries two movements, merge three that each land, isolate the one that needs to stand alone. The cheapest and most underused move is the one-line paragraph after a long one: it costs a line of white space and buys a beat of silence.

**Voice and distance pull in opposite directions.** A close narrator (`references/narrative-theory.md` §4, psychic distance) has access to the character's phrasing and loses the ability to comment; a distant narrator can judge and cannot render. The voice contract has to choose, and the choice shows up in syntax before it shows up in content: close narration borrows the character's incomplete sentences, distant narration completes them. If the contract says close and the prose says complete, the reader will feel a narrator standing behind the character, explaining them, and the intimacy will not arrive however much interiority you add.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- **You cannot state the emotional movement.** Then the rhythm has nothing to track, and any variation you add is decoration.
- **Every beat has the same function.** A scene of six *carry* beats is a scene with one note, however well the sentences are built.
- **The distribution is uniform and the meaning is not.** Three or more consecutive sentences within a few words of each other in a passage whose beat is changing.
- **The voice contract has fewer than four filled slots**, or the refusals list is generic ("avoid clichés", "don't be pretentious"). A prohibition that any writer could adopt is not a prohibition; it is a taste.
- **The neutral rewrite loses nothing.** The paragraph was written in the register of "good prose", which is to say in no one's voice.
- **The figuration comes from four domains.** Weather, machinery, food, and legal procedure in one page is not a sensibility; it is a browse.
- **The prose is doing the scene's work for it.** A sentence that explains what the beat already showed is an [L4] failure wearing [L3] clothes.

One failure is repairable in a pass. Three mean the scene's decisions are unsettled and E10 is being used to avoid E11.

### 6. Typical errors

- **Uniformity by correction.** The writer notices long sentences, cuts them, and produces a new uniformity of medium sentences. Rhythm is variation, not a preferred length.
- **Rhythm as performance.** Every paragraph gets a fragment for emphasis; the prose starts to stutter, and the reader stops hearing emphasis because everything is emphasised.
- **Voice as diction.** The narrator is given unusual words and a habit of metaphor, and no bias of attention. The result is a style, not a person: it can be pasted onto any scene.
- **Voice as a list of tics.** Three verbal habits and nothing else. Habits without attention are a costume; attention without habits is invisible. You need both, and attention is the load-bearing one.
- **The neutral-rewrite test run on the wrong paragraph.** Testing a paragraph of pure action, where any narrator would sound the same, and concluding the voice is weak. Test the paragraph where the narrator's judgement enters.
- **Fixing sentences to fix a scene.** If the scene's turn is unearned, no rhythm repair will help; the reader will feel a well-written scene that does not matter.
- **Applying the contract to dialogue.** Narration and speech have different rules; a narrator whose sentences match their characters' has lost the distance that lets the reader hear both.

### 7. Output format

```
Movement:      enters <state>, ends <state>, turn at beat <n>
Functions:     b1 land / b2 carry / b3 accelerate / b4 refuse / b5 land
Distribution:  <longest run of similar lengths> at <location>; intentional: yes/no
Repairs:       <repair> at <sentence> — because <function>
Voice:         attention <…> | syntax <…> | lexicon <…> | figuration <…> | attitude <…>
Refusals:      1 <…> / 2 <…> / 3 <…>
Neutral test:  lost <…>; kept <…>
Verdict:       pass | fail at <criterion> — <one line>
```

### 8. Micro-example

**Before** — five sentences of near-identical length and identical shape, in a beat that should be accelerating:

> She walked to the window and looked out at the yard. The yard was empty and the gate was open. She thought about the man in the kitchen. He had been kind to her for eleven years. She did not know what to do about that.

**After** — split, isolate, then extend:

> She walked to the window. The yard was empty, the gate open.
>
> Eleven years of kindness, and she did not know what to do about it.

The split separates looking from seeing, and the comma lets the emptiness arrive in the same breath. The thought about the man is dropped, because the man is already inside "eleven years of kindness". The isolation gives the turn its own air, and the coordination lets the second clause carry the judgement without naming it. Nothing was added that the scene did not already contain.

## E11 — Default-Avoidance / Continuation Divergence

### 1. Purpose

Convert the first available choice into a *visible* choice, then decide whether to keep it. The first idea is not a failure; it is a sample. But an unexamined sample is a decision made by the setup rather than by the author, and the reader can feel the difference even when they cannot name it.

The output is not novelty. It is a **receipt**: the modal version, the alternatives considered, the one chosen, and the licence that permitted it. Most of the time the modal version survives — and now it survives as a decision, which is all this engine promises.

### 2. When to use and when not

Use E11 at load-bearing decisions: the turn of a scene, the outcome of a plot beat, an ending, a reveal, the choice a character makes when the story's question is at stake. Also use it when a draft reads as competent and unowned, or when a beta reader says "I saw that coming" without meaning it as a compliment.

Do not use it on every sentence. Divergence applied uniformly produces texture without structure and prose that strains; the evidence supports concentration — the signal is strongest on paragraph-length units and weakest where everything already reads as distinctive. Do not use it on a genre beat the form has promised and the reader is owed: the reunion, the confrontation, the verdict. And do not use it when the setup is under-prepared — if no alternative has a licence, the problem is upstream in E1/E2/E8, and this engine will only generate contrivance.

### 3. Inputs

- The unit, with its boundary stated in one line: "scene 4, from her entering the workshop to the first lie".
- The scene card or equivalent: goal, obstacle, turn, cost.
- The licence pool: what the text has already established — traits, objects, wounds, debts, pressures. This is the material every alternative must draw on.
- The story's question, in one sentence. An alternative that does not touch the question is texture.

### 4. Procedure

**Step 1 — write the modal version in one sentence.** Not a paragraph, not a strawman. The thing that obviously happens next, phrased flatly: *she opens the unit and finds her mother's things.* Write it fast; the speed is the instrument. If you deliberate, you will produce an improvement rather than a baseline.

**Step 2 — generate 3–5 alternatives at the level of decision.** Declare the axis for each, one axis per alternative, from a fixed set: **who acts**, **what it costs**, **who pays**, **what is withheld**, **when it is known**, **who is telling**, **at what scale**. If two alternatives differ only in wording, you have produced one alternative and should discard one.

**Step 3 — score each on three questions**, one point each, writing the answer beside the point so the reasoning survives the number:

| Question | 1 point if… | 0 points if… |
|---|---|---|
| **Licence** | A named earlier line, object, trait, or pressure makes this available | "It's in character" or "none" |
| **Cost** | Something is lost by someone, and the text can spend it in scene | The cost exists only in the synopsis |
| **State change** | Knowledge, power, relationship, or possibility differs at the end | The scene ends where it began |

**Step 4 — choose.** Take the **highest-scoring non-modal alternative**. If it scores 3 and the modal scores 3, prefer the non-modal — the modal is available by default and does not need your help. If the best non-modal scores 2 and the modal scores 3, keep the modal **deliberately**, and make it specific (step 5b). If every non-modal scores 1 or less, stop: the setup is under-prepared, and the repair belongs to the scene that should have planted the licence.

**Step 5a — write the chosen version.** Write it as the scene, not as a sketch. The choice is not made until the prose exists, because a decision that cannot survive being written is a preference.

**Step 5b — or keep the modal and specify it.** Specificity is the fallback for a correct but generic choice. Replace the general with the particular that could only belong to this story: not *she finds her mother's things* but *she finds the jar of labelled screws, and the label on the hinge one is in a hand that is not her mother's*. The event is unchanged; the evidence is now unowned by anyone else. Specificity is not a substitute for divergence — it is what a deliberate modal choice requires in order not to read as a default.

**Step 6 — run the regeneration test.** Delete the chosen beat, keep the setup, and check what a competent re-writer would produce. The full protocol is below. If the regenerated scene is dramatically equivalent to yours — same event, same agent, same state change — the choice failed, and the fix is upstream: change the setup so the modal continuation stops being available, not the sentences.

**Step 7 — stop after two rounds.** Round one is the choice; round two is one repair of the setup if the regeneration test failed. A third round produces contrivance, because each additional divergence is bought with plausibility, and the account empties. Log the result and move on. A scene that fails twice is escalated to E1/E2, not re-tested.

**When the setup is the defect, E11 reports it and stops.** If no alternative has a licence, the missing material belongs to the scene that should have planted it (E8) or to the causal spine (E2). If the modal choice is the only one that serves the story's question, the question is too narrow for the scene (E1). Escalate with the receipt, not with a rewrite: the receipt turns "this scene feels flat" into a named missing licence.

### The regeneration protocol

Six steps. It takes one scene and about fifteen minutes, and it is the only part of E11 that produces evidence rather than opinion.

1. **Pick the hinge.** The beat where the scene turns — the decision, the reveal, the refusal. Cut immediately *before* it, not at the end of the scene. `[data: DNA-GPT]` The paper's optimal truncation ratio is around 0.5 of the text; ours is chosen for dramatic reasons, which is a deliberate departure.
2. **Cut 150–250 words** from the hinge onward and set them aside. Do not paraphrase them in your head, and do not leave them visible while regenerating. The object being measured is what the setup alone forces.
3. **Regenerate twice, cold.** Same POV, same tense, same voice contract; aim for *the* continuation, the one the setup makes easiest, not a good one. `[data: DNA-GPT]` The paper uses 5–20 samples and saturates around 10; two is a budget downgrade that catches agreement but cannot estimate a distribution. Say so when you report.
4. **Compare on four axes, in this order:** *event* (same action?), *agency* (same character causes it?), *state change* (same thing different at the end?), *left open* (which thread does the continuation refuse to resolve?). State change decides; the others explain.
5. **Apply the equivalence rule.** Equivalent means the same state change, through the same agent, in the same order of events — even if every sentence differs. Not equivalent means a different state change, or the two regenerations disagree with each other about what the scene is for. Wording is irrelevant at this step.
6. **Read the verdict.** Both equivalent → the beat was modal; rewrite the *setup*. Equivalent but differing in detail → the setup is under-determined; your version is one valid choice, acceptable only if you can name why. Not equivalent, and the two disagree → the scene carries authorship at the hinge; stop. Not equivalent because one continuation went somewhere impossible → the setup is unclear, not original; fix the setup.

**What the test cannot do.** It cannot tell you the modal beat was wrong — only that it was modal. It cannot run on atmosphere, on a passage shorter than a setup, or on a text the model has memorised. And it is a craft verdict on your own draft, never an accusation about authorship.

**Calibration by unit.** The same protocol does not apply at every scale, and running it outside its range produces confident nonsense.

| Unit | Cut | Cold regenerations | What the verdict is worth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sentence | none | — | Nothing. No setup, so agreement is trivial. Use the paraphrase test instead. |
| Scene turn | 150–250 words before the hinge | 2 | Agreement detection. Enough to say "this beat was modal", not enough to estimate a distribution. |
| Plot turn | the outcome, plus the two beats after it | 3–5 | Stronger than prose, because outcomes are more constrained than sentences. |
| Chapter or longer | the last scene's hinge | 2 | A sample of the chapter, not a verdict on it. |

**The dialogue variant.** An exchange has a hinge too — the line where the scene turns — and the cut goes before it, keeping the preceding three or four lines as the setup. Regenerate the reply cold, twice, from the setup and the voice contracts of both speakers. The comparison axes change slightly: *who speaks next*, *what they do with the other's act*, *whether the exchange ends in the same power balance*. A regenerated exchange that lands the same information in the same order through the same speaker is a modal exchange, however well the lines are written — and the repair is not better banter but a different thing being wanted in the room.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- **The modal version was never written**, so there is nothing to compare and the "choice" is a preference with a rationale attached afterwards.
- **The alternatives are paraphrases.** Two alternatives that a single sentence could cover are one alternative; the axis was not real.
- **Every alternative has no licence.** The scene is under-prepared, and the divergence is being used to buy originality with a turn instead of with setup.
- **The chosen alternative scores 3 but does not touch the story's question.** It is a good decision in a different story.
- **Specificity was used instead of divergence without recording it.** Keeping the modal is legitimate; keeping it silently is the failure this engine exists to catch.
- **The regeneration test is equivalent and the response was a rewrite of the sentences.** The setup was the problem; sentence-level work cannot fix a modal scene.
- **A third round was run.** Contrivance is the predictable result, and the engine's own budget names two rounds.

### 6. Typical errors

- **Strawmanning the modal version.** Writing an obviously bad default so the alternative wins. The baseline must be the *best* obvious version, or the whole test is theatre.
- **Divergence at the wrong level.** Rewriting a sentence when the decision is modal, or changing a plot turn when the problem is that the prose is dead. Name the unit before you run the pass.
- **Alternatives generated from the same axis.** Three versions of "who acts" is one axis, not three alternatives. Rotate the axis deliberately.
- **Choosing the least likely option.** The inverse of the default, which is another default. Unpredictable without licence is arbitrary, and readers experience it as noise.
- **Ignoring the genre promise.** A crime novel owes the reader a solution; refusing it in the name of originality breaks a contract the reader paid for. Deliberate modal choices are how forms keep their promises *and* remain authored.
- **Running the test on a scene that has no hinge.** Atmosphere, description, and transition have no turn, so the test returns agreement for trivial reasons — the setup determined everything because there was almost no setup.
- **Treating the regeneration result as a score.** It is a verdict about dramatic equivalence, produced by two samples. It is not a measurement, and no number from the papers belongs in a critique.

### 8. Micro-example

The scene-level walkthrough below runs longer than the 60–120-word micro-example format, because E11's output is a scored table rather than a paragraph. The sentence-level pair at the end is the short demonstration.

**Scene level.** Setup: Nadia manages a storage facility. A man arrives to clear unit 114, rented in his mother's name; he has no key and no death certificate.

The four versions: the modal opens the unit and hands over the photographs; A1 refuses by the book and lets him go politely; A2 opens it and finds it emptied three weeks earlier by the mother herself; A3 opens it and hides the contents from the reader.

| Version | Axis | Licence | Cost | State change | Score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Modal** | — | the paperwork is in order | none | the unit is emptied; nothing Nadia can act on | 1 |
| **A1** | who pays | the rules she enforces daily | she goes home and checks her own mother's unit | her own arrangement becomes visible to her, and nothing else | 2 |
| **A2** | what it costs | the cleaning is in the access log | a lie, or the loss of his gratitude | she knows the mother is alive, and must act | 3 |
| **A3** | what is withheld | the reader's access, not hers | none in-story | the reader's knowledge changes; hers does not | 2 |

A2 wins on all three. A1 is the near-miss and the useful one: it has a licence and a real private cost, but the scene's question — the man and his mother — is untouched, so the change is hers alone and the scene ends where it began for everyone else. A3 scores the same as A1 for a worse reason: the state change happens in the reader, and the withheld contents are information Nadia already has, which is cheating unless her not-looking is characterised.

Withholding runs one way only. The reader may know more than the character — that is irony, and it is free. The character may not know more than the reader without a reason the text has established, because that is the narrator lying by omission.

Regeneration from the same setup produces the modal or A1, not A2, because the cleaning record is a detail the setup does not force anyone to notice. The scene carries authorship at the hinge.

**Sentence level.** Modal: *She felt a wave of grief.* The axis is *what the sentence is for* — report the feeling, or report the fact that carries it. Alternative: *The tape was still sticky.* Licence: the jar has been in the drawer throughout. Cost: the orientation that naming the grief would give — the reader has to infer the emotion from the object, and the character gets no relief. State change: the reader now knows the dead woman handled the tape recently, which the character does not. Chosen. Regeneration from the same paragraph produces the first sentence almost every time, because naming the emotion is the setup's easiest move.

**If the modal had been kept**, specificity would require something like *She felt the grief arrive the way the dentist's anaesthetic does, half a face at a time* — the same decision, made unshareable. That is a legitimate outcome; the receipt is what makes it one.

## E12 — Narrative Diversity (portfolio)

### 1. Purpose

Manage originality across a *body* of work rather than inside a single piece. The failure this engine catches is invisible from inside any one story: five defensible pieces that are the same piece in five costumes. The output is a profile of the work on six dimensions, the cluster it reveals, and one axis to move on the next unit.

### 2. When to use and when not

Use E12 for a collection, a series, a long work with many chapters or sections, or a writer's backlist being assembled. Also use it when a reader who has read two of your pieces says the third is "the same, somehow" and cannot say how.

Do not use it on a single short story — there is no portfolio yet, and applying the sheet to one unit produces arbitrary variety. Do not use it to make every chapter different: a long work needs internal consistency, and a novel whose chapters each innovate on a different dimension has no shape. Do not use it to chase the rare tail of a distribution: originality is proxied by rarity in the research, and rarity is not merit.

**The single long work.** A novel is usually one unit, but a long work with distinct parts — a braided novel, a book of linked novellas, a serial — can be scored part by part. Ask whether the parts differ in *decision* or only in *event*. If only in event, the work has one unit and the matrix should say so, rather than being gerrymandered into variety.

### 3. Inputs

- The units, defined: stories in a collection, books in a series, parts of a long work. Decide the unit before scoring, or the matrix will mix scales.
- One sentence of *decision* per unit — not of subject. *The professional chooses the task over the person and is not punished.* This sentence is what the scoring will confirm or contradict.
- The genre contracts each unit owes its reader, so that a "repeated" dimension can be distinguished from a promised one.

### 4. Procedure

1. **Score every unit on six dimensions, 0–3, using the anchors.** Score the executed work, not the intention.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Premise type** | Same situation as another unit, different nouns | Same family of situation (a return, an inheritance) | Related but distinct pressure | A different kind of pressure entirely |
| **Structure** | Same order and framing | Same order, different frame | Different order, same number of lines | Different architecture (braid, frame, reverse, mosaic) |
| **POV / voice** | Same person, distance, register | Same person, different register | Different person or distance | Different reliability or a different teller entirely |
| **Moral frame** | Same judgement, same standard | Same standard, different verdict | Different standard of judgement | The unit refuses to judge where the others judged |
| **Ending mode** | Same mode | Same mode with a different final image | Different mode (resolved vs deferred) | The ending changes what kind of story it was |
| **Sensory / emotional register** | Same dominant channel and temperature | Same channel, different temperature | Different channel, same temperature | A different channel and a different temperature |

2. **Write the vector, not the average.** Each unit is a six-number profile: `premise 2, structure 1, POV 0, frame 0, ending 1, register 1`. Do not sum or average. `[hypothesis]` An average hides exactly the thing you are looking for — two units can average 1.5 with completely different profiles, and three units can average the same while clustering on one axis.
3. **Find the cluster.** `[hypothesis]` Two units that score within one point of each other on four or more of the six dimensions are the same unit twice, whatever their subjects. Mark the shared dimensions; the cluster is the signature.
4. **Separate signature from rut.** The signature is the dimension you return to with a reason and can defend in a sentence. The rut is the dimension you did not notice returning. Both look identical in the matrix; only the defence distinguishes them. Write the defence or mark it a rut.
5. **Change one dimension for the next unit, by at least two points.** Hold the other five. Changing three dimensions at once produces a unit that belongs to a different body of work, which is how a collection loses its reader.
6. **Check that the change is a dimension, not a subject.** A new setting, century, or profession scores 0 on every dimension — it is the same unit in new furniture. The test from `references/originality.md`: describe the new unit in one sentence of decision. If the sentence matches an earlier one, nothing has moved.
7. **Check consistency after drafting.** The change must not break the world's rules, the series' promises, or the voice the reader came for. Diversity is variation inside a contract, not the cancellation of one. If the new unit requires the reader to relearn the book, the change was too large or in the wrong dimension.
8. **Re-score the drafted unit and record the vector.** Intention and execution diverge; the score is of the draft. Append it to the matrix and stop — do not iterate until the numbers look varied. The matrix is a diagnostic, not a target.

A worked matrix, five stories, scored from the drafts:

| Unit | Premise | Structure | POV | Frame | Ending | Register | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| U1 nurse | 3 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | task over person, unpunished |
| U2 translator | 2 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | task over person, unpunished |
| U3 locksmith | 3 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 2 | 1 | task over person, unpunished |
| U4 stenographer | 2 | 2 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | task over person, unpunished |
| U5 courier | 3 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 2 | task over person, unpunished |

Five units, four dimensions within a point of each other, one decision sentence repeated. The matrix is not subtle: premise varies, structure barely moves, and the moral frame does not move at all.

### 5. Criteria — what counts as failure

- **The average was used.** A single number per unit, or a portfolio mean, hides the cluster by construction.
- **The same decision sentence fits three or more units.** The premise varies and the decision does not; this is the core failure the engine exists to catch.
- **A dimension was "changed" by changing the subject.** New setting, same profile: 0 on all six.
- **Three or more dimensions moved at once.** The unit is now a different project, and the collection has no through-line.
- **The change broke a promise.** A series that stops delivering its contract in the name of variety loses the reader it had.
- **The matrix was optimised.** Scores were adjusted until the profile looked diverse, which produces a body of work designed by spreadsheet.

### 6. Typical errors

- **Confusing diversity with inconsistency.** A collection can be wildly varied in premise and utterly consistent in voice; that is a signature. Inconsistency is variation the reader cannot integrate, usually because it arrived without a frame.
- **Scoring the premise and stopping.** Premise is the most visible dimension and the least load-bearing. The clusters that hurt are in moral frame and ending mode.
- **Unit inflation.** Scoring chapters of one novel as if they were separate works, then concluding the book is repetitive. A novel is one unit for most purposes; use the parts only to check that the middle is not the beginning again.
- **Series blindness.** A long series legitimately repeats world and voice. The repeated *moral frame* is the problem: if every book delivers the same verdict, the series has one ending and many plots.
- **Using rarity as a target.** Pushing each unit toward the statistical tail produces incoherence, and the research's own framing treats rarity as a proxy for originality, not a measure of quality.

### 7. Output format

```
Units:      <n>, defined as <collection stories | series books | long-work parts>
Matrix:
  U1  premise 2 | structure 1 | POV 0 | frame 0 | ending 1 | register 1
  U2  …
Cluster:    U1/U3/U4 within 1 on <dims> — same decision: <one sentence>
Signature:  <dimension> — defence: <one line>
Rut:        <dimension> — undefended
Next unit:  change <dimension> from <x> to <x+2>; hold the rest
Verdict:    pass | fail — <which criterion>
```

### 8. Micro-example

Five stories: a nurse, a translator, a locksmith, a stenographer, a courier. Premise scores 2–3 across the set; structure 1 everywhere; POV 0; moral frame 0; ending 1; register 1. One cluster on four dimensions, and the decision sentence is identical five times: *the professional chooses the task over the person and is not punished*. The premises are diverse and the body of work is one story.

The next unit changes the moral frame by two points: keep the professional, and make the task and the person the same thing, so that choosing one is choosing both. Everything else holds. The sixth story is now in the same world as the other five and is not the same story.

## How these engines combine

The three engines work at different scales and fail into each other. E11 decides; E10 executes; E12 audits the set. Run them in that order within a unit, and in the reverse order across a project.

**The sequence for a single scene.** E11 first, because a beautifully written modal scene is the most expensive kind of waste. Then E10, on the version that survived, because the voice has to carry the decision and a decision the prose cannot carry will read as an idea. Then the scene gate in `SKILL.md`, which will catch the case where both passes were competent and the scene still changes nothing.

**The sequence for a project.** E12 first, before the next unit is planned: the matrix tells you which axis is available, and that constraint is more useful than a blank page. Then E11 at the unit's load-bearing decisions. Then E10 at the paragraph.

**Where they conflict, and how to resolve it.**

- **E10 can make a modal scene beautiful.** Voice work is seductive: the sentences get better and the scene gets no less inevitable. If the regeneration test is equivalent, stop polishing and go back to the setup. The prose was never the problem.
- **E11 can produce a decision the voice cannot carry.** A choice that requires a register the narrator does not have will read as a stunt. Either the voice contract expands deliberately (and the expansion is recorded), or the choice is wrong for this teller.
- **E12 can push variety into incoherence.** A unit that changes three dimensions to satisfy the matrix is a unit written for the matrix. The portfolio rule is one dimension, held.
- **E12 and E11 can both claim the same decision.** The distinction: E11 asks whether *this* choice is modal within the scene; E12 asks whether the *kind* of choice is modal across the body of work. A scene can pass E11 and still be the fifth time you have made that kind of decision.

**Stop rules.** Two rounds of E11, one repair pass of E10, one dimension per E12 unit. When a budget runs out, the defect is upstream and further work at this level produces polish on a structural problem.

**What each engine hands to the next.**

| Engine | Produces | Consumed by | If skipped |
|---|---|---|---|
| E11 | a receipt: modal version, alternatives, licence, verdict | the prose pass, and the decision ledger | a well-written scene nobody chose |
| E10 | sentence functions, the repairs made, the voice contract | every later scene in that narrator's voice | each scene arrives in a slightly different register, and the book has no teller |
| E12 | a six-number vector per unit, and one axis to move | the plan for the next unit | the body of work converges and the fifth piece is the first one again |

**A route, in order.** Take a scene that reads flat. State its movement (E10 step 1); if you cannot, the defect is E8 and no prose pass will find it. Run E11 at the turn and look at the receipt; if the modal version survives with a licence and a cost, keep it and go to E10. If the receipt shows no alternative had a licence, stop and fix the setup. Only after the decision is settled does E10 earn its time: functions, distribution, repairs, contract. Then, once per project rather than once per scene, score the unit on the six dimensions and ask which axis the *next* unit will move.

**What none of them do.** They do not measure. There is no probability, no divergence score, no detection, no rarity percentile computed on your draft. The papers supply the shape of the problem; the tests are craft procedures run by hand, and their output is a verdict with a reason, not a number.

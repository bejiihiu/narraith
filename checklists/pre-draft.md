# Pre-draft checks

> Run before the first sentence. A "no" here costs a minute; the same "no" found in revision costs the draft.

Answer in a scratch note, not in the reply. The artifact stays behind.

## The ten decisions

- [ ] **Question.** Can you phrase what the reader wants answered as a question with at least two possible answers? *If it fails:* you have a topic. Find the decision the story is waiting on and write toward it — or open the ending so the answer is genuinely in doubt.

- [ ] **Pressure.** Name the force that resists, and one reason it is right from inside itself. *If it fails:* you have a villain, not pressure. Give the opposition something it is trying to avoid losing.

- [ ] **Cost.** Name what acting loses, and what succeeding loses. *If it fails:* the stakes exist only in the synopsis. Add a loss the character would feel on the page today, not at the climax.

- [ ] **Information plan.** List the three or four revelations in the order the reader receives them, and name what keeps each gap open until then. *If it fails:* the plot will leak. Fix what the reader knows at the midpoint and what they cannot know yet.

- [ ] **Time.** Which order serves the question, and why is it not chronological? *If it fails:* you took the default. Find one scene whose meaning changes when moved, and move it.

- [ ] **Refusals.** Name two obvious solutions you are rejecting, and what replaces each. *If it fails:* you will write the modal draft. Write three alternatives in one line each and choose with a reason.

- [ ] **Voice contract.** POV, distance, tense, register, the narrator's attitude toward the material, three prohibitions, three obligations. *If it fails:* the draft will find a voice by accident and lose it by the third scene.

- [ ] **Length form.** Name the form and what it promises about scope. *If it fails:* a novella's material compressed into a short story reads as summary, and a story's material stretched to a novel reads as padding.

- [ ] **Genre promise.** Name the contract the reader is owed, and the one place you will honour it unusually. *If it fails:* you will break the contract by accident or obey it by default — both are failures, and the second is harder to see.

- [ ] **One image.** Name one concrete object that recurs and changes meaning across the story. *If it fails:* the story has no anchor for its meaning, and the theme will end up stated because there is nothing else to infer it from.

## Tier check

- **Tier A** (a line, an image, an exchange under ~300 words): skip this file. Write. Check only that it contains one concrete thing and does not explain itself.
- **Tier B** (scene, story, chapter): run 1–6 in your head. Five minutes, no document.
- **Tier C** (novella, novel, continuation, multi-thread): run all ten and keep the answers where you can re-read them at chapter three.

## Worked instance

For a story about a woman asked to cover her father's debt. Not for output — for calibrating how short an answer can be.

1. *Will Anna take the loan, and what will it make her?* Two answers, both live.
2. The sister's kindness: she is right that the family will lose the house, and she is using that to win an old argument.
3. Signing costs the nine-year refusal that was Anna's only independence. Refusing costs the house.
4. Reader learns: the debt exists (p1); it is the father's (p2, before the dinner); Anna has refused it before (p3, from the sister, not from Anna).
5. Chronological, except the father's death, which is withheld until after the signature so the reader reads the signature twice.
6. Refused: the sister is secretly in debt herself; the father is alive. Replaced by: the sister is right, which is worse.
7. First person, close, present tense, dry register, contempt for euphemism. Never: explain the father. Never: let Anna cry on the page.
8. Novella. Three weeks, one house, four people.
9. Domestic realism's contract is that the change is internal and the world does not adjust. Honoured unusually: the money is real and the sums are exact.
10. The second cup. Put out, not put out, put out again.

## The gate

- Checks 1–3 are blocking. A "no" on any of them means the draft is not ready; write the answer first.
- Checks 4–10 are not blocking. A "no" means you mark an assumption and start anyway — the draft will answer it faster than more planning will.
- If you cannot answer a blocking check and the user is present, ask one question. If the user is not present, choose the most defensible answer, name it as an assumption in one line, and proceed.

## Not a questionnaire

Never put these ten questions to the user as a form. Infer what the material implies, and name only the assumptions that would change the draft. A writer who wanted a scene should not receive an intake interview.

## Record

- Keep one line per decision, in a scratch file beside the draft.
- Re-read it at the start of every session and after every structural change. A decision that no longer matches the text is either a stale note or a drift — decide which before writing on.
- Delete the note when the draft is finished. It is scaffolding, and shipping it teaches the writer to plan instead of write.

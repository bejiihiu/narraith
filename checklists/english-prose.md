# English prose checks

> Twelve checks for text written in English. Each names a mechanism; none of them is a ban on a word.

## Sentence and paragraph

- [ ] **Register holds.** One diction level across the narration, and a different one in dialogue, consistently. *If it fails:* an anachronism, a Latinate word in a scene of Anglo-Saxon monosyllables, or a tonal wobble between paragraphs. Find the outlier and bring it to the surrounding level — or make the shift a character's, not the narrator's.

- [ ] **Free indirect discourse is marked.** When the narration slides into a character's idiom, tense, pronouns and deictics stay consistent and the slide is signalled by diction. *If it fails:* the reader cannot tell whether the judgement is the narrator's or the character's. Signal it with one idiom the narrator would not use, then keep the grammar steady until the slide out.

- [ ] **No accidental comma splices.** Two independent clauses joined by a comma, unless the voice uses them as a rhythm. *If it fails:* use a full stop, a semicolon, or a dash — and check whether the second clause was meant to be subordinate, in which case the sentence is doing two jobs.

- [ ] **Latinate fog cleared.** Nominalisations and abstract nouns where a verb and a concrete subject belong: *utilisation, implementation, in order to, prior to, commence*. *If it fails:* find the verb the noun is hiding and give it a subject. *The implementation of the policy was undertaken* becomes *the council applied the policy*.

- [ ] **Rhythm varies by function.** Long for a complex thought or a sustained image, short for a landing. *If it fails:* every sentence is within a few words of the same length, so nothing is emphasised. Read aloud; where you run out of breath, split; where the sentence lands a change, cut it to the verb.

- [ ] **Paragraph shape.** One beat per paragraph; the break lands before the explanation, not after it. *If it fails:* the paragraph contains two thoughts and the second is buried. Split at the turn, and check that dialogue puts each speaker in their own paragraph.

## Diction

- [ ] **Filter words cut.** *She saw, felt, noticed, heard, realised* stand between the reader and the event. *If it fails:* the POV is already established, so the filter adds a layer with no information. Delete the filter and let the perception be the sentence: *she noticed the door was open* becomes *the door was open*.

- [ ] **The adverb is not doing the verb's work.** *Walked quickly, said angrily, closed it firmly.* *If it fails:* replace the pair with the one verb that contains the adverb — *hurried, snapped, slammed* — or cut the adverb and let the line carry the manner. Keep the adverb where it contradicts the verb on purpose.

- [ ] **Sentence openings vary.** No default *There was / It was*, and no stack of participial openings (*Walking to the door, she…*). *If it fails:* the first three words of every sentence do the same grammatical job and the prose reads as a list. Front an object, an adverbial, or a subordinate clause.

- [ ] **Concrete over evaluative.** *Beautiful, terrible, amazing, significant* replaced by the property that produced the judgement. *If it fails:* the reader is told the response instead of given the cause. Name the thing that is beautiful and let the reader supply the adjective.

- [ ] **Tense and person hold.** No drift inside a scene; the past perfect used for anterior events only, and released as soon as the anteriority is clear. *If it fails:* a paragraph slides from past to present, or a page is a thicket of *had had*. Anchor the tense at the scene's first sentence and drop the past perfect after the first verb.

- [ ] **Dialogue tags disappear.** *Said* and *asked* by default; an action beat where the beat carries information; no adverbial tags and no said-bookisms (*opined, ejaculated, retorted*). *If it fails:* the tag is telling the actor how to play the line. Cut the adverb; if the manner is not in the line, the line is wrong.

## What is not a defect

- **Sentence fragments.** A fragment is a rhythm decision. It fails only when it is unintentional or when it hides a missing verb the reader needed.
- **Adverbs.** One adverb per hundred words is not a problem; an adverb propping up a weak verb is. Judge the pair, not the count.
- **The em dash, the semicolon, *suddenly*.** Each is a tool with conditions. A dash for an interruptible thought, a semicolon for two clauses that need each other, *suddenly* for the one event that is genuinely sudden.
- **Passive voice.** Correct when the agent is unknown, unimportant, or being concealed — and the last is a craft decision, not a grammar error.
- **A comma splice inside a voice.** If the narrator's syntax is run-on by design, the splice is the voice. The check is consistency, not correctness.
- **Repetition.** A repeated word can be the point. The defect is a repeated word doing no work, not repetition itself.

## Two tests

- **Read a page aloud.** Every place you stumble is a rhythm or syntax defect; every place you run out of breath is a sentence that needs splitting; every place you speed up is working.
- **Count the openings.** Take twenty consecutive sentences and write down their first three words. If more than half share a grammatical shape — subject, pronoun, *There was* — the prose has a default. Vary one in three and re-read.

## One-minute pass

Three checks catch most of it: read a page aloud, scan for comma splices, and search the page for *-tion* and *-ment*. Everything else here needs a slower read and a decision.

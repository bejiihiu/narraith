# Continuity sweep

> Fourteen checks on a finished draft. Build the list from the text, not from memory — memory is what produced the contradictions.

## Facts and bodies

- [ ] **Hard facts.** Every date, age, distance and duration, collected in one list and compared. *If it fails:* two dates disagree. Pick the one the plot needs and change the other, then search for every mention of both.

- [ ] **Injuries.** Each wound has a location, a severity, a treatment and a recovery time, and the character's capability matches it in every later scene. *If it fails:* a broken wrist lifts a suitcase in chapter nine. Either heal it on the page or add the limitation into the action.

- [ ] **Objects.** Each load-bearing object has one description, one location and one owner. *If it fails:* the letter is in the coat in chapter four and the drawer in chapter eleven. Choose the location the scene needs and update every reference.

- [ ] **Weather and season.** Consistent with the calendar, and with what characters wear and can do. *If it fails:* it is February and the windows are open in the south of England. Either move the scene or use the weather as pressure instead of decoration.

- [ ] **Technology.** Every device does what the fiction's world says it does, and its limits are stable. *If it fails:* the phone works in the cellar this time. Fix the limit, or make the failure a plot event rather than an oversight.

- [ ] **Money.** Sums, prices, who owes whom, what has been paid. *If it fails:* the debt is 40,000 on one page and 14,000 on another. Recalculate the whole chain and correct it everywhere; money errors are the ones readers notice most, because they do arithmetic while reading.

## Knowledge and debts

- [ ] **Knowledge.** For each character, list what they know and when they learned it. *If it fails:* someone refers to a fact they are told two chapters later. Either move the discovery earlier or cut the reference — never let a character know something for convenience.

- [ ] **Debts.** Promises, threats, bargains, unanswered questions. *If it fails:* someone promises to visit and never does, with no consequence. Either pay the debt or convert the default into an event the story notices.

- [ ] **Off-page events.** What happened while the POV was elsewhere, who was told, and what they now believe. *If it fails:* two characters discuss an event neither was present for and neither was told about. Add the telling in summary, or remove the reference.

## World and names

- [ ] **Names.** Spelling, capitalisation, and every variant — nicknames, surnames, titles. *If it fails:* a name has two spellings. Pick one and replace all instances, including in dialogue and in the narrator's references.

- [ ] **Geography.** Distances, directions, travel times, and what is visible from where. *If it fails:* the station is a ten-minute walk in chapter one and an hour's drive in chapter six. Fix the map, not the scene, unless the scene depends on the distance.

- [ ] **Time.** Time of day, elapsed time between scenes, and whether the season and the moon agree. *If it fails:* the same evening lasts three chapters and then it is dawn twice. Write the timeline out in one column and re-anchor the scenes to it.

- [ ] **Forms of address.** Who uses a first name, a patronymic, a rank, a diminutive, and when that changes. *If it fails:* a character is `ты` in one scene and `вы` in the next with no event between. Make the shift an event, or restore the form.

## Promises to the reader

- [ ] **Every promise is kept or deliberately broken.** A gun on the wall, a name mentioned twice, a question the narration raises — each is a promise. *If it fails:* a detail was given weight and then dropped. Either pay it in a later scene or reduce its weight in the original so it reads as furniture.

## What the sweep cannot fix

- **A contradiction the plot needs.** If the plot requires the character to have known all along, the sweep finds a structural problem, not a typing error. Do not paper over it with a line of explanation — change what they knew, or make the concealment the story.
- **An apparent contradiction under unreliable narration.** Check the claim against the narrator's knowledge and motives, not the author's. A liar's inconsistency is characterisation; the test is whether the text gives the reader a way to suspect it.
- **An error nobody can see.** Readers miss most continuity slips. Fix them anyway — not because they will be caught, but because the ledger is the only thing keeping the author's model of the world consistent while they write the next chapter.

## Running the sweep

1. Sweep backwards, from the last chapter to the first. Backwards is faster because a late fact tells you what the earlier text must have said.
2. Two passes, two questions: *what contradicts* (this list) and *what is unresolved* (the promises).
3. Record every fix in the ledger as you make it. A fix made without a record is a new contradiction waiting for the next sweep.

A contradiction is not a detail problem. It is the reader's reason to stop trusting the narration, and it costs more than the error deserves.

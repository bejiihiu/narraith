# Russian prose checks

> Twelve checks for text written in Russian. The failure this list exists for is translationese: Russian sentences distributing information the way English does.

Russian examples are given in italics with an English gloss. The point of each is the mechanism, not the phrase.

## Sentence mechanics

- [ ] **Pronouns dropped.** Consecutive sentences beginning *он / она / они* where the verb ending already names the subject. *If it fails:* delete the pronoun wherever the verb form carries the person; keep it at a paragraph's first sentence or where the emphasis falls on *who*, not *what*.

- [ ] **Aspect carries the meaning.** Each verb's choice of perfective or imperfective matches its job: imperfective for process, repetition, background; perfective for a single completed act, a result. *If it fails:* a one-time completed action sits in the imperfective (*она открывала дверь* — she was opening / used to open it) when the scene needs *она открыла дверь*. Re-aspect the verb; be suspicious of *начал / стал* + infinitive, which is often a calque for a perfective.

- [ ] **Word order does informational work.** Known information early, new information late; the rheme lands at the end. *If it fails:* every sentence runs subject–verb–object, and the intonation goes flat. Front the adverbial, move the new fact to the final position, invert where the sentence's weight is wrong.

- [ ] **Participle and gerund chains broken.** No more than one participial phrase (*причастный оборот*) or verbal adverb (*деепричастие*) per sentence, and never three in a row. *If it fails:* Russian tolerates more than English here, which is exactly how the bureaucratic chain gets in. Split into a finite clause or a new sentence; keep the phrase that carries information the clause cannot.

- [ ] **Relative clauses thinned.** Three or more *который* in a paragraph is English sentence architecture wearing Russian words. *If it fails:* turn one into an apposition or a dash construction, and restructure another into a second sentence. Do not simply convert them all to participles — that trades one chain for another.

- [ ] **Long sentences exist.** At least one periodic sentence per page or two: subordinate material first, predicate delayed. *If it fails:* the prose is a run of short declaratives, which in Russian reads as translated or as a report. Build the period; keep the short sentence for the landing.

## Voice and stance

- [ ] **Particles present.** *же, ведь, вот, -то, -ка, ли, всё-таки, просто* — the markers of stance. *If it fails:* their total absence is the strongest single translationese signal, because English has no systematic equivalent and the calque drops them. Add one where the attitude is missing: *это же твой отец* is not the same sentence as *это твой отец*.

- [ ] **Narrator keeps its licence.** Russian narration may comment, digress and generalise; the invisible camera is an English import. *If it fails:* you have neutralised the voice to satisfy a rule from another tradition. Let the narrator have an attitude, and let one digression stand.

- [ ] **Register holds.** No bureaucratic calques where a verb belongs: *является, данный, осуществлять, имеет место, в рамках, на сегодняшний день*. *If it fails:* find the verb the noun is hiding and give it a subject. *Осуществляет контроль* becomes *проверяет*.

- [ ] **Address is deliberate.** *Ты / вы*, name, patronymic, diminutive — chosen per relationship and changed only as an event. *If it fails:* a shift between scenes with nothing between them reads as an error, not a decision. Make the shift happen on the page, or restore the form.

## Conventions

- [ ] **Dialogue punctuation is one system.** Dialogue is set with the dash (*— Реплика.*), not with quotation marks; guillemets are for quoted speech *inside* narration (*Он сказал: «Я не вернусь»*). The author's words after a dash are lowercase, and a quotation followed by the author's words takes a comma and a dash, not a full stop. *If it fails:* mixed systems and English-style quote placement. Convert the whole book; a manuscript that switches conventions in chapter nine looks unedited.

- [ ] **Typographic consistency.** *« »* not `" "`, an em dash *—* not a hyphen, *ё* either used throughout or not at all, ellipsis as a single character if the house style says so. *If it fails:* the inconsistency is not a craft defect but it is the first thing a Russian reader sees, and it signals that nobody read the file.

## Two tests

- **The particle test.** Take a paragraph of dialogue and delete every particle. If nothing is lost, the voice was already flat — the particles were absent to begin with.
- **The rheme test.** Cover the last three words of each sentence. If the sentence still makes its main point, the word order is carrying no information and the new fact is in the wrong place.

## What is not a defect

- **Lexical repetition.** Russian prose repeats a key word on purpose; the English habit of elegant variation — swapping in a synonym to avoid saying *дверь* twice — reads as nervousness. Repetition is a defect only when the repeated word is doing no work.
- **Short sentences.** In a *сказ* narrator, a child's view, or a hard-boiled register, the declarative run is the voice. The check above applies to neutral narration, not to a narrator whose flatness is characterisation.
- **A long period.** One sentence of eighty words with the predicate at the end is a resource, not an excess. The defect is a page of them, not one.
- **A single participial phrase.** One is normal Russian. The chain is the problem, not the construction.
- **Inverted order in emotional speech.** *Странный он человек* is not a mistake; it is how the evaluation lands. Restoring subject–verb order would flatten it.

## Order of repair

Aspect and word order first — they change what the sentence says. Then chains, particles and register, which change how it sounds. Punctuation and typography last, in one pass, because they are mechanical and cheap.

Everything here is `[theory]` — Russian craft norms, not measurement. Do not port English-calibrated statistics (sentence-length averages, function-word ratios) onto Russian and call the result an analysis; the fingerprinting study's own cross-lingual numbers are a warning about exactly that.

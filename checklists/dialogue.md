# Dialogue checks

> Twelve checks on one exchange. If more than three fail, the exchange needs a rewrite, not a repair.

- [ ] **Two agendas.** Write what each speaker wants from the other *in this scene*, in the margin above their first line. *If it fails:* if the agendas are identical, one speaker is redundant; if either is missing, the exchange is exposition. Invent the want before rewriting a line.

- [ ] **Nothing is asked for directly — unless transparency is the effect.** Each speaker pursues the agenda by indirection: a test, a provocation, a complaint about something else. *If it fails:* they are negotiating, not talking. Give each one a reason the direct request would cost too much. *The exception:* a confession, a declaration, a briefing, a formal warning, or a scene whose subject is the difficulty of saying the thing. There the direct line is the choice, and the failure is blurring it.

- [ ] **Reply to the act.** For every reply, name the act it answers: testing, retreating, claiming innocence, changing the subject. *If it fails:* the reply answers only the literal content. Replace it with a line that responds to what the other person is *doing*.

- [ ] **No on-the-nose lines — unless the line is the event.** No character states the theme or the relationship's condition as a summary. *If it fails:* you have written the subtext as text; cut the summary and let the evasion around it carry the meaning. *The exception:* a character may name their own motive or the relationship accurately when the naming is itself the act — a confession, a diagnosis, a refusal dressed as honesty. The test is whether the line changes what the other person can do next.

- [ ] **Surface is real.** The ostensible subject is genuinely discussable — the parking fee, the sister's operation, the shift rota. *If it fails:* the surface is a thin pretext and the reader feels the puppetry. Make the ostensible subject matter enough that they would discuss it anyway.

- [ ] **Idiolect by syntax.** Cover the names: can you tell who is speaking? Each speaker has a sentence shape and a small set of recurring words. *If it fails:* everyone speaks in your prose. Give one character short flat declaratives and another subordinate clauses, and remove the phonetic spelling of accent.

- [ ] **Silences are placed.** Who refuses to answer, who answers a question with a question, who changes the subject — and whether that is visible without a stage direction. *If it fails:* the pauses are marked instead of dramatised. Delete "he paused" and make the next line do the pausing.

- [ ] **Logistics carry.** The precise time, the exact sum, the name nobody will say. *If it fails:* the specifics are neutral furniture. Keep the one detail that reveals who is in control of the conversation.

- [ ] **The ritual is cut.** No greetings, no confirmations, no "as you know". *If it fails:* enter after the opening and leave before the close. The first line of pressure is the first line.

- [ ] **The shift is locatable.** Name the line where something changes: a concession, a threat made real, a fact released, an alliance moved. *If it fails:* no shift means no scene. Find the pressure the exchange is avoiding and let one speaker apply it.

- [ ] **The scene ends on the shift.** If the shift is in the last line, the scene is correctly shaped. *If it fails:* everything after the shift is a farewell. Cut it, or move the shift to the end.

- [ ] **Tags are invisible.** `said` and `asked` by default; action beats where the beat is information. *If it fails:* an adverbial tag ("she said bitterly") is the writer telling the actor how to play it. Cut the adverb; if the bitterness is not in the line, the line is wrong.
- [ ] **The brief's own constraint.** If the user asked for directness, brevity, or a plain register, check the text against that constraint before the ten rules above. A rule that overrides the brief is a defect in the application, not in the story.

## Three things not to confuse

- **Dialect with idiolect.** Spelling an accent phonetically marks origin; it does not make a character. Syntax makes a character.
- **Silence with absence.** A character who says nothing is still acting, if the others have to work around the silence. If nobody notices, the silence is a hole.
- **Conflict with volume.** Two people being polite while one of them is losing something is higher conflict than two people shouting. Volume is the cheapest signal of stakes and the first one draft dialogue reaches for.

## Common failures and their causes

- **Everyone sounds the same.** You wrote the narrator's voice into every mouth. Cause: no idiolect decision. Fix the syntax of one speaker only, and the others will separate by contrast.
- **Everyone is clever.** Two performers, not two people. Cause: the lines are written to be quotable. Give one speaker a line that fails — a joke that does not land, a point made badly.
- **Nothing changes.** No shift. Cause: the scene was written to deliver information, so no one had a reason to win anything.
- **Everyone agrees too fast.** No agendas, or the same agenda twice. Cause: the writer needed the plot to advance. Make one character refuse something trivial, and the agreement has to be earned.

## The two-minute test

1. Cover the names. Read the exchange. If you cannot assign at least half the lines, idiolect has failed.
2. Mark the shift with a vertical line. If there is no line to mark, the scene has no turn.
3. Read the last three lines. If they could be deleted without loss, the scene ends too late.

## One exchange, diagnosed

The shape of a working exchange, as a calibration target: two agendas, one unspoken on each side; three failed attempts before the shift; the shift arriving as a concession the weaker speaker did not intend to make; the last line *being* the concession, with nothing after it.

If the exchange has four attempts, cut one — the third is usually the second restated. If it has one, the pressure was applied once, and once is not pressure.

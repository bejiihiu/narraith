# English Prose: Stress, Register, and the Inheritance

> Purpose: name the English-specific defaults that shape a sentence — stress-timed rhythm, the Latinate/Germanic axis, free indirect discourse — and the workshop inheritance that quietly governs them.
> Read when: you are writing in English, matching or auditing an English voice, or importing English pacing advice into another language.

## The inheritance

"Show, don't tell" is not a discovery about prose. It is an artefact of three institutions that converged in the twentieth century. Screenwriting needed it because a camera cannot photograph an interior state, so the script must externalise or lose the information. The workshop needed it because it is checkable: a teacher can point at a sentence and say *that is telling*, and the student can point at another and say *that is showing*, and the class can agree. And a strand of modernist criticism — Lubbock's dramatisation, the Jamesian novel — gave it a theory: the reader should be present at the scene, not briefed on it.

Each of the three is a legitimate reason for a local technique. None of them is a law of prose, and the rule fails in three specific ways.

**First, it manufactures somatic cliché.** Told that emotion must be shown, a writer who has nothing to show reaches for the body, and the body has a small repertoire: the tight chest, the cold sweat, the stomach dropping, the breath catching. The result is a text where every character feels the same thing in the same body. `[data: StoryScope]` This is measurable at population level: AI stories convey emotion through physical sensation and bodily metaphor in 81% of cases against 38% for human stories. The rule, applied as dogma, produces the signature the rule was supposed to prevent.

**Second, it refuses necessary summary.** Some information must be told. A character's employment history, the terms of a will, the fact that two years passed — these are summary, and summary done well is a craft, not a failure of nerve. A writer who dramatises everything produces scenes that exist to deliver a fact, which is the most expensive way to deliver a fact and the least interesting.

**Third, it confuses inference with quality.** Showing works when the reader's inference is the point — when the gap between the behaviour and the meaning is where the scene lives. It fails when the inference is trivial: *she poured the tea slowly* does not require the reader to deduce grief; it requires them to notice a slow pour and convert it, which is busywork if the scene has already established the grief. Naming is sometimes the stronger move because it is faster, and speed is an effect. See `references/prose-engineering.md` §9 for the channel-by-function calibration; the point here is only that the English inheritance supplies one channel and calls it virtue.

**When the rule does hold.** The rule is right under three conditions, and it is worth stating them because they are the conditions under which the technique earns its cost:

- **The behaviour is observable and specific.** Not *she was upset* converted into *she was upset and showed it*, but a particular act that only this person would perform in this room.
- **The inference is available but not automatic.** The reader must be able to get there, and must have to do one step. If the inference is instant, the detail was a label in costume; if the inference is unavailable, the detail is a private code.
- **The scene has time for it.** Showing costs words. In a paragraph that must cross a year, telling is the correct instrument, and the writer who dramatises the year has spent the scene budget on transport.

**What "this is telling" usually means.** When a workshop reader says a passage is telling, the useful translation is rarely "you named an emotion". It is usually one of three things: the information was not needed, the information was needed but arrived at a speed the reader did not choose, or the information arrived before the scene that would have made it mean something. All three are real faults and none of them is about the noun. Diagnose which one before reaching for a solution, because "dramatise it" is only a change of speed: it fixes the second and makes the first and third worse.

## Stress-timed rhythm

English is stress-timed. The reader's ear counts **stresses**, not syllables and not words, and the intervals between stresses tend toward equal duration — which is why a line of iambic pentameter and a line of four heavy monosyllables can occupy the same felt time.

The practical consequence is that word count is a bad proxy for sentence length. *The committee will consider the proposal at its next meeting* is ten words and seventeen syllables with five stresses spread thin across them; *The committee meets next week to decide* is seven words and ten syllables with the same five stresses packed tighter, so it arrives faster and lands harder. If you "vary sentence length" by counting words, you will produce sentences of different lengths that feel identical, because their stress patterns are identical.

**Mark the stresses.** Underline the content words, read the line aloud, and listen for the pattern. Three things go wrong:

- **The iambic thump.** Ten or more syllables of alternating weak-strong, sustained across two sentences, reads as verse or as a chant. Break it with a fronted element, a three-syllable word, or a full stop.
- **The stress pile-up.** Four or five stressed monosyllables in a row (*and then the door shut hard*) is a hammer; it works once, for impact, and reads as shouting when repeated.
- **The buried payload.** A sentence whose most important word sits in an unstressed position cannot land, no matter how good the word is. Move the payload to the final stress.

**Latinate and Germanic weight.** English has two word-hoards, and they behave differently under stress. Germanic words are usually short, concrete, and monosyllabic or trochaic — *buy, house, deep, start, help, dead*. Latinate words are longer, more abstract, and carry more unstressed syllables — *purchase, residence, profound, commence, assist, deceased*. The Latinate word takes more time, spreads its stress over a longer span, and raises the register. That makes it an instrument:

- *He commenced the acquisition of the property.* Five Latinate words, stresses spread thin, the act buried.
- *He bought the house.* Two stresses, both landing on the payload.

Neither is better in itself. The first is right for a narrator who is a lawyer, or for a comic gap between the language and the squalor of the transaction. The second is right when the act is the event. What is never right is a Latinate abstraction in the impact position of a scene that needs a fact.

**One fact, three stress distributions.** The material: the inspector has come, and the narrator's father is not going to answer the door.

| Version | Stresses | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| *My father did not move towards the door when the inspector rang.* | 5, evenly spread | Report. Nothing is emphasised, so nothing is at stake; the sentence could be a stage direction. |
| *When the inspector rang, my father did not move.* | 4, last on *move* | The refusal is the payload. The sentence now has a subject — the not-moving — and the paragraph knows what it is about. |
| *The inspector rang. My father did not move.* | 2 + 2, two landings | Two events, and the second is the answer to the first. The white space between them is the pressure. |

The third is not "better writing" in the abstract; it is the version that commits. It says the scene is about a man not answering a door, and it spends two sentences to say it. The first version says the same thing and commits to nothing, which is why it reads as machine output even though every word is correct.

**The read-aloud procedure.** Three passes, and do not combine them:

1. **Stresses only.** Read a paragraph marking each stressed syllable with a dot. The pattern is the paragraph's rhythm, and it is either doing something or repeating itself.
2. **Pauses only.** Read for where the sentence makes you breathe, and mark it. If the breath falls in the same place in three consecutive sentences, the syntax has a habit.
3. **Meaning only.** Read for what you understand at each full stop, and note where you had to go back. Backtracking is the only reliable measure of a sentence that failed.

## Register

Register in English is mostly a function of three dials: **word origin** (Germanic to Latinate), **formality** (contraction and elision), and **specificity** (general to technical). Turn them independently and you can place a voice without a single marker of accent.

**The Latinate/Germanic axis** is the strongest and least discussed. *Ask* and *request*, *end* and *conclude*, *show* and *demonstrate*, *need* and *require* — the pairs are near-synonyms with different social weights. A narrator who says *require* throughout is a narrator with an institution behind them. A narrator who says *need* is a person in a room. Shifting between them mid-paragraph is jarring unless the shift is the point.

**Formality and contraction.** Contractions are a period marker as much as a class marker. *Do not* in dialogue signals stiffness, seriousness, or an earlier era; *don't* signals ease. In narration, the convention of avoiding contractions belongs to a formal register that is now mostly archaic outside of certain genres — but archaic is sometimes exactly what you want. Decide the period first, then let the contractions follow.

The same line at three periods, and note that only the contractions changed:

- *"I cannot tell you what he said."* — a narrator who is being careful, or a period before ease was available in print.
- *"I can't tell you what he said."* — neutral contemporary.
- *"Can't tell you what he said."* — the subject dropped as well, which is a different register entirely: not period but intimacy or insolence, depending on who is listening.

Contraction is not a single dial. Dropping the subject is a second, and it does more work than the apostrophe.

**Class without caricature.** The reliable signals are not accent but **what a character is confident about**, **what they apologise for**, and **which register they switch into under pressure**. A character who becomes formal when frightened is characterised by the switch. A character who uses *quite* as a hedge, or *actually* as a soft contradiction, is placed by the habit rather than by a vowel.

**One request, three registers.** The same ask, at three settings:

- *We need to talk about the money.* — Germanic, contracted, direct. A person in a room.
- *I think we should have a conversation about the finances.* — Latinate, uncontracted, an institution's phrase for a personal problem. Not politer: it puts a table between them.
- *The finances require discussion.* Agentless. The speaker has disappeared into the verb, which is what a character does when they want the decision to belong to nobody.

The gradient is not politeness. It is **distance and accountability**, and a character's position on it can shift within a page, which is how a voice becomes a person rather than a setting.

**Register as a scene decision.** Register belongs to a scene's pressure, not to the book's taste. The same narrator can be Latinate in a courtroom and Germanic in a kitchen, and the shift is characterisation provided it answers the room. The fault is a register that rises because the plot has become serious: the prose turns formal when someone dies, which is the author reaching for gravity rather than the narrator responding to anything in front of them. Test it by asking what in the scene caused the change of diction; if the answer is "the importance of the moment", the register is decorative.

- **Do not** spell accent. *'E said 'e wasn't comin'* is a costume, and it makes the reader hear the author's ear rather than the character's mouth. Mark speech with syntax, idiom, and lexicon.
- **Do not** use the Mitford U/non-U vocabulary (*serviette/napkin*, *sofa/settee*, *lavatory/toilet*) as a live class marker. It was a 1955 parlour game and it is now a period signal: it places a text in the 1950s, not a character in a class.
- **Do** use the register switch. The moment a character's sentences get longer and their words more Latinate is the moment they have lost control, and the reader feels it without being told.

## Free indirect discourse

Free indirect discourse is the English novel's characteristic instrument: third person, past tense, no reporting clause, and the character's diction and judgment inside the narrator's grammar. Austen does it, Flaubert perfected it in French and the English novel imported it, and Woolf and Joyce made it the default interior mode of the century. It is the only technique that lets a narrator stay outside a character while the character's bias leaks into the narration — which is why it carries irony, and why it is the standard vehicle for unreliable narration in third person.

**Three signals, and you need at least one per passage:**

1. **A deictic shift.** *Here*, *now*, *tomorrow*, *this* — words that only make sense from inside the character's position at that moment. *She would be there by six.* The *would* is future-in-past, which is the narrator's grammar; *by six* is the character's clock.
2. **An evaluative word or idiom that belongs to the character.** *Of course he would be late.* The *of course* is not the narrator's analysis; it is her resignation, in her words.
3. **A syntactic feature the narratorial voice would not produce.** A question, an exclamation, a fragment, a tag question, a sentence that stops early. *Nine minutes late. Nine.*

**How to move in and out.** Enter on a deictic or an idiom; leave on a fact, a name, or a summary. The exit is the important half, because a passage that enters FID and never leaves becomes interior monologue by accident, and the reader loses the frame that made the irony possible.

*She would be there by six. The train, of course, would be late, and there would be no one to meet her, and she would have to carry the case up the hill in the rain. Mrs Ellery had never once met a train on time in thirty years.*

The first three sentences are hers — *of course*, the resigned *would*, the indignity of the case. The fourth is the narrator's: a proper name, a fact, a retrospect. The exit is marked by the name, which belongs to the narrator's world rather than to the platform. Without that fourth sentence, the paragraph would still work, but it would have no irony — only complaint.

**The commonest fault** is a passage that is *almost* FID: the diction is the character's but the judgment is the narrator's, so the reader cannot tell whether the irony is the character's self-deception or the author's contempt. The test: remove the evaluative word. If the passage becomes neutral report, the word was the signal and the passage was FID. If it becomes incoherent, two voices were fighting and one of them has to go.

**Three interior modes, one moment.** She has been waiting two hours and the person has not come.

| Mode | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Psychonarration | *She had been waiting two hours, and she was beginning to be afraid that he would not come.* | The narrator reports the state in the narrator's language. Clear, distant, no irony available. |
| Free indirect discourse | *Two hours. Well, of course he would not come now. He had never come when it mattered, and she had known that at nine o'clock, and she had stayed.* | Her diction, his grammar. The *of course* and the *well* are hers; the tense is the narrator's; the verdict on her own staying is available because the narrator is not commenting. |
| Direct thought | *Two hours. Go home. Go home now.* | Quoted or italicised, unmediated. Immediate, and it closes the ironic gap, because there is no narrator left to see her from outside. |

The middle version can do something neither of the others can: it lets the reader watch her be wrong about her own motives. That is the whole reason the technique exists, and it is why the exit sentence in the Mrs Ellery example matters so much — without a narrator to return to, there is no outside from which the self-deception is visible.

**FID in first person.** The technique is usually described as a third-person tool, but the same movement happens inside an *I*: the narrating self reports the experiencing self's diction without endorsing it. *I told him I would think about it. Of course I would think about it. I had been thinking about it for eleven years.* The *of course* belongs to the earlier self, and the last sentence is the later narrator's verdict on her. The distance between the two selves is the irony, and it is the same distance as between narrator and character in third person — which is why a first-person narrator with no gap between the two selves reads as flat, however eventful the story is.

## The comma splice and other "errors" as voice

A comma splice joins two independent clauses with a comma. Prescriptive grammar calls it an error; prose calls it a tool, and the tool has a specific use: it makes two clauses **one gesture** in a way no conjunction can.

*He put the letter in the drawer and shut it, he did not lock it.*

Replace the comma with *and* and the two acts become sequential. Replace it with *but* and they become contrastive. Replace it with *so* and the second becomes a consequence. None of those is the relation: the relation is that the not-locking is part of the same motion as the shutting, and only the splice says that.

**The test for a deliberate splice:** try each conjunction. If one of them fits, use it — the splice was an accident. If none fits and the relation is real, the splice is doing work. Then check consistency: does this narrator splice elsewhere? A voice that splices once in forty pages has made an event; a voice that splices every third sentence has a syntax, and the syntax must hold.

**Fragments.** *Because he was tired.* is a fragment that answers a question the previous sentence raised, and the answer is the emphasis. Three working types:

- **The answer.** *Why didn't you say anything? Because he already knew.* The fragment completes a question the paragraph raised.
- **The refusal.** *Not tonight.* The missing verb is the refusal's force.
- **The correction.** *He was late. Very late.* The fragment revises the previous sentence's degree, and the revision is the emphasis.

Used systematically, the fragment is a syntax; used randomly, it is a typo the author has decided to defend. The check is the same as for the splice: does the fragment complete a move, and does the narrator do it again later in the same way?

**The semicolon, and the overcorrection.** A writer told that splices are wrong often replaces them with semicolons, which produces a different fault: an essayistic rhythm in which every clause is weighed against the next. The semicolon is right when the two clauses are **parallel in structure or opposed in meaning**, and the mark says so: *He had come for the money; he stayed for the argument.* It is wrong when the relation is causal or sequential, because then it is a comma splice wearing a tie.

**The rule against correction.** A copy editor's job is consistency, not grammar. If a narrator's voice is built on splices, fragments, and comma-less lists, correcting them is not editing, it is dubbing. The question is never "is this standard" but "is this the same voice on page 40 as on page 4".

## Dialogue in English

**Said-bookisms.** *He opined. She ejaculated. He retorted.* The tag is doing work the line should do, and the reader feels the strain. The reason is mechanical: a tag is a stage direction, and stage directions are for information the scene cannot carry. If the line is a retort, the tag *retorted* adds nothing; if the line is not a retort, the tag is a lie about it.

**Action beats versus tags.** A beat replaces the tag and adds business: *"I'm not angry." She put the cup down without looking at where it went.* The beat does three things at once — identifies the speaker, gives the scene a physical event, and characterises the anger by the carelessness of the placement. That is a better trade than a tag, which does one thing. The failure is beat inflation: a beat after every line turns dialogue into stage management, and the reader stops hearing the voices because the camera keeps moving.

**"Said is invisible" examined.** The claim is that *said* is a neutral word the eye skips, so it should carry the load. It is half true. *Said* is the least marked verb of speech, so it claims least, so it disappears — that part holds. But invisibility is not the same as absence: a page of nothing but *said* is flat, and not because *said* is wrong but because the sentences around it are doing nothing. The tag is a rhythmic slot as well as an informational one; when every slot is filled with the same monosyllable, the paragraph has a metronome. The fix is not a thesaurus. It is a beat, a line that needs no attribution, or a period of unattributed exchange once the voices are established.

**Punctuation, US and UK.** The two conventions differ in a way that changes the look of a page:

| | US | UK |
|---|---|---|
| Primary quotes | Double: "He said, 'Go.'" | Single: 'He said, "Go."' |
| Punctuation placement | Commas and periods always inside the closing quote | Only punctuation belonging to the quoted matter goes inside |
| Example | "I'm leaving," she said. | 'I'm leaving', she said. |

Neither is more correct. Mixing them is the error, and mixing them within one manuscript is the kind of inconsistency a reader notices without knowing why. Decide by market and hold it.

**The em dash.** In dialogue it marks interruption — *I was going to—* — and in narration it marks an aside or a broken construction. Both are legitimate. The overuse is a specific pattern: the dash used for every pause, so that the text has one punctuation mark for hesitation, interruption, apposition, and reversal. That is not emphasis, it is a tic, and the diagnostic is mechanical: count the dashes on a page and ask, for each one, which of the four jobs it is doing. If more than two are doing the same job, the mark has stopped meaning anything.

**Adverb tags.** *"I'm not angry," she said angrily.* The adverb repeats the line or contradicts it. If it repeats, cut it. If it contradicts, move the contradiction into the beat — *She said it to the window* — where the reader can see it.

**A short exchange, annotated.** No tags after the second line, and nothing is lost:

*She found him at the sink, sleeves rolled, washing one cup.*

*"You didn't call."*

*He turned the tap off. "I did call."*

*"Your mother called. That's not the same thing."*

*He put the cup on the rack. It slipped; he caught it. "All right. I didn't call."*

Four things are doing the work. The opening beat establishes who is where, so the first line needs no attribution. *He turned the tap off* is both a beat and a delay — he buys time before answering, which is an admission in advance. The semicolon in *It slipped; he caught it* makes the slip and the catch one gesture, and the gesture is his composure failing and being recovered, which is what the scene is about. And *All right* is the capitulation; the beat before it has already told the reader it was coming, so the words can be quiet.

**Unattributed exchange.** Once two voices are established, three or four lines can run without tags, and the paragraph gains speed. The limit is the reader's tracking: the moment a line could plausibly belong to either speaker, the exchange needs a beat. The rule is not "fewer tags"; it is "the reader must never have to go back".

**Dialogue tags and period.** A tag can be a period marker without a single archaic word: *he returned*, *she observed*, *he remarked* belong to an earlier novel, and using them now is either pastiche or a character who talks like a book. The neutral contemporary set is small — *said*, *asked*, *told* — and the rest of the work is done by the line's content and the beat. This is why dialogue in a historical novel is so often the first thing to go wrong: the writer reaches for the tag to signal the period and forgets that the *syntax* of the line is doing that job already.

## American and British defaults

The differences that matter for prose are spelling, idiom, and the social geography a reader will assume.

**Spelling.** *color/colour*, *-ize/-ise*, *traveled/travelled*, *license/licence* (noun/verb in UK), *gray/grey*, *toward/towards*. Choose one variety for the whole manuscript. Mixed spelling inside one book reads as an unedited translation, and it is the single most common tell of a non-native or carelessly localised text.

| US | UK | Note |
|---|---|---|
| color, honor, favor | colour, honour, favour | the *-or/-our* class |
| traveled, canceled | travelled, cancelled | doubled *l* before a suffix |
| analyze | analyse | *-yze* is US; UK writes *-yse* |
| organize, realize | organise, realise | UK also permits *-ize* (Oxford style), so check house style |
| license (noun and verb) | licence (noun), license (verb) | UK splits by part of speech |
| defense, offense | defence, offence | *-se* in UK nouns |
| gray | grey | both current, one national |

**Idiom.** *gotten* (US) against *got*; *fall* against *autumn*; *elevator/lift*, *trunk/boot*, *apartment/flat*, *pissed* (US: angry; UK: drunk — a genuine hazard). Prepositions differ more than vocabulary: *on the weekend* (US) against *at the weekend* (UK); *write me* (US) against *write to me* (UK). Idiom is where a text reveals its origin, and no amount of spelling correction hides it.

**Class markers.** British class is encoded in vocabulary and in the register switch far more than in accent, and the encodings are not intuitive to an American ear: *tea* as a meal, *supper* against *dinner*, *pudding* as dessert, *school* meaning private school. American class is encoded more in consumption and in region. Getting this wrong produces a character who is socially impossible.

**The television Britain.** The characteristic failure of non-British writers is a Britain assembled from period drama and television comedy: everyone has a manor or a council flat, everyone says *quite* and *rather*, the working class is cockney, the upper class is clipped, and the weather is a character. The tell is not inaccuracy in any single detail but the absence of the ordinary — traffic, weather that is merely wet, the specific tedium of a provincial town, the fact that most British people live in neither a manor nor a tower block. If you cannot supply the ordinary, set the book somewhere you can.

**Period consistency.** Decide the decade before the first sentence. *Gotten* in a novel set in 1930s England is an anachronism; *wireless* in a novel set in 2010 is a joke. Contractions, slang, brand names, and forms of address all date, and a single anachronism breaks the frame more thoroughly than a plot error, because it tells the reader the author is guessing.

**Mid-Atlantic prose.** A manuscript with British spelling and American idiom, or with no locatable idiom at all, reads as translated even when it was written natively. This is sometimes a deliberate choice — an international narrator, a corporate setting, a voice that belongs to airports — and then it must be *chosen and consistent*, because the reader will otherwise assume the author could not decide. The diagnostic is a single question: where does this narrator buy groceries, and what do they call the bag? If the text cannot answer, it has no country, and that is a decision whether or not it was made.

## A revision checklist for English

Ten checks, in the order that catches the most text.

1. **Mark the stresses in the first sentence of each paragraph.** If three consecutive sentences have the same stress pattern, the rhythm is a metronome regardless of word count.
2. **Count the Latinate abstractions** (*-tion*, *-ment*, *-ity*, *-ance*) in any scene of action. Keep the ones that are the point; convert the rest to verbs.
3. **Find every *was* + adjective.** Replace one in three with a verb that does something. *She was tired* against *She sat down before she meant to*.
4. **Check the payload position.** For each sentence, is the most important word at or near the final stress? If not, move it or cut what follows.
5. **FID audit.** Any passage where the diction is the character's — is there an exit? Name the word that returns the narration to the narrator.
6. **Comma splices.** For each one, try *and*, *but*, *so*, *because*. If a conjunction fits, the splice was an accident.
7. **Dialogue tags.** Any verb other than *said* or *asked*: is it doing informational work? If not, cut it. Any adverb tag: cut it and give the beat the job.
8. **Attribution density.** If every line is tagged or beaten, establish two voices and let three or four lines run unattributed.
9. **Consistency.** Spelling variety, idiom, period, and punctuation convention: one of each, throughout.
10. **Read a page aloud.** If you breathe at the same point in three consecutive sentences, the syntax has a habit and the habit is not a voice.

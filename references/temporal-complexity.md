## Chronology is a decision

Chronological telling is not the neutral option. It is the option that costs the reader least and buys the least, and choosing it by default is how a story ends up with a shape nobody chose.

What reordering buys, exactly — four different goods, frequently confused:

- **Suspense.** A prolepsis gives the reader a fact the character lacks. The reader's question stops being *what happens* and becomes *when does she find out, and what will it cost her*. Suspense is produced by a gap in knowledge, not by danger.
- **Irony.** The reader holds two facts at once; the character holds one. Irony is a two-level structure and it dies the moment the character catches up. This is why ironic scenes must be short — the structure has a shelf life measured in pages.
- **Withheld causation.** Effect first, cause later. The reader is given a behaviour without a reason, and reasons are what readers actually read for. A stranger's odd act in chapter one is a stronger hook than a stranger's explained act.
- **Comparison.** Juxtaposing two moments makes an argument that neither moment makes alone: the same gesture in 1974 and in 2019, the same lie told to two people. This is the only one of the four that produces meaning rather than tension, and it is the one most drafts never use.

What it costs:

- **Orientation.** Every shift spends the reader's map. A lost reader is not intrigued; a lost reader is working. Keep the distinction sharp: withholding withholds *answers*, confusion withholds *positions*. A reader who does not know where they are cannot be suspenseful, only patient.
- **Accumulation.** Feeling accumulates in duration, not in information. A structure that keeps jumping never stays anywhere long enough for the reader's body to catch up, which is why clever nonlinear books so often read as intelligent and cold. If you want the reader to grieve, you have to leave them in the room.
- **The revelation budget.** Each revelation can be spent once. Telling the reader early does not add a fact; it removes a later scene's currency. Ask what the early telling *replaces*, not what it adds.
- **Reconstruction competes with presence.** A reader assembling a timeline is not inside a fight, a kiss, or a diagnosis. Put the difficult chronology where the scene can survive being read at a distance, and keep the linear stretch for the moments that need a body in the room.

The decision rule is the shape of the question, not a taste for complexity. *What happens next* runs chronological. *How did this happen* wants a withheld cause. *What did it mean* wants juxtaposition. *Will she survive what I already know is coming* wants a prolepsis. Reordering redistributes a pressure that already exists; it cannot manufacture one. A story with a weak question becomes a weak story told backwards.

Two cases where linear is not merely acceptable but correct, so that the rule above does not harden into an allergy. **When the pleasure is accumulation** — a domestic novel, a thriller on a clock, a story whose whole effect is the reader's growing familiarity with a place — jumping costs exactly the thing being built, and a flashback is a withdrawal from an account you were still paying into. **When the pressure is external and continuous** — a pursuit, a flood, an interrogation — the reader's orientation is part of the tension, and a shift of period releases it. In both cases the linear telling is a decision with a reason, which is all this file asks of any order.

## The four temporal operations

| Operation | Definition | What it buys | Use when | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Order** | The relation between story sequence and the sequence of telling | Suspense, irony, withheld causation, comparison | The question is *how* or *what it meant*, not *what next* | Movement that reads as restlessness; the reader loses position without gaining a question |
| **Duration** | The ratio of story time to text time for a given passage | Emphasis; the reader infers importance from space | The rate no longer matches decision density — a choice is rushed or a corridor is dwelt in | Uniform rate, so that nothing is emphasised and the reader has no signal |
| **Frequency** | How many times an event is told, and at what level of generality | Compression, characterisation, revelation through re-telling | A life needs a norm to break, or a fact needs re-hearing with new knowledge | Repetition that adds words and not knowledge |
| **Access** | What the narration is permitted to know at each point — whose mind, which facts, how far | Intimacy, mystery, the reader's sense of a limited world | The scene needs a limit on knowledge that the reader can feel as a limit | Knowledge the teller could not have; withholding the teller has no reason to withhold |

Order, duration, and frequency come from Genette and are explained there (`references/narrative-theory.md` §2). **Access** is the fourth operation and the one most craft books fold into "point of view", which loses the point: access is not a settings choice made once at the start, it is a variable that changes scene by scene, and it constrains what the other three are allowed to contain.

Access has two layers that must be kept apart. **Entitlement** is what the narrator could plausibly know: a first-person narrator who was not in the room cannot report the room, and an omniscient narrator can, but then must also report the cost of knowing. **Selection** is what the narrator chooses to pass on. Violating entitlement is an error. Exercising selection is a decision, and a decision that has to be justified — usually by the narrator's character, which is E15's territory.

The four operations interact, and the interaction is where most of the craft is. A flashback at scene speed with restricted access is a memory: vivid, partial, and suspect. The same flashback at summary speed with omniscient access is a case history: authoritative, cool, and hard to doubt. Same material, opposite epistemologies. Choose the speed and the access *together* or you will get a memory that reads like a file.

The same event, four ways — note that the *facts* do not change:

**Scene, restricted.** The kitchen smelled of coins. Her mother put the jar on the table between them and said, "Count them," and Mira counted, and her mother watched her hands.

**Scene, omniscient.** The kitchen smelled of coins. Her mother put the jar on the table and watched Mira count, thinking: *she is slow with numbers, she gets that from him, and he will not be here to be blamed for it.*

**Summary, restricted.** The year her father left, her mother made her count the coins in the jam jar every Sunday, and Mira never learned what the counting was for.

**Summary, omniscient.** The year her father left, her mother began making the child count the coins in the jam jar each Sunday — a ritual with no purpose she could have stated, and one she could not stop.

The first is a scene the reader is inside. The second is a scene the reader is above, and it costs the mother her opacity. The third is a fact with a hole in it, which the reader will want filled. The fourth is a diagnosis, and diagnoses are for the end of a story, not the middle. All four are legitimate; only one of them is the right instrument for the scene you are writing, and the choice is not about which is better written.

## Analepsis and prolepsis

A flashback is not a container for information. It is an argument about the present, and like any argument it needs a reason to be made now, by this narrator, at this point.

**Rule one: it must change the meaning of the present scene.** The test is brutal and reliable — delete the anachrony and read the present scene again. If it means the same thing, the past material was information. Information belongs wherever the plot needs it, ideally in a sentence of summary, not in a scene you paid for.

**Rule two: it must be entered on a hook in the present.** Not "She remembered the summer of 1998." The hook is a physical thing in the room that the past explains: a jar of labelled screws, a smell of coins, a form with a box ticked in someone else's hand. The hook does two jobs at once — it makes the transition feel caused rather than administered, and it guarantees the past is arriving to answer a question the present has just raised. Enter the past at the latest possible moment; skip its setup. The reader will reconstruct more than you think, and will trust the reconstruction more than your exposition.

**Rule three: exit on the line that changes the present.** Not on the last thing that happened in the past. The final beat of an analeptic block should be the one that retroactively re-colours the scene the reader was in — the sentence that makes an earlier gesture mean something else.

Reach and extent are separate decisions (narrative-theory §2). A flashback can reach thirty years and stay for three lines; it can reach a week and stay for ten pages. Deep reach with wide extent is a second story; shallow reach with narrow extent is a grace note. Drafts usually err toward wide extent — the past gets a whole scene because it is interesting, and the present waits.

**Three structures, three different arguments.**

*The withheld cause.* The present shows a behaviour that looks like a flaw, and the past supplies the reason. The argument is: *you judged her too quickly.* The reader's judgement of the present is corrected after the fact, which is a stronger effect than being told the reason first, because the reader has to give something up.

- Failure: the cause is supplied too completely. Once the behaviour is fully explained it stops being a scene and becomes a diagnosis. Leave the reason adjacent to the behaviour, not identical with it — the reader should be able to say *that is why* without being able to say *and therefore she is fine*.

*The repeated wound.* The same past event returns at three points, each time with more of it visible, and each return changes what the character can do in the present. This is the only structure that uses frequency as an argument: the event is not one fact but a series of discoveries about one fact.

- Failure: the returns add detail but not consequence. If the third telling leaves the character with the same options as the second, it is a longer version, not a deeper one. Each pass must cost something or unlock something.

*The false memory.* A past presented as reliable, later contradicted by another version — a document, a sibling, a photograph with someone cropped out. The argument is: *the version you were given was a kindness, and kindnesses are expensive.*

- Failure: no cost to the correction. If the false version cost the reader nothing, the correction is a trick played on them rather than a discovery made by them. The false version must have been doing work — explaining a choice, excusing a person, holding a family together — so that losing it costs the present something.

**Prolepsis works by the same logic in reverse.** It spends surprise to buy dread, and the exchange rate matters. A good prolepsis tells the reader *that* something happens and withholds *how it felt*: the shape without the texture. *By the following winter the shop was empty* is usable. *By the following winter the shop was empty and she had stopped speaking to her brother, which she regretted* has spent the scene it interrupts.

One more use, often better than either: the prolepsis that narrates the *aftermath* of the story's present, in the voice of a narrator who has survived it. That is a frame device, and it converts the whole middle of the book into a question about how the narrator got from here to there. It is powerful and it is easily overused — a narrator who keeps announcing outcomes teaches the reader to wait rather than to read.

## Duration

| Technique | Time ratio | Reader effect | Typical misuse |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scene** | Story time ≈ text time | Presence. The reader is in the room and cannot skim without losing something | Scene inflation: whole encounters that change nothing, because "scene" was mistaken for "good" |
| **Summary** | Story time ≫ text time | Range and information; the reader learns what a season was like in a paragraph | Summary where a decision was required; the draft reports the story instead of playing it |
| **Stretch** | Text time > story time | Attention narrows; subjective time dilates | Applied to ordinary action, where it reads as the author stalling |
| **Ellipsis** | Story time passes, text skips it | A gap that the reader must fill, and a question about what was in it | A gap that is never asked about, which reads as a continuity error rather than a choice |
| **Pause** | Story time stops, text continues | Reflection, description, reframing; knowledge can rise while tension falls | Three sentences of weather before anything happens, because the narrator has nothing to decide |

The full decision account of these five is in narrative-theory §2. What matters here is the distribution, because that is the part drafts get wrong.

**The dominant-speed rule.** Most drafts run at one speed for almost their whole length, with the other speeds used only as transport. `[hypothesis]` Either the manuscript is nearly all scene, with summary appearing as *Later that week* between chapters, or it is nearly all summary, with scenes reserved for the moments the writer has decided are important. Both feel wrong, and writers usually misdiagnose the cause: the all-scene draft gets called "slow", the all-summary draft gets called "distant", and the writer responds by adding the missing technique everywhere. That makes it worse.

Both feel wrong for the same reason: **a uniform rate carries no signal.** Pace is how the text tells the reader what to attend to. If everything runs at scene speed, the funeral and the parking meter get equal weight, and the reader's attention flattens to match. If everything runs at summary speed, nothing is ever inhabited, and the reader receives a very well-organised account of a story rather than a story.

The repair is not "add summary" or "add scenes". It is to make rate track **decision density**: slow down where a character is choosing under pressure and the choice is expensive; speed up where consequences are being transported; use pause where the reader needs to hold an object or a fact long enough for it to acquire weight; use ellipsis where the period contains no decision, or where the gap itself is the point.

Two corollaries worth keeping in front of you while drafting.

*Space is an argument about importance.* The reader infers what matters from how long you spend on it, and they infer it faster and more reliably than they infer it from your theme. If the funeral gets a clause and the parking dispute gets a page, you have told the reader what the story is about, whatever the narrator says.

*An ellipsis is a question.* Every skip asks *what happened in there?* If the story never answers, or never even acknowledges the asking, the reader concludes the writer lost track. The exception is the deliberate gap that the whole structure is built to circle — the missing year, the unmentioned child — and that gap must be visibly a gap, marked by the other characters' avoidance.

**Dialogue pins you at scene speed.** This is the mechanical reason talky drafts feel flat, and it is worth understanding rather than correcting by feel. Speech cannot be summarised without losing its texture and cannot be stretched without becoming absurd, so every exchange runs at something close to real time. A manuscript that is mostly dialogue has therefore fixed most of its rate at one setting, no matter how the writer varies the rest. The fix is not to cut dialogue; it is to put the summary *inside* the conversation — the paragraph that covers the four hours between the accusation and the apology, the pause that is one line long and holds a season. A dialogue scene with no interior duration is a transcript, and a transcript has only one speed.

## Frequency

Three modes, and one of them is a structural instrument most drafts never touch.

**Singulative** — tell once what happened once. The default, and the reason a draft can feel like a list: everything has equal claim on the reader's attention because everything is delivered in the same mode.

**Iterative** — tell once what happened many times. *On Sundays we walked to the reservoir and my father counted the boats.* This is compression, but its real work is characterisation: a habitual tense builds a self out of routine, and the routine is a claim about who someone is. It also quietly establishes a norm, which is what makes the break out of it legible. Switch from iterative to singulative and the reader knows the routine has failed before anyone says so — the cheapest turn signal in prose, and it costs one verb.

- Failure: the whole story in the iterative. If everything always happens, no day is the day, and nothing can occur, because occurrence requires a background of repetition to occur against.

**Repeating** — tell many times what happened once. Legitimate when the second telling knows something the first did not: a different witness, a detail that has become load-bearing, a narrator who has since learned what the gesture meant. Then repetition is the mechanism of revelation — same event, changed knowledge.

- Failure: repetition that adds words rather than knowledge. A scene retold at greater length with the same understanding is a draft the writer has not finished thinking about, and the reader can feel the difference between a return and a re-run.

The mode is also a moral instrument. What a narrator chooses to describe as typical, and what as a single event, is a judgement about which parts of a life were real. *He was always leaving* and *he left on a Tuesday* are the same fact under two frequencies, and they accuse different people.

## Multiple timelines

Three timelines is the working limit without explicit apparatus — a document, a second narrator, chapter headings that do the bookkeeping `[hypothesis]`. A fourth does not fail because readers are stupid; it fails because each additional line raises the re-entry cost until the reader is spending more attention on orientation than on the story.

**The anchoring rule.** Each timeline needs a distinct sensory world and a distinct tense or pronoun signature. Not a date stamp.

The reason is that a date stamp is metadata and a sensory world is orientation. *1974* tells the reader a number and gives them nothing to see; a fridge running on a gas bottle, a party line shared with two neighbours, and a phone that has to be booked through an operator tells them a decade, a class, and a set of constraints, all at once, in things the scene needed anyway.

The second signature is cheaper and wears out faster. Tense contrast (past against present) is legible for about two pages and then becomes wallpaper; pronoun contrast (first against third) survives longer but imports a whole set of assumptions about whose story this is. Use them, but do not rely on them alone. What does not wear out is a **world** the reader can smell, and a **body** in a particular condition: the knee that works in one line and does not in the other does orientation, chronology, and cost in a single detail.

**The re-entry rule.** Every cut back costs orientation, so short alternations need strong signatures and long blocks can coast on momentum. A structure that alternates every scene for three hundred pages is asking the reader to re-anchor dozens of times; either give each line longer blocks to live in, or accept that the reader will start skimming the line they find less legible, and design around that rather than pretending it will not happen.

**The convergence rule.** Timelines must eventually constrain each other. If A and B only take turns, one of them is a subplot with a better title. The test is causal: change something in A — a decision, a death, a letter sent — and ask whether B's ending is affected. If it is not, the lines are parallel in the geometrical sense, which is to say they never meet.

The reader also needs to know the *relation* between the lines even when they do not know the content: the same person at two ages, two people who will meet, two versions of one night, a cause and its consequence. An unmarked relation leaves the reader holding two stories with no reason to compare them, and comparison is the only thing multiple timelines are for.

**The hard case: one narrator, one tense, two periods.** When the same voice tells both lines in the same tense, neither signature is available and the anchors have to do all the work. Three options, in order of reliability. Give each period its own *vocabulary of constraint* — what the narrator cannot do, cannot buy, cannot say aloud — because constraint is more distinctive than decor. Give each period a different relation to a recurring person: the sister who is a child in one line and the one making the decisions in the other, and let her grammar shift. Or accept an explicit marker and spend it once per section, at the top, in a phrase the narrator would actually use ("the year of the flood", "before the shop"), so that the label is characterisation rather than bookkeeping.

## Structure patterns that work

These are not templates. Each is a way of putting a question into the shape of a telling, and each fails in its own way. Take the question you actually have and find the pattern that serves it; do not take the pattern and look for a question to fill it.

**Braided.** Two or more lines alternate, each with its own question, and they intersect at points that change both. It serves the question *do these two lives make each other possible, or only bearable?*

> A locksmith who has been changing the locks of a woman's house for eleven years without ever meeting her; a woman who has been leaving the house each time he comes. The braid alternates his rounds and her departures until the day she stays. The intersection is not a meeting, it is the loss of the arrangement that kept them apart.

Failure: alternation without constraint — the reader learns to skip whichever line is less interesting, and the braid becomes two stories printed together.

**Frame.** An outer narrator whose situation is changed by the inner story, so that the telling is an act with a cost. It serves *why is this being told now, and what does the telling cost the teller?*

> A stenographer taking down a statement for a fee, in a room with a clock she is being paid by. The statement is the inner story; the outer story is what the fee is doing to her attention. By the end she has decided to charge for a different number of hours than she worked.

Failure: the frame that exists only to justify the telling — the found manuscript, the letter in an attic, the grandchild who asks what happened. If the outer situation does not change, the frame is a preface with delusions.

**Reverse.** Consequences before causes, so that the reader's knowledge runs ahead of their understanding. It serves *how did this become inevitable?*

> Opens with a man being released; then the hearing that put him away; then the night that led to the hearing. Each earlier block makes the release more damning. The last block is the one that was never mentioned, and it reframes the release as a purchase.

Failure: no discovery. If the reader can predict each earlier block from the later one, reverse structure collapses into waiting, and waiting is the least interesting thing a reader can do.

**Mosaic.** Fragments ordered by association rather than chronology; meaning is made by adjacency. It serves *what does this life add up to when it is not arranged as a life?*

> Thirty-one short blocks: a recipe, a hospital form, a list of things sold, a phone message. They are ordered so that each block comments on the one before it without either block knowing. The chronology is reconstructable but never stated, and the reconstruction is the reader's work.

Failure: no reconstructable chronology at all. Juxtaposition needs a background order to cut against; if the reader cannot build any sequence, the arrangement is arbitrary and the associations are the writer's private ones.

**Spiral.** Returns to the same scene repeatedly, each pass with more knowledge, until the scene means something different. It serves *what did this moment actually mean, and when did that change?*

> A dinner party told four times: first as social comedy, then as a negotiation, then as the night a decision was made, then as the last time everyone was in one room. The events do not change; the frame around them narrows. By the fourth pass the reader knows what the hostess is doing with her hands, and the comedy has become a countdown.

Failure: passes that repeat instead of deepening — same information, more sentences. A spiral that does not tighten is a circle, and a circle is a scene the writer cannot leave.

**Dual-track convergence.** Two lines move toward a meeting the reader anticipates and at least one party does not. It serves *what does each of them do in the hour before they meet?*

> A courier driving north with a box he is not allowed to open; a woman in the north who has been expecting a delivery for six years and has stopped believing in it. The reader knows they are on the same road forty pages before either does. The meeting is not the climax; the climax is what each of them does in the hour before it.

Failure: the convergence substitutes for a plot. If the meeting is the only event, the book is a travel schedule with suspense about the timetable.

**In medias res with delayed exposition.** Begin at maximum pressure and backfill only what the pressure requires, when it requires it. It serves *what pressure produced this moment?*

> A woman on the floor of a stockroom, counting boxes she has already counted, while someone calls her name from the front of the shop. The name is not hers. What the reader needs — why she is hiding, whose name that is — arrives in the next four pages, each piece triggered by a sound from the front.

Failure: the delayed exposition becomes a prologue in instalments, or the opening is confusing rather than pressuring. The distinction is whether the reader has a question or merely a deficit. A question has a shape; a deficit is just missing information, and readers do not enjoy being told they are missing something.

## Orientation without hand-holding

The reader needs to know *when* they are, and the honest way to tell them is with things that exist in the time rather than with labels pasted onto it.

Anchors that work:

- **Technology and its cost.** Not "a phone" but a phone that has to be booked through an operator; not "a car" but a car that needs the choke out for a minute in winter. Technology dates a scene and prices it in the same gesture.
- **Weather as season, not mood.** Rain that has been going on long enough for the lane to be a channel; a heat that has made the tar soft. Weather orients when it has consequences and fails when it is atmosphere.
- **A body's condition.** A pregnancy, a knee that has not healed, a mother's hands in two different decades. The best anchor available, because it carries time, cost, and character in one detail.
- **Money and administrative detail.** What a note buys; the name of the form; the stamp that is required and the one that is not.
- **A recurring object, changed.** The jar of labelled screws, full in one line and holding three screws and a bent nail in the other. This is the strongest anchor of all, because it does orientation and meaning simultaneously, and it costs the story nothing it did not already own.

**Two anchors per shift** `[hypothesis]` — one sensory, one social or causal. One anchor can be read as scenery; three become a caption. And both must be load-bearing: the anchor should be something the scene needed anyway, not a signpost erected for the reader's benefit. A date stamp is the signpost case — accurate, inert, and it tells the reader where they are without giving them anything to feel. Use one at the top of a section if the reader genuinely needs the number; never use one to do the work a world should do.

## Foreshadowing and planting

Three different objects, routinely confused, with different rules.

**Foreshadowing is a promise.** The text tells the reader something is coming, and the promise creates anticipation. *She would remember the smell of coins for the rest of her life* is a promise. Promises are cheap to make and expensive to break: an unkept promise is not a subtlety, it is a small betrayal that teaches the reader to discount the narrator.

**Planting is a fact that becomes load-bearing.** No promise is made. The jar of screws is planted; nothing in the scene says it will matter. Later it does, and the reader's pleasure is the pleasure of having been in the presence of the answer without knowing it was a question.

**Cheating is information withheld from the reader that the POV character has.** Not information the character lacks — that is ordinary restriction, and it is the engine of most plots. Cheating is the narration stepping around something the teller knows, in order to produce a surprise at the end.

**The fairness rule.** At the moment of revelation, every element needed for the inference must already be in the text. The reader need not have noticed, and usually will not have. Fairness is about availability, not salience. A reveal that requires a fact the text never supplied is not a twist; it is a correction of the reader's understanding by an authority that has been lying by omission, and the reader's response is not admiration but a quiet withdrawal of trust.

Cheating has three licensed exceptions. **(a)** The narrator is deliberately unreliable and the suppression is characterised — the reader can see that this teller has reasons to skip (E15). **(b)** The POV is a child, an animal, or a mind impaired in a way the text has established, so the gap is the point; but the reader must be able to see *that* a gap exists, or they will read the gap as the author's. **(c)** The information is genuinely not in the character's mind at that moment — they have forgotten, and forgetting has been established as a trait rather than used once as a convenience.

`[data: StoryScope]` Across 61,608 stories, human fiction shows more nonlinear framing for delayed disclosure than model fiction — 1.96 against 1.68 on a 1–5 scale — and more chronological discontinuity, 2.40 against 2.12. The gaps are small and the study's prompts suppress exactly these structures (a ≤120-word premise cannot ask for a second timeline), so read the direction, not the size.

The correction is not "be nonlinear". It is **choose the order that maximises the question**. A linear telling maximises *what happens next*, which is a good question and the one most stories actually have. A withheld cause maximises *how did this happen*. A prolepsis maximises *will she survive what is coming*. These are different stories, and the order is part of how the story is stated, not a decoration applied to it. If the question is weak, no arrangement will rescue it — reordering only changes where the pressure is felt, never how much of it there is.

## Unreliable narration and temporal structure

A narrator who lies does most of the lying with time, not with facts. Facts are checkable, and a careful liar knows it. Order, duration, frequency, and access are where the work gets done, because they look like technique rather than testimony.

- **Order.** The exonerating version comes first; the contradiction is postponed to a point where it can be read as an unfortunate detail. The narrator controls what the reader has already accepted when the new fact arrives, and acceptance is a function of sequence.
- **Duration.** Three pages on a receipt; half a sentence on a death. The disproportion is the confession. A narrator who dwells on the trivial and skips the decisive is telling you where the pressure is while claiming to tell you what happened.
- **Frequency.** The alibi is told four times, in slightly different words each time; the wound is told once. Or the reverse: a single exception is buried inside an iterative *we always*, so that a habit is claimed where an event occurred.
- **Access.** Claims not to have known; reports other minds with a confidence the rest of the narration does not support; describes a room in a detail that only someone who was there could have.

**The reader detects unreliability through disproportion before they detect it through contradiction.** This is the practical consequence, and it cuts both ways. If you are writing an unreliable narrator, the signal must be in the emphasis, because emphasis is what a reader can feel without evidence. If you are writing a reliable one, watch your own proportions — a scene that spends its length on the wrong thing will read as evasive whether or not anyone is lying.

The second signal is a **slip in access**: a first-person narrator who reports a conversation they were not present at, or knows a fact before they could have. Done once, it is an error. Done at the exact point of pressure, it is a symptom, and the reader will remember it.

Unreliability requires a recoverable second version. A text where nothing can be reconstructed is not unreliable narration, it is confusion with a defence. The reader must be able to assemble, by the end, a version of events that the narration was avoiding — not necessarily the true one, but one that is more coherent than the telling.

## Temporal diagnostics

Eight questions, in order. Stop at the first one that has no answer.

1. **State the question the order serves, in one sentence.** If the answer is "it's just how it happened", the order is a default and the story is paying for it without receiving anything.
2. **Delete each anachrony and re-read the scene it interrupts.** What is lost? If the scene means the same thing, the block is information, not structure — move it into summary or cut it.
3. **After every shift, name the two anchors.** One sensory, one social. If you cannot name them, the reader cannot either, and the shift is costing orientation for nothing.
4. **What is your scene-to-summary ratio, and does the rate track decision density?** Find the passage where the rate is flattest. That is where the reader's attention is flattest, whatever the content.
5. **Which passage runs in the iterative, and where does it break into the singulative?** If there is no break, the routine never fails, and the story has no day.
6. **If there are multiple timelines, what does line A make impossible for line B?** If nothing, they are not one story.
7. **List every withheld fact and who knows it at that moment.** Any fact the POV character knows and the text hides needs a reason that lives in the narrator's character, not in the plot's convenience.
8. **At the reveal, was everything needed already in the text?** Not salient — available. If not, the surprise is a correction, and corrections cost trust.

# Quality Levels: The Seven-Level Grid

> Purpose: the shared vocabulary for diagnosing prose at the right altitude, so a structural problem is not line-edited and a line problem is not rewritten as structure.
> Read when: before any critique, revision plan, or quality gate; when two readers disagree about whether a draft is "good"; when a fix at one level has already failed twice.

## How to use the grid

The seven levels are **diagnostic axes ordered by scope**, not a hierarchy of prestige. L1 is not "basic" and L7 is not "advanced"; a book can be lexical genius and narrative void. The numbering says only how much text a decision governs: a word, a sentence, a voice, a meaning, a plot, a person, a whole.

Two rules govern every use:

- **Assign the defect to its owner.** A sentence that feels muddy may be a syntax problem or an information problem wearing syntax. Fixing the sentence without finding the owner produces a cleaner version of the same failure.
- **Never fix downstream while upstream is open.** If the scene has no turn (L5), rewriting its adjectives (L1) is work done twice. The order in the final section exists to prevent exactly this.

Owner engines: L1–L2 → E10; L3 → E10 and E12; L4 → E4 and E14; L5 → E2, E6, E7, E8; L6 → E3, E4, E5; L7 → E1, E12, E14.

**Durability under rewriting.** Not every level survives an edit equally. `[hypothesis]` This ordering maps the
fingerprinting study's signal ladder onto the grid, and it matters when you decide what to fix: a defect at a
durable level is worth more than a defect at a fragile one.

| Level | What the evidence covers | Durability | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | lexical n-grams | high in-language, collapses across languages | word-level fixes are real but do not travel |
| L2 | part-of-speech and syntax statistics | low | rhythm breaks first under paraphrase |
| L3 | stylometry, function words | low, especially across languages | voice judgments need a long sample |
| L4 | semantic representation | highest across languages | meaning-level repairs survive translation |
| L5 | narrative features | high, survives span-level rewriting | structural repairs are the best investment |
| L6 | character logic, inferred from semantic content | high but untested directly | judge from behaviour, not from stated motive |
| L7 | whole-text structure | high, untested directly | do not diagnose from an excerpt |

Cite a level in a critique as `[L5]`, and cite the level only when you can quote the evidence for it. "This feels like an L5 problem" is a hypothesis; "the climax is caused by a storm the first act never planted — L5/E2" is a diagnosis. A level's score describes the passage in front of you, not the writer's ability and not the book's potential.

## L1 — Lexical

### What this level governs

Word choice: whether the noun names the thing, whether the verb carries the action, whether the register belongs to this narrator and this world, and whether a word is doing two jobs because the writer has not decided which one is meant. It is the smallest unit of decision and the easiest place to mistake vagueness for lyricism.

### Observable signals

- **Concrete-to-abstract ratio.** In a 100-word scene sample, count nouns that denote photographable things against nouns that denote concepts. Scene prose below roughly 1:1 is abstraction-heavy `[hypothesis]`.
- **Filler inventory.** Count *thing, stuff, something, somehow, a sense of, a feeling of, seemed to, managed to* per 500 words. Each one is a place a decision was deferred.
- **Modifier load.** Nouns with two or more adjectives; adverbs propping up verbs of speech and motion. Count them, do not judge by feel.
- **Register breaks.** A Latinate abstraction in a colloquial narrator, or slang in a formal one, without a motive visible in the scene.
- **Verb strength.** Sentences whose main verb is *be, have, make, do, get* rather than a specific action or state.
- **Period and world accuracy.** Whether an object's name belongs to its decade, class, and trade — a "smartphone" in 1910 is an L1 error even if the sentence is beautiful.

### Levers

- Replace the abstraction with the object that caused it: not "a sense of loss" but the empty hook by the door.
- Choose the verb that makes the adverb unnecessary; if no verb does, the action is not yet clear.
- Cut the adjective that repeats what the noun already entails ("cold snow", "small kitten").
- Enforce a local lexicon: five words each character or world owns, and a short list they never use.
- Delete the key noun and read the sentence. If the meaning survives, the noun was filler.

### Failure signatures

- **Evaluative adjective instead of an object:** "a beautiful fog" — beautiful to whom, and what does it look like?
- **Abstract emotion doubled:** "she felt a sense of deep unease" — *sense of* plus *deep* plus *unease*, three layers of distance from anything.
- **Intensifier instead of behaviour:** "he was extremely angry" — the reader learns the label, not the event.
- **Adjective stack as decision substitute:** "the ornate, dilapidated, ancient house" — three adjectives, no chosen detail.
- **Register break:** "the family utilised the kitchen" — the narrator is not the kind of person who says *utilised*.

### Diagnostic questions

- Which nouns in this paragraph could be photographed?
- Which words would this narrator actually know, and which are the author's?
- Where is the sentence's energy — in the noun or the verb?
- Which word is doing two jobs because two different ideas are being avoided?

### Interface with other levels

L2 rhythm **masks** L1 vagueness: a well-shaped sentence makes an imprecise word sound chosen. L1 can **mask** L4: a page of precise objects can hide the fact that no idea is being tested. L1 is frequently a **symptom** of L5 or E6 — adjective fog appears where the information has not been decided — and of L6, when a character has no specific relation to language. Do not fix the diction until you know which of these is true.

### Genre and language variance

Genre diction is a feature, not a defect: hard SF needs technical nouns, fantasy needs invented ones, noir needs flat ones. Dialect and translated prose have their own norms; do not score them against a neutral literary English that does not exist. Russian carries information English does not: the choice between imperfective and perfective **aspect** changes what is being reported, not just how it sounds; *сказ* (skaz) makes an oral narrator's lexicon the whole voice technology; Church Slavonicisms and Soviet-era clichés carry period and ideological weight. See `references/russian-prose.md` before applying any English lexical anchor to Russian.

## L2 — Syntactic

### What this level governs

Sentence length, rhythm, clause order, subordination, and stress. Syntax decides what the reader receives first, what is held back, and which word lands last. It is the level most often mistaken for voice: a distinctive rhythm can make a page feel written when nothing in it has been decided.

### Observable signals

- **Length sequence.** Record sentence lengths across 20 consecutive sentences. If most fall within ±4 words of the mean, the prose is metrical in the wrong sense `[hypothesis]`.
- **Main-clause delay.** Count sentences where subject and verb arrive after eight or more words of preamble `[hypothesis]`.
- **Sentence openings.** Repeated pronouns, conjunctions, or participial phrases at the start of consecutive sentences.
- **End-stress.** Whether the sentence's last content word is its most important, or merely its last.
- **Paragraph shape.** Whether the final sentence is the paragraph's strongest or its most abstract.
- **Punctuation habit.** Dashes, semicolons, and colons used as rhythm (fine) or as hesitation between two undecided clauses (not fine).

### Levers

- Put new information in the stress position; move old information left.
- Split a sentence that carries two decisions; merge two that carry one.
- Vary length by function: short for a turn, long for immersion, medium for delivery.
- Read aloud and mark the breaths; cut one clause per breath that runs out.
- Convert a subordinate clause into its own sentence when it contains a decision.

### Failure signatures

- **Participle stack:** "Walking home, thinking of her mother, feeling the cold, she remembered the letter." Four actions, one weak main clause.
- **Uniform length:** every sentence 18–22 words, so no sentence can land.
- **Empty frame:** "It was then that she realized that…" — the syntax announces a revelation the content has not earned.
- **Weak ending:** a paragraph that closes on "and she went inside", the least important fact in it.
- **Syntax promising a turn:** a long periodic sentence that resolves into the same information it started with.

### Diagnostic questions

- Read the first ten words of ten consecutive sentences. What is the pattern?
- Where is the new information in this sentence, and is it where the stress falls?
- Can you delete the first clause without losing anything?
- Does the sentence end on its strongest word?

### Interface with other levels

L2 **masks** L1: rhythm makes vague words sound intentional. L2 is often a **symptom** of L5/E6 — the sentence is tangled because the writer has not decided what the reader should learn — and of L3 when the narrator's attitude is unstable. A syntactic fix applied to an information problem produces a smoother sentence saying the same nothing. L2 can also **mask** L4 by making an empty idea sound resolved.

### Genre and language variance

Action prose earns short sentences; lyrical prose earns long ones; the standard is fit, not variety for its own sake. Russian word order is information structure (theme–rheme), not decoration: moving a word changes what is presupposed. The long period with participial chains is normative in nineteenth-century prose and a liability in most contemporary Russian; do not "correct" it without knowing which period the text is written in. See `references/russian-prose.md`.

## L3 — Stylistic

### What this level governs

Voice, tone, figurative density, and the consistency of the narration's attitude. Style is not ornament added to a decision; it is the audible trace of who is telling, from where, and with what stake. A style is achieved when the prose could not be moved into another book without damage.

### Observable signals

- **Figurative density.** Figurative sentences per 100; a scene with more than one image per paragraph is usually decorating, not narrating `[hypothesis]`.
- **Image-field coherence.** Whether images come from one domain — weather, machinery, money, the body — or from whatever was nearest.
- **Voice consistency.** Whether narration and dialogue share a plausible relation; whether the diction changes when the focalizer changes.
- **Genericity test.** Swap the character names and the setting. If nothing breaks, the style is a default.
- **Tone stability.** Where irony, earnestness, and grief collide without a hinge.
- **Attitude.** Whether the narrator has a stance toward the events — judging, mourning, amused, withholding — and where it shows.

### Levers

- Write a voice contract: POV, distance, tense, register, attitude, three prohibitions, three obligations.
- Choose one image system per scene and cut every image outside it.
- Narrow the narrator's vocabulary below the author's; a narrator who knows fewer words is a person.
- Cut any metaphor that explains an emotion rather than replacing it.
- Decide what this narrator would never say, and keep the prohibition.

### Failure signatures

- **Universal literary voice:** polished, riskless prose that could be moved into any contemporary novel unchanged.
- **Purple prose:** image density with no event underneath, so the paragraph is all surface and no pressure.
- **Explanatory metaphor:** "the city was a wound, which is to say she had never healed" — the figure is followed by its own gloss.
- **Tone whiplash:** a comic simile in a grief scene with no hinge to justify the shift.
- **Narrator knows too much:** limited third that suddenly reports a fact the focalizer could not have.

### Diagnostic questions

- Could this paragraph be moved unchanged into another book?
- Is there an image this narrator would not make?
- Does the figurative language come from the character's world or the author's reading?
- What is the narrator's attitude, and where in the text does it become visible?

### Interface with other levels

L3 **masks** L4: beautiful prose hides absent meaning, and readers forgive a page that sounds wise. L3 is **masked by** L1: a few vivid words simulate a voice that is not there. It is often a **symptom** of L6 — the narrator sounds generic because the character has no specific relation to language, class, or period — and of L7 when the whole has not been conceived, so the voice has nothing to be a voice *of*. Fixing L3 without those is style-pasting.

### Genre and language variance

Noir is spare by contract; fantasy is ornate by contract; YA first-person present has its own grammar of immediacy. Judge the style against the genre's promises, not against a single literary norm. Russian has a strong voice technology in *сказ* — Leskov, Zoshchenko — where the narrator's oral syntax is the style; the Soviet "sincere" register and the intelligentsia's ironic distance are also period styles with rules. See `references/russian-prose.md`.

## L4 — Semantic

### What this level governs

Meaning, subtext, ambivalence, and echo: what the story is about as opposed to what happens in it, and whether that meaning is *in* the events or *next to* them. This level asks whether the reader can infer more than the narrator says, and whether the text rewards the inference.

### Observable signals

- **Theme statement.** Whether the theme appears verbatim, where, and in whose mouth.
- **Defensible readings.** How many interpretations of the ending you can support with quoted evidence.
- **Recurring object.** An object that appears at least three times and means something different each time.
- **Cost.** What the ending takes from the protagonist that they cannot get back.
- **Counter-evidence.** A character or event that refutes the stated theme.
- **Retroactive echo.** Whether the last page changes the meaning of the first.

### Levers

- Move the meaning into an object that changes hands, and let the change carry the argument.
- Cut the thesis sentence; if nothing is lost, it was never doing work.
- Give the character a belief the story disproves without commenting on it.
- Build the information ledger: what the reader knows, what the character knows, and when each learns it.
- Make the ending cost the thing the character wanted most.

### Failure signatures

- **Thesis in the narrator's mouth:** "And so she learned that home was not a place."
- **Symbol explained:** "the caged bird represented her marriage" — the figure is annotated instead of dramatised.
- **Ambiguity by omission:** two readings are possible only because neither has evidence.
- **Untested theme:** a moral question no event in the story puts at risk.
- **Resolution by fiat:** the ending declares a value the plot has not paid for.

### Diagnostic questions

- State the theme in one sentence. Is it anywhere in the text verbatim?
- What does the last paragraph cost the protagonist?
- Can you argue the opposite reading with evidence from the text?
- Which object appears three times and changes meaning?

### Interface with other levels

L4 is **masked by** L3 (fine prose) and by L5 (a strong plot carries a thin idea). It is often a **symptom** of L6: no contradiction in the characters means no subtext, because subtext is what a person cannot say. L4 is frequently **misdiagnosed as L1**: the reader says the prose is vague when the real problem is that the story has no idea precise enough to be vague about. L4 can also **mask** L6 by giving a character a thematic function instead of a psychology.

### Genre and language variance

Genre fiction may state its theme more openly: romance promises a value, thriller promises clarity, and a mystery that withholds meaning is not being subtle, it is failing its contract. Children's fiction often needs the theme nearer the surface. Russian prose has a specific history here: *тенденциозность* (tendentiousness) is the charge against a story whose idea is asserted rather than embodied; Chekhov's refusal of the thesis is a posture, not a universal law. See `references/russian-prose.md`.

## L5 — Narrative

### What this level governs

Plot, causality, agency, time, and scene construction: whether events are caused or merely sequenced, whether the protagonist makes the story happen, and whether each scene changes a state. L5 is where "and then" becomes "therefore" or "but", and where the reader's questions are designed.

### Observable signals

- **State change.** For each event, name what changed: knowledge, relationship, balance of power, or possibility. No change means no event.
- **Connective test.** Between consecutive scenes, write *therefore*, *but*, or *and then*. Count the *and then*s.
- **Agency at each turn.** Who initiates the change — the protagonist, another character, an accident, or the author?
- **Climax causality.** Whether the final turn is caused by a choice planted earlier, or by a fact introduced on the last page.
- **Time jumps.** Each anachrony's trigger: which question in the present does it answer?
- **Scene shape.** Question, entering state, turn, cost, exit state — five slots; a scene missing two is a summary with dialogue.

### Levers

- Write the fabula in seven bare sentences, chronological, named agents, active verbs.
- Convert every *and then* into *therefore* or *but*, or delete the event.
- Give the protagonist the decisive act; if they fail to act, make the failure the decision.
- Add a subplot with its own agent and cost that pressures the main theme from a different angle.
- Make the ending cost the protagonist the thing they wanted in act one.

### Failure signatures

- **External solution:** a storm, an inheritance, or a stranger resolves the plot the protagonist could not.
- **Last-page fact:** the answer was never planted, so the ending is a correction rather than a consequence.
- **Decorative subplot:** a second thread that never touches the main line's odds.
- **Research ellipsis:** a time jump that exists only to skip the part the writer did not want to dramatise.
- **Turnless scene:** two people talk, the reader learns nothing, and nothing is at risk.

### Diagnostic questions

- What does the protagonist want at the start, and what do they do about it in scene two?
- Which event could be deleted with no change to any later scene?
- Who causes the final turn, and was that cause planted before it was needed?
- What question does the reader hold at the end of each scene?

### Interface with other levels

L5 **masks** L6: plot momentum hides people with no inner life. It is **masked by** L3: beautiful prose over a broken spine. L5 problems often **present as L2** — the sentences are muddy because the causal relation is undecided — and as L4, when the theme feels thin because no event tested it. L7 problems (no whole) can also look like L5 problems, so check the premise before restructuring the plot.

### Genre and language variance

Literary fiction tolerates low event density if the state changes are internal and consequential; genre fiction requires external stakes and a legible promise. A quiet novel is not a plotless one, but the standard for "change" is different. Russian distinguishes *фабула* (the events in order) from *сюжет* (the order of telling), and the multi-line novel is a native form, not an exception. See `references/russian-prose.md`.

## L6 — Psychological

### What this level governs

Motivation, contradiction, defence, and cost: whether a character wants something specific enough to act on, whether they lie to themselves, and whether the story makes them pay for changing. L6 is the difference between a person and a function in a plot.

### Observable signals

- **Want and need.** Both statable in one sentence each, and in conflict; if they are the same, there is no story.
- **Self-description contradicted.** A character's account of themselves that a scene disproves.
- **Relational variation.** Whether the character behaves differently with different people.
- **Defence in behaviour.** A strategy visible in action — joking, diagnosing, withdrawing — rather than named by the narrator.
- **Cost paid on page.** The scene where the change costs a relationship, a status, or a self-image.
- **Privacy.** What the character would never say aloud, and where the text leaks it.

### Levers

- Give the character a self-description the next scene contradicts.
- Make the want and the need incompatible, not sequential.
- Put them where their usual strategy fails.
- Cut the explanatory backstory and put the same information into a choice.
- Make the change cost a relationship, and show the cost on the page.

### Failure signatures

- **Diagnosis as motivation:** "She was afraid of intimacy because her father left" — the backstory explains, the scene does not pressure.
- **One-note bravery:** a character who is courageous in every scene, so courage is a setting, not a choice.
- **Costless change:** an epiphany that costs no relationship and no self-image.
- **Backstory dump:** a chapter of history that would be better as one impossible choice.
- **Author's mouthpiece:** a character whose dialogue is the thesis, with no private stake in it.

### Diagnostic questions

- What does the character want, and what do they need?
- What would they never say aloud, and where does the text leak it?
- What do they lose by changing?
- Do they behave differently with different people?

### Interface with other levels

L6 is **masked by** L5: a well-plotted story can run on momentum with thin people. It **presents as** L4 when there is no subtext — because there is no contradiction to withhold — and as L3 when the voice is generic, because the character has no specific relation to language. L6 cannot be fixed at L1 or L2: no diction or rhythm supplies a want. It can itself **mask** L4 by turning a character into a case study whose psychology replaces the story's argument.

### Genre and language variance

Comedy can run on type rather than depth, and a thriller may need a legible motive more than a buried one; the standard is the contract, not interiority as such. Russian prose has a strong *психологизм* tradition — Tolstoy's "dialectic of the soul", Dostoevsky's ideological person — and a counter-tradition of the *положительный герой* (positive hero) whose psychology is subordinated to a thesis. Both are available; neither is the default. See `references/russian-prose.md`.

## L7 — Literary

### What this level governs

Composition, wholeness, originality, and impact: whether the parts are necessary to a whole, whether the ending changes the beginning, and whether the story could be described in one sentence that is itself interesting. L7 is not "better writing"; it is the level at which a work justifies its own existence rather than its sentences justifying themselves.

### Observable signals

- **Retroactive change.** Whether the last page makes the first page mean something it did not mean on arrival.
- **Principle of selection.** Why these scenes and not the equally good ones that were left out.
- **Title function.** Whether the title organises the reading or labels the topic.
- **Central question.** Whether the question the book asks is the question it is actually about.
- **Formal risk.** A structural or vocal risk that earns itself, rather than decorating.
- **One-sentence description.** Whether the book can be described without genre labels and without losing what it is.

### Levers

- Write the one-sentence "what this is" and test every scene against it.
- Design the ending first, then build the causal chain backwards.
- Choose a formal constraint that pressures the theme rather than illustrating it.
- Cut the scene that is good but not necessary.
- Make the title do work: a promise the text complicates.

### Failure signatures

- **Sum of good parts:** every scene works, and the book does not.
- **Twist as replacement:** a reveal that substitutes for meaning instead of deepening it.
- **Borrowed ending:** a final movement that could belong to another book.
- **Decorative form:** frames, footnotes, or multiple narrators with no pressure on the theme.
- **Premise as setting:** a world with no question in it.

### Diagnostic questions

- What is this book about in one sentence, and is that sentence interesting?
- What would be lost if the last chapter were removed?
- Does the first page mean something different after the last?
- Which scene is the one the book exists for?

### Interface with other levels

L7 is **masked by** L5 and L6: a well-made plot with real people can hide the absence of a whole. It is **misdiagnosed as L4** when the theme feels thin because the composition never earns it. L7 problems are often **premise problems** (E1): the story is well executed and should not have been written in this shape. Fixing L7 by adding a twist or a frame is the characteristic error; the repair is usually a decision about what the book is for.

### Genre and language variance

Series fiction values consistency and continuation over singularity; experimental fiction may choose opacity as a contract; a novella may legitimately be a single movement rather than a whole architecture. Russian has its own measure of *цельность* (wholeness) and a tradition of compositional looseness — Eikhenbaum on how Russian prose is built by accumulation rather than by a single architectonic plan — so a Western three-act standard is not the only test. See `references/russian-prose.md`.

## Level interaction map

This is the most useful table in the file. Read the symptom, resist the first diagnosis, run the test.

| Symptom | Looks like | Actually often | Test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every sentence has the same rhythm | L2 | Nothing new is being delivered sentence to sentence (L5/E6) | Mark the new information per sentence; if there is none, fix the scene |
| Description feels purple or vague | L3/L1 | The scene's function is undecided (L5/E8) | State the scene's turn; if you cannot, adjectives are substituting for it |
| Character voice sounds generic | L3 | The character has no specific relation to language, class, or period (L6/E3) | Name what this character would never say |
| Dialogue is on the nose | L3/E9 | Both speakers want the same thing (L6/E5) | Write each speaker's private goal for the scene. Unless naming is the act — a confession, a declaration, a warning — in which case directness is the choice |
| Ending feels unearned | L4/L7 | The climax is caused by an external event or a new fact (L5/E2) | Who acts, and what was planted before it was needed |
| Theme feels heavy or stated | L4 | No event contradicts the theme (L5/L6) | Find the counter-example; if there is none, write one |
| Story feels slow | L2/L3 | The scene has no turn (L5/E8) | Fill goal/obstacle/turn/cost/exit |
| Time jumps feel arbitrary | L5/E7 | The anachrony is not raised by a question in the present | Name the question the present scene has just created |
| Reader cannot tell who is speaking | L2 | Character diction is undifferentiated (L6/E9) | Remove dialogue tags and count idiolect markers |
| First chapter feels slow | L5 | The premise is unclear, so the reader has no question (L7/E1) | What question does the reader hold after page one? |
| Subplot feels irrelevant | L5 | The subplot repeats the main line instead of pressuring it (E12/E2) | Does it change the main line's odds? |
| Symbol feels heavy-handed | L4 | The symbol replaces an event instead of carrying one (L5) | What happens because of the object? |
| Character change feels sudden | L6 | No cost is paid on the page (L5) | Which scene shows the price? |

The map lists **common** misattributions, not laws; a symptom can genuinely belong to the level it resembles. Nor is it a licence to skip the lower level: if the test confirms L2, fix L2. The point is only that you run the test first.

## Scoring without false precision

Score each level 1–5 on the passage, using anchors. The anchors are about **function**, not beauty: 3 means the level is doing its job without distinction; 5 means it is doing structural work the text could not survive without; 1 means it is failing visibly and cannot be repaired locally.

| Level | 1 — broken | 3 — functional | 5 — structural |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | Words misname things; register is incoherent | Words are accurate; few are memorable | A word choice changes what the scene can mean |
| L2 | Syntax obscures; rhythm is accidental | Sentences are clear and varied enough | Rhythm carries the scene's tension or distance |
| L3 | Voice is generic or inconsistent | Voice is consistent and unremarkable | Voice is inseparable from the story's way of knowing |
| L4 | Meaning is asserted or absent | Meaning is inferable and supported | The ending retroactively changes the story's argument |
| L5 | Events are sequenced, not caused | Causality and agency hold | Structure is the story's meaning, not its container |
| L6 | Motivation is explained, not enacted | Want, need, and cost are legible | Contradiction generates the plot and the subtext |
| L7 | Parts do not form a whole | The whole is coherent and conventional | The whole is necessary and could not be another book |

Scores 2 and 4 are the intervals between these anchors. If you cannot say which side of 3 a passage falls on, score 3 — the anchor, not your mood, decides.

Four rules:

- **Never average.** The levels are not commensurable: a 5 in L1 cannot compensate a 2 in L5. An average of 3.9 can describe a book that fails at the only level that matters, and the number hides which level is binding.
- **Score the passage, not the book.** A brilliant chapter does not raise the score of a broken one; choose the unit and stay in it.
- **Take the lower score, then write the sentence that would have earned the higher one.** If you cannot write it, the lower score stands. If you can write it and it is present in the text, score higher. Optimism is not evidence; a missing sentence is.
- **Do not score what you cannot quote.** A level with no quoted evidence is unscored, not average.

Report a **profile**, not a total:

```
L1 3 · L2 2 · L3 3 · L4 2 · L5 4 · L6 3 · L7 2
Binding constraint: L7 — the parts are stronger than the whole.
Defects: (1) L7/E1: the premise is a setting, not a question.
         (2) L4: the ending asserts a value the plot never tests.
Invariant: keep the harbour scene; it is the book's one necessary place.
```

The profile has four parts: seven anchors, **one** binding constraint, at most two structural defects, and one invariant. The binding constraint is the level whose repair would change the most downstream text — not the lowest number. A 2 at L7 outranks a 2 at L1 every time.

Anchor calibration: re-score the same three passages later in the same session without looking at the first pass. If any level moves by more than one point, the anchor is underspecified — write the missing criterion instead of adjusting the score. Two readers who disagree by two points are not disagreeing about the text; they are using different definitions, and the definitions are the thing to fix.

## The seven-level pass in practice

Order: **L7 diagnosis → L5 → L6 → L4 → L3 → L2 → L1 → L7 re-check.**

Why this order:

- **L7 first, as diagnosis only.** The whole-work question sets the constraints for everything else. Do not fix it first; just know whether the book is the right book.
- **L5 before L6.** A scene with no turn cannot reveal a character; fix the event before the psychology.
- **L6 before L4.** Subtext is what a person cannot say; without contradiction there is nothing to withhold.
- **L4 before L3.** Meaning decides what the voice is for. A beautiful voice serving no argument is the most expensive thing to revise.
- **L3 before L2 and L1.** Voice decides which sentences are in register; line-editing before that is polishing the wrong diction.
- **L1 last.** Word choice is the cheapest repair and the most seductive distraction.

Rules for each pass: read the whole passage once without a pen; score only the level you are on; keep a "do not touch" list of things that are working; stop when the failure signature disappears, not when the level is perfect.

### Worked example

> Maria had always loved the sea. When she was a girl her father had taken her to the harbour every Sunday, and she had learned the names of the boats, the smell of diesel, and the way the gulls followed the trawlers in. He had told her that a boat was a promise you made to the weather. Now she stood on the same pier with the letter in her hand. The wind pulled at her coat. The harbour was quiet. A gull landed on the bollard and watched her. She had read the letter twice on the train and once more at the barrier. She thought about everything her father had taught her, and she understood that some things were more important than money, and that he would have understood too. She folded the letter and walked back towards the town, feeling lighter than she had in years.

**L5 first.** Nothing changes. Maria receives an offer — the letter implies the sale of something she loves — and by the end she feels lighter without any decision being dramatised. The letter's content is withheld; there is no obstacle, no counter-party, no choice, and no cost. The state-change test fails on all four counts: knowledge, relationship, balance of power, possibility. This is the binding defect, and no sentence-level repair touches it.

**L4 second.** The theme is stated ("some things were more important than money") and nothing in the scene tests it. The father's line about a promise to the weather is a real seed, but it is never put under pressure. The scene needs a counter-argument — the harbour is failing, the offer is real, the money would keep her mother in the house — and an object that carries the argument: the boat's name, the letter, the key.

**L2 third.** Three consecutive sentences begin with "She"; the only long sentences are the memory and the reflection; the paragraph ends on its most abstract phrase. But the monotony is a symptom. Because there is no decision, there is nothing for the rhythm to break around. Rewriting the syntax first would produce elegant sentences arranged around an event that still does not happen.

**L1** confirms the pattern: "everything her father had taught her" is the abstraction, and "feeling lighter" is an unearned label. Both resolve once L5 decides what the letter offers, what Maria does, and what it costs. One scene-level decision generates repairs at four levels; four line-level repairs generate none.

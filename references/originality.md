## What originality is not

Four things are routinely mistaken for originality, and each one is cheap to produce and easy to detect.

**Not novelty of premise.** A premise is the cheapest component of a story to replace and the least visible in the reading. *A woman inherits a house with a room she cannot open* is new; it is also the kind of new that any competent generator produces on request, because premises are generated by recombination and recombination is what the machine is for. A story with a familiar premise and an unfamiliar cost is more original than the reverse, and always has been.

**Not weirdness.** Strangeness is a surface property. A story can be strange in its furniture — a talking dog, a second moon, a narrator who is a filing cabinet — and entirely conventional in its decisions: the dog helps the lonely man, the second moon causes the flood, the filing cabinet is lonely. Weirdness changes what the reader looks at; it does not change what the story does.

**Not twist density.** A plot with six reversals is not six times as original as a plot with one. It is a plot with six attempts to be surprising, and surprise decays with use: the reader who has been reversed four times stops investing in any version of events, because investment requires the possibility of being right.

**Not obscurity.** A text that refuses to be understood is not difficult, it is unfinished, and the reader's response to it is not awe but fatigue. Confusion with a defence is the most common way a draft protects itself from the question *what did you decide?*

The general failure is **surprise for its own sake**: a deviation with no licence. Its effect on a reader is not admiration but a small loss of trust, because the reader has been asked to revise their model of the story without being given anything to revise it *with*. A surprise that costs the reader orientation and returns nothing is a tax, and readers stop paying.

The reframe that makes the rest of this file usable: **originality is not a property of the material, it is a property of the choice relative to what the setup made available.** Two writers can use the same event; one takes the move the setup hands them, the other pays a price the setup had been quietly accumulating. The second is original in the only sense a reader can feel.

The distinction has a working name: **novelty of object versus novelty of relation.** A new object — a machine, a creature, a profession, a city — is a new noun in a familiar sentence. A new relation — between two people, between a cost and the person who pays it, between what the reader knows and what a character can bear — is a new sentence. Objects are visible and cheap; relations are invisible and expensive, and relations are what a reader carries out of the book. The test: describe the original thing in your story without using any noun that could appear in a synopsis. If nothing remains, the originality was in the furniture.

**What the reader actually registers.** Not the new thing, but the moment a familiar thing is priced differently. A reader who has seen a hundred funerals does not need a new funeral; they need the funeral to cost something they had not considered — the eulogy that has to be written by the person the dead man ruined. This is why originality so often feels, in the reading, like recognition rather than surprise: the material is known and the arrangement is not. Surprise without recognition has no purchase; there is nothing for it to be surprising *about*.

## The modal-continuation problem

`[data: DetectGPT]` Text sampled from a language model tends to sit in regions of negative curvature of that model's log-probability function: step to any meaning-preserving rewrite and the probability falls. Human text usually sits on a slope instead, where rewrites score up and down and average near the original.

`[data: DNA-GPT]` Cut a text at the midpoint, keep the first half, and let a model regenerate the rest several times. Model-written remainders come back close to the original; human-written remainders come back further away. The paper's interpretation, in its own words: "the thought process of human writing does not simply follow the likelihood maximisation criterion" (§3).

Neither study measured stories. DetectGPT's fiction set is where its margin is weakest, and DNA-GPT tested no fiction at all. What they measured is *lexical continuation* — how close a re-write lands to the original words. Everything below extends that finding upward, from the sentence to the decision, and that extension is ours, not theirs.

`[hypothesis]` **The first idea is a sample from the modal distribution of "what happens next in a story like this."** Not from all possible next events — from the ones the setup makes available, weighted by how often they occur in stories that resemble this one. The result feels inevitable to the writer, and the inevitability is real: the idea was drawn from exactly the distribution that makes it feel that way.

Three consequences follow, and they are the operating assumptions of E11.

1. **Inevitability is not evidence of quality.** A choice can feel right because it is the only choice the setup offered, in the same way a sentence can feel right because it is the likeliest next sentence. The feeling is produced by fit, and fit is what a mode is.
2. **The second idea is usually still modal.** Asked for alternatives without a declared axis, a writer produces the second-most-likely event, then the third: same kind of choice, different nouns. This is why "think of three options" so often yields three versions of one option. Strictly, there is no single mode but a modal *region* — the handful of continuations the setup makes easy, most of which differ from each other only in detail. The test is therefore not "is this the likeliest single thing" but "would a re-writer working from this setup arrive here".
3. **The mode is a property of the setup as much as of the writer.** A scene whose setup leaves only one thing to do will be written the same way by everyone, and rewriting the sentences will not change that. The repair is upstream.

In literary terms: the modal continuation is **the scene a competent re-writer would produce from the same setup**. Not a bad scene — competent is the problem. It is well-made, causally clean, emotionally legible, and it belongs to the setup rather than to an author. The setup wrote it; you typed it.

This is also why the most common advice about originality fails. "Be surprising" selects for low probability without checking licence, which produces the arbitrary. "Be specific" produces detail without decision, which produces the merely textured. The workable question is narrower: *which decision here was supplied by the situation, and what would it cost to make a different one?*

**Why the mode feels like inspiration.** The modal choice arrives faster than deliberation and with a feeling of rightness: this is the scene, this is obviously what happens. The speed is the tell. Deliberation is slow because it compares; recognition is fast because nothing is being compared. A writer who takes the first idea is not being lazy — they are receiving a very well-fitted answer from a distribution built out of everything they have read, which is precisely the distribution that also built the reader's expectations. The fit is real. It is just not authorship.

**What a re-writer cannot reproduce.** Three things survive the truncate-and-regenerate test badly, and they are the three levers available at the line: a word chosen against the grain of the sentence's own logic, a specific detail that pays no immediate rent, and a thread left deliberately open. `[theory]` Of the three, the unpaid detail is the most durable, because it is content rather than phrasing — a paraphrase keeps the detail and loses the word. This is why "add a strange detail" is better advice than "use unusual words", and why neither is as good as changing a decision.

The essential discipline: **alternatives must differ in decision, not in wording.** *She refuses to sign* and *she declines to put her name to it* are one choice. *She refuses to sign* and *she signs and then spends the advance on a lawyer* are two. If your alternative set can be paraphrased into a single sentence, you generated vocabulary, not divergence.

**The mode is form-specific.** Every genre carries its own modal continuation, and this is where "avoid the default" is most often misapplied, because rejecting the form's promise and rejecting its furniture are very different acts.

| Form | The promised move (keep it) | The modal furniture (free to change) | A less-modal cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Crime** | A discovery that reorders suspicion | The damaged detective, the bottle, the dead partner | The detective must conceal evidence to protect a colleague, so competence and loyalty become the same act |
| **Romance** | An obstacle that holds until a choice is made | The misunderstanding one conversation would clear | One of them must give up the thing that made them attractive to the other |
| **Horror** | An intrusion that cannot be reasoned with | The isolated house, the sceptical spouse, the child who sees it | The witness must be believed, and belief costs them their standing in the town |
| **SFF** | A world whose rules produce a consequence | The chosen one, the empire, the prophecy | The rule that saves the world also makes the protagonist's own life unlivable, in a specific domestic way |
| **Literary** | A shift in understanding, paid for | The unhappy marriage, the return home, the withheld confession | The character understands, does nothing, and the story refuses to punish them for it |
| **Historical** | A period rendered with consequence | The war, the letter, the servant's view of the great house | The period's law is the mechanism, not the backdrop: a form, a tax, a marriage settlement that decides the plot |

A murder discovered by the detective is promised by the form; a detective who drinks alone is promised by nothing except the last forty years of the form. The first is a convention the reader is paying for. The second is a habit the writer is paying for, usually without knowing it.

**Two ways the mode is misidentified.** First, mistaking your first draft for the mode. The mode is not your first sentence; it is the choice a competent re-writer would make from the same setup, which is often *not* what you wrote at speed — speed produces your particular habits, and habits are idiosyncratic as well as generic. Second, mistaking a sentence-level mode for an arc-level one. A fresh-sounding sentence can execute a completely conventional decision, and a plain sentence can execute a startling one. Judge the level you are actually working at, or the test will flatter the prose and miss the story.

## The five originality moves

Five moves cover most of the usable space. Each leaves the causal chain intact and changes the *arrangement* of cost, knowledge, or scale — which is why each can be plausible when the setup has prepared it. Every example below is a structure, not prose.

**(a) Change the cost, not the event.**

> Ilya agrees to drive his brother to the clinic on Thursday. The drive costs him a shift at the depot, which he can make up. What it costs him is a signature on the admission form — the brother has told the family that Ilya is the one who decides things, and the form makes the story true.

It stays plausible because nothing in the world has been altered: clinics have forms, brothers have arrangements, and a day's pay is a real cost. The move only re-specifies *which* cost the scene is about, and the new cost was already latent in the situation. The implausible version replaces the event instead — the brother dies in the car — which requires new machinery and buys a smaller return.

**(b) Change who pays.**

> The three siblings sell the house within a month, and the money solves what it was supposed to solve. The neighbour who has cut the grass for eleven years is the buyer, at the asking price, with no conditions. Nobody notices that he is the only one who has been paying for the house all along, and now he has stopped.

It stays plausible because the neighbour is established by an ordinary fact — the grass — before he is given weight. Moving the cost to someone with no legal standing in the transaction is a decision about *whose story this is*, and it works when the person was already visible in the margins. The implausible version introduces the neighbour at the moment he becomes important, which turns him into a device.

**(c) Change the timing of a revelation.**

> Nadia learns on a Tuesday that her partner has been moving money. She spent Monday in a meeting telling the bank that he is the only person she trusts. The revelation arrives after the defence, so the reader watches her spend the next scene deciding whether to retract it or live inside it.

It stays plausible because the facts are identical and only the schedule moved. Timing is free — it requires no new character, no new document, no change of setting — and it is the highest-yield move available, because it changes what the revelation *does* rather than what it is. The implausible version withholds the fact from the reader while the character knows it, which is cheating (see `references/temporal-complexity.md` §9).

**(d) Change the frame.**

> A man describes the night his foreman died, in a room with a stenographer and a clock. He is not confessing; he is establishing that he was asleep, and she is being paid to make his account permanent. By the third hour the account has changed shape, and she is the only one who notices, because she is the only one writing it down.

It stays plausible because a deposition is an ordinary procedure with an ordinary pressure — the record, the clock, the fee. Changing who is told, and why now, changes what the telling is *for*, and that changes every sentence inside it without touching a single event. The implausible version adds a frame that only supplies a reason for the telling: the found manuscript, the grandchild who asks.

**(e) Change the scale.**

> The wedding is in a hotel because the bride's village is under water. There are a hundred and forty guests, a band, and a cake that took two days. The only thing anyone will remember is who was allowed to sit at the table with the bride's mother, and that was decided in ninety seconds by a man with a seating chart and a pen.

It stays plausible because the large event is used as *setting*, not as plot, and the intimate stake is exactly the kind of thing that decides real weddings. Shrinking the stakes inside a big event, or enlarging them inside a small one, changes the story's resolution without changing its furniture. The implausible version makes the flood the antagonist and the seating chart a metaphor, which is two stories fighting for one page.

The moves are not exclusive and not exhaustive. They are five levers on the same machine: what it costs, who pays, when it is known, who is telling, and at what size. When E11 asks for alternatives "on a declared axis", these are the axes.

**Budget: at most two moves in one scene.** Each move changes the reader's model of what the scene is doing, and two changes at once is not a surprising scene, it is a different scene. The exception is a move that *depends* on another — changing who pays usually requires changing the timing so that the payer is the one who learns. When you stack, name the dependency; when you cannot, take one.

**Escalation is not a cost change.** The commonest misreading of move (a) is to make the cost bigger. Bigger is a quantity; different is a kind. A man who might lose his job and a man who might lose his children are the same story with the volume up, and volume is the dial a reader stops hearing after two turns. The move is to change what *kind* of thing is at stake: from money to permission, from reputation to custody, from dying to being forgiven by one particular person.

## Plausibility accounting

A surprising choice must be **prepared** and **priced**. Preparation is what makes it available; price is what makes it cost something. Neither alone is enough: a prepared surprise with no cost is a cleverness, and a costly surprise with no preparation is an accident.

**The three-part test.** Before committing to a non-modal choice, answer all three in writing:

1. **Was there a seed?** Name the earlier line, object, trait, or pressure that made this choice available. Not "it's in character" — the actual material. If the answer is "none", the choice is not available yet, and the fix is to go back and plant it, not to defend the choice.
2. **Is there a cost?** Name what is lost, by whom, and whether the text spends anything on it. Cost that exists only in the synopsis is not cost. The text must pay in scene, in consequence, or in a visible change of possibility.
3. **Does the character's history make it possible?** Not "would anyone do this" but "could *this* person, given what they have done and been, do this without becoming someone else". A choice that requires a new person is a different story wearing this one's clothes.

If any answer is no, the surprise is arbitrary, and the reader will feel the arbitrariness before they can name it. The feeling is specific: not "that was unlikely" but "that was unearned", which is a judgement about the author rather than about the character.

A single no is a repair instruction rather than a verdict, and each one has its own fix. **No seed** → go back and plant it, in a scene that had its own reason to exist. **No cost** → write the scene where it is paid; do not add a second seed to compensate. **No history** → either the choice or the character's past has to move, and moving the past is usually the honest option, because the choice is what the story is about.

**Preparation is broader than foreshadowing.** Foreshadowing is a promise; preparation is availability. A competence can be prepared by the way a character holds a tool in chapter two, with no hint that it will matter. A moral limit can be prepared by a refusal in an unrelated argument. The test is availability at the moment of use, not salience beforehand — which is the same fairness rule that governs reveals (`references/temporal-complexity.md` §9).

**Setup and preparation are different budgets.** Setup is what the scene needs to be legible: who is in the room, what they want, why now. Preparation is what the *choice* needs to be available. Drafts over-fund the first and under-fund the second, which is why a scene can be perfectly clear and still feel unearned: the reader was told everything except the one thing that would have made the outcome possible.

**Price is what makes a surprise retroactively necessary.** After the fact, the reader should be able to see that the surprising choice was the *cheapest* available option — that the alternatives would have cost more, in money, in shame, in exposure, in the kind of person the character would have had to become. A surprise that looks cheap in retrospect reads as a shortcut even if it was well prepared.

One practical consequence: when a choice passes the three-part test but still feels wrong on the page, the problem is usually price. The seed is there, the history is there, and nothing was spent. Add the scene where it costs. Do not add another seed.

**A case that fails, and its repair.** The surprise: *the foreman's death was arranged by the narrator's mother, who has been dead six years.* Run the test. **Seed:** none — the mother appears in two sentences and is characterised only by her illness. **Cost:** none to her; she is beyond consequence, so the revelation costs the narrator only a feeling. **History:** nothing establishes her as capable of it, or as having any motive that the foreman's death would serve. Three noes, so the surprise is arbitrary, and readers will say the ending "came out of nowhere" without being able to say why.

The repair is not to add a scene of the mother being sinister. It is to give the revelation to a person whose history already permits it: the foreman's brother, who has appeared three times doing small favours — driving the narrator to the site, lending a ladder, signing for a delivery. **Seed:** the favours, which now read as proximity. **Cost:** he has to be the one who benefits from the death, and the narrator has to decide whether to expose a man who has been kind to her. **History:** a brother with a grievance and access is the ordinary shape of such a crime, and the text has already been showing the access. Same revelation, moved to a person the story had been quietly preparing, and now it costs someone something.

## Avoiding repetition across a body of work

Originality is not only a property of a story. It is a property of a *body* of stories, and the failure mode is invisible from inside any single one: every piece is defensible, and the fifth piece is the first one again with different weather.

`[data: StoryScope]` In a feature space of narrative decisions, human stories sit further from the centre and further apart from each other than model stories: mean rarity percentile 0.71 against 0.49, Cohen's d = 0.83, and 24.7% of human stories fall in the corpus-wide rarest decile against 7.1% of model stories. The paper operationalises originality as statistical rarity — a proxy, not a measure of merit — and its own limitations section says so.

The usable finding is about *dispersion*, not about the tail: human stories are spread across the space, model stories cluster. A writer producing several stories has the same problem a model has, and for the same reason — the same sensibility, applied to the same kinds of material, produces the same region of the space.

**The practical rule: vary which dimension you innovate on, and do not innovate on all of them at once.**

Six dimensions are enough to track:

| Dimension | What varies | The tell that you have stopped varying it |
|---|---|---|
| **Premise type** | The situation and its central pressure | Every story is a return, an inheritance, or a disappearance |
| **Structure** | Order, framing, number of lines | Every story runs chronologically with one flashback near the middle |
| **POV / voice** | Person, distance, register, reliability | Every narrator is a wry, wounded first person with a good eye |
| **Moral frame** | Who is judged, by what standard, and whether the judgement holds | Every story forgives its protagonist and indicts a parent |
| **Ending mode** | Resolved, unresolved, reversed, cost paid, cost deferred | Every story ends on an image of weather and a small peace |
| **Sensory / emotional register** | The dominant channel and temperature | Every story smells of rain and is sad in the same key |

**The same-story-twice test.** Describe each work in one sentence *of decision*, not of subject: *a woman chooses accuracy over loyalty and is punished by the institution she served*, *a man chooses silence to protect a dead friend's version of events*. If two sentences are the same, you have repetition regardless of setting, century, or genre. Subject is the easiest thing to vary and the least visible to a reader who has read you before.

**Series and collections.** A series reuses a world and often a voice, by design, and that is not repetition — a reader returns for the world. What must not repeat is the *decision structure*: the cost the story charges, the moral frame it judges by, the mode it ends in. The specific risk is the long-running series that varies premise every volume and repeats the same moral frame thirty times, so that the reader can predict not the crime but the verdict. If the detective always ends by understanding the killer, the series has one ending and many plots.

**Signature versus rut.** Both look the same from outside: you keep making a certain move. The difference is whether you can say why *this* piece needed it. A signature is a dimension you return to with a reason; a rut is a dimension you return to because it is where you are fluent. Fluency is the danger, not the repetition — the move you can make without thinking is the move that will keep being available, and availability is how a body of work converges.

**A case of concealed clustering.** A writer's last five pieces, described by decision: a nurse chooses the patient over her sister; a translator chooses accuracy over her brother; a locksmith chooses the job over the woman who hired him; a stenographer chooses the record over the client; a courier chooses the delivery over his mother. Five worlds, five professions, five endings that feel different. One decision, five times: the professional chooses the task over the person and is not punished for it. The premises vary and the moral frame does not, which is why readers who like the first one will say the fifth is "the same, somehow", and be unable to say how.

The correction is one axis, not a new subject: keep the professional, and change the frame so that the task and the person are the same thing — the translator's accuracy is the only way to protect her brother, so choosing one is choosing both, and the cost lands where she cannot see it. Same furniture, one dimension moved.

**The opening test.** Read the first two pages of your last five pieces back to back. Clustering is visible there before it is visible anywhere else, because openings are where a writer's defaults are least defended: the same hour of the day, the same kind of narrator, the same shape of first sentence. If four of the five openings could be swapped between pieces without anyone noticing, the body of work has one door.

## The anti-originality trap

Chasing rarity produces incoherence, and it does so by a predictable route. Each deviation costs the reader orientation; deviations compound; at some density the reader stops building a model of the story and starts waiting for the next oddity. The text becomes a sequence of surprises with no through-line, which is the one thing a surprising text cannot afford.

**The rule: one deliberate deviation per story, defended, with everything else conventional enough to carry it.** Not one per page, not one per scene. One, at the level of the decision the story is built on — the cost nobody else would charge, the frame nobody else would choose, the scale nobody else would notice — and a conventional apparatus around it so the reader has somewhere to stand while they absorb it.

The reason is economic. Conventions are not a failure of imagination; they are the reader's payment system. Genre furniture, familiar rhythms, recognisable emotional beats — these let a reader read without effort, and the effort they save is spent on the one thing you are asking them to do differently. A story that innovates on every axis has no payment system and asks the reader to work on credit.

Three conditions where the rule relaxes, so that it is a rule and not a prohibition:

- **Very short work.** A two-page story can afford a higher deviation density because the reader's orientation cost is bounded and the whole piece can be held in mind at once.
- **Works that teach their own rules.** A long text can accumulate deviations if each one is introduced as a rule the reader can then apply — an absurdist world establishes its logic in the first ten pages and spends the rest being consistent with it.
- **Deliberately strange modes.** In surrealism, satire, or fabulism, deviation *is* the convention, and the conventional apparatus is the strangeness itself. The rule then applies in reverse: be rigorous inside the strangeness, because there is nothing else holding the reader.

Outside those conditions, if you find yourself with three defended deviations, you are probably writing two stories. Choose the one that carries the question and make the others ordinary.

**The second deviation is usually the one you can defend.** A first deviation tends to be structural and unnerving — a frame, a reversal, an unsympathetic protagonist. A second one, added later in revision, tends to be the one with a ready argument attached, because you added it to solve a problem and the argument is the solution's packaging. Treat your own fluency in defending a choice as a warning sign, not a credential. The load-bearing deviation can be defended in one sentence that names a cost; the surplus deviation needs a paragraph and still feels thin.

**Which deviation is load-bearing.** Remove each deviation and ask whether the story's question survives. If the question survives intact, the deviation was decoration and can be normalised without loss — this is the test that keeps a strange draft from becoming a strange draft with a plot attached. The load-bearing deviation is the one whose removal changes what the ending *means*, not merely what it looks like.

## Originality diagnostics

Six questions. The first two are the ones that catch most drafts.

Run them on a finished draft, not on an idea — an idea has no costs yet, so every answer will be flattering. Answer in a sentence each; a question answered in one word is a question not answered.

1. **State the story's central decision in one sentence, then ask: what would a competent writer do here, and what did I do instead?** If the two are the same, the decision is modal — not necessarily wrong, but it needs a reason, and the reason has to be more specific than "it works".
2. **Which cost in this story is invented, and which is inherited?** Inherited costs (grief, money, illness, duty) are free and therefore generic. The invented cost is usually where the story actually lives.
3. **Name the seed for the story's most surprising moment.** The earlier material that made it available. If you cannot name it, the surprise is arbitrary, and no amount of revision at the sentence level will fix it.
4. **Who pays for the ending?** If the answer is "the protagonist, emotionally", the ending is the default ending. Find the person whose payment the story has not yet collected.
5. **Whose story is this told as, and why now?** If the answer is "the protagonist's, because it is hers", the frame is unexamined. The frame is free to change and it changes everything inside it.
6. **Across your last three pieces, which dimension did you innovate on each time?** If it is the same dimension three times, you have a signature, which is fine — and one region of the space, which is the problem. Vary the axis next time, not the intensity.

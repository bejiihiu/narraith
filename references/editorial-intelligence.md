# Editorial Intelligence: From Diagnosis to Draft

> Purpose: the four editorial passes, the order they run in, and the repair patterns that turn a finding into a changed sentence.

## The four editorial passes

| Pass | Territory | May change | Must not change | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Developmental** | premise, question, protagonist, ending, cost, the shape of the whole | the ending, a character's role, the question itself | sentences, except to point at them | verdict, rewrite-or-repair decision |
| **Structural** | scenes, order, proportion, subplots, POV per scene | cut, merge, expand, move, re-enter a scene | the premise, the ending's answer | scene inventory, cut/merge list |
| **Line** | paragraphs and sentences: meaning, information, rhythm, words | rewrite sentences, split and merge paragraphs, cut explanation | what happens; the beats | annotated text or line audit |
| **Copy** | consistency, punctuation, facts, style mechanics | spelling, punctuation, names, numbers | voice, rhythm, meaning | clean text, query list |

A pass may not do the next pass's work. The structural editor who rewrites a sentence has begun line-editing without a diagnosis. The line editor who cuts a beat has begun structural editing without a plan. One person may run all four; the constraint is the order, not the headcount.

**Four clocks.** The passes run at different speeds: a premise change takes a week, a comma takes a second. The order exists so the cheap passes are not spent on text the expensive one is about to replace.

**Why line-editing before structure is a mistake, not merely inefficient.**

1. **It wastes the work.** A structural repair rewrites the paragraphs the line pass polished. The polish is not lost in principle — some of it survives — but it was performed against a text whose job was about to change.
2. **It hides the problem.** Polished sentences make a broken scene read as working. This is the most expensive effect: the editor loses the ability to see the defect precisely because the surface is now clean, and the draft goes out with a well-written scene that does nothing.
3. **It teaches the wrong lesson.** A writer whose flat scene comes back with better sentences concludes that the problem was diction. The next draft repeats the structure and gets polished again.

The passes are not a waterfall. Developmental editing is re-entered when a structural pass reveals that the premise cannot carry the scenes. But the loop always runs cheap-to-expensive: changing a premise costs a draft, changing a comma costs a second.

**When the request is "edit this".** The four passes are a sequence, not a menu. Decide which pass the sample can support, run that one, and say which passes you did not run and why. Running all four on 800 words produces surface corrections on a structure nobody has checked — and the writer receives a clean copy of a scene that still does not work. Running only the pass the sample supports is the honest version, and it is the one that produces advice usable today.

*Exception.* Line-editing a short sample to demonstrate a mechanism is legitimate — a 200-word passage rewritten to show what "cut the explanation" means. Label it as demonstration and do not present it as the revision order.

## Developmental editing

Interrogate four things: the premise, the question, the ending, the protagonist's cost. The rest of this pass is detail on those four.

**The ten questions.** Ask them of the whole work, not of a chapter. Each has a failure answer; the failure answers are the point.

1. **What question does the reader want answered?** Phrase it as a question with at least two possible answers. *Failure:* you can only state a topic ("grief, and a house").
2. **Whose story is it, and is that the person who pays?** *Failure:* the POV character observes while someone else absorbs the cost.
3. **What does the protagonist want, and what do they need that contradicts it?** *Failure:* want and need are the same thing, so the story has no inner pressure.
4. **What do they believe that is false, and where did the belief come from?** *Failure:* the false belief is named in the synopsis and never visible in behaviour.
5. **What does acting cost, and what would success cost?** *Failure:* one of the two is missing, which makes the climax either free or pointless.
6. **What is the pressure, and why is it right from inside itself?** *Failure:* the antagonist is wrong and knows it, which produces melodrama.
7. **What does the ending answer, and is it question 1?** *Failure:* the ending answers "will they be together" when the book asked "can she be forgiven".
8. **What is the reader allowed to know, and when does each revelation land?** *Failure:* the information order is whatever the chronology supplies.
9. **Which obvious solution was refused, and what replaced it?** *Failure:* nothing was refused; the draft is the modal continuation.
10. **What form is this, and does the material fit it?** *Failure:* a novella's material in a short story's frame, or a short story's material stretched to a novel by subplot.

**Two tests for the premise.** *The "and then" test*: state the premise, then ask "and then?" three times. If the third answer is a variation of the first, the premise contains one move. *The "so what" test*: state the premise in a sentence to an imaginary reader and ask what they now want to know. If the answer is "nothing in particular", the premise has a situation and no question. Both tests are cheap, and both are usually failed at the premise rather than in the draft — which is why they belong here and not in diagnosis.

**Repair or rebuild.** Both are legitimate; confusing them is not.

**Repair** when the question is sound and the execution fails. Signature: you can state a fix that does not change what the story is about. Missing cost can be added; a scene can be moved; an ending can be delayed; a POV can be moved to the person who pays.

**Rebuild** when one of these is true:

- the "question" cannot be phrased as a question;
- the protagonist is not the person who pays, and cannot become one without a different story;
- the ending answers a different question than the opening asked;
- the premise contains its own conclusion — there is nothing the protagonist could discover that would change anything.

**The test:** try to state a repair. If every repair you can state changes the question, you are rebuilding. Say so before you start. A rebuild keeps the premise's *idea* and treats the draft as source material; a repair keeps the draft. The difference determines whether the next month produces a manuscript or notes.

## Structural editing

**Scene inventory.** Fill this before deciding anything. One row per scene, in order.

| # | Scene | POV | What changes | What it costs | Reader learns | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Anna refuses the loan | Anna | refusal stands | sister's goodwill | the debt exists | keep |
| 2 | Dinner, the offer | Anna | she accepts | nine years of refusal | father's debt is hers | keep |
| 3 | Anna tells Michael | Michael | nothing | nothing | Anna accepted | cut or merge |
| 4 | The office, the transfer | Anna | she lies to keep the job | a colleague's trust | the job is at risk | keep, move earlier |

How to read the table:

- A **"nothing" in "what changes"** means the scene is summary. Either give it a change or fold it into a scene that has one.
- A **"nothing" in "what it costs"** across three consecutive rows means the middle has no price. This is the commonest structural defect in a draft that reads well and does not hold.
- **Two rows with the same change** are one scene. Merge them, or make the second a consequence rather than a repetition.
- A change of **POV** with no new information is drift. POV moves when the new vantage point tells the reader something the old one could not.

**Cut, merge, expand, move.** Four decisions, four criteria.

- **Cut** when the scene's change is repeated elsewhere, or its only function is information that can travel in a line of dialogue or a paragraph of summary.
- **Merge** when two scenes change the same state under the same pressure. The second is an echo; the echo may be the scene's best material, in which case move the best beat into the first and delete the rest.
- **Expand** when the change is load-bearing and happens off-page, in a clause, or in retrospect. The turn needs to be dramatised at the distance where its cost is visible.
- **Move** when the scene is right and its position is wrong. Moving is cheaper than rewriting and is the most under-used repair: a revelation two scenes early flattens everything between.

The default is not "cut". Cutting a scene whose cost is unique removes the story's price, and the draft gets faster and thinner at the same time.

**Proportion.** Structural editing is also the pass that checks how much room each part gets against what it carries. The check is crude and effective: which relationship receives the most words, and is it the one the story is about? If the scene carrying the turn is shorter than the scene setting it up, the setup is the story the draft is actually telling. Measure by scene count and page space, not by importance in the synopsis. A subplot that occupies a third of the middle and touches the spine once is not a subplot; it is the story the writer was interested in. Either promote it or cut it — leaving it at a third and calling it a subplot produces the characteristic long-novel sag.

**Subplot audit.** Each subplot must do three things: touch the spine at two or more points, change the main line's stakes or the protagonist's understanding, and carry its own question with an answer. A subplot that only varies tone is a digression; one that restates the main question is a dilution. Both are cuttable regardless of how good the writing is.

**The ending test.** Four checks, in order:

1. Restate the opening question and the closing answer. Same question? If not, the ending is the answer to a story you did not write.
2. Did the answer cost? An answer obtained without loss is a resolution, not an ending.
3. Is the last line on the change, or after it? The commonest ending failure is a good final beat followed by two sentences of settling.
4. What does the reader know at the end that they could not have known at the start — and is that knowledge the point? If the knowledge is incidental, the ending is a stop, not a conclusion.

## Line editing

**Order of operations.** Four passes over the same paragraph, in this order. Do not collapse them.

1. **Meaning.** What does this sentence assert, and is it the right assertion? If not, this is a structural problem in miniature: cut or replace, do not polish.
2. **Information.** What does the reader learn here, is it new, and is it in the right order relative to what they already know?
3. **Rhythm.** Where does the sentence want its stress? Does its length match its job — long for a complex thought or a sustained image, short for a landing?
4. **Words.** Only now: precision, register, repetition.

The order exists because each earlier pass invalidates work done in a later one. Polishing words first locks in an assertion you are about to change, and the next pass then fights the polish.

**The seven repair patterns.** Each is a mechanism, not a rule. The before/after pairs are illustrative — the point is the move.

**1. De-abstract.** Replace a concept with the object or habit that produced it.
`Before:` A vague unease had settled over the house since the letter.
`After:` Since the letter his mother had stopped putting out the second cup.

**2. Unstack.** Move one modifier into its own sentence so the reader is not holding three unresolved.
`Before:` He opened the swollen, warped, paint-blistered door and stepped into the smell of her.
`After:` The door had swollen with damp and took his shoulder to open. Inside, the smell of her.

**3. Split.** Break a sentence carrying two jobs into two, each with one. The split often reveals the evasion the compound was hiding.
`Before:` She had come to tell him about the diagnosis, but the kitchen was clean for once, so she asked whether he had eaten.
`After:` She had come to tell him about the diagnosis. The kitchen was clean, which it never was. She asked whether he had eaten.

**4. Merge.** Join two sentences that share a subject and a thought into one period that holds the relation. Merging earns length by carrying an inference.
`Before:` The river had dropped. You could see the old bridge footings again.
`After:` The river had dropped far enough to show the old bridge footings, which meant the drought was worse than the ministry admitted.

**5. Invert.** Reorder so the new information lands where the language puts stress — final position in English. Use it when the sentence's emphasis is on the wrong element.
`Before:` A knife was in her hand when he came in, which he noticed before anything else.
`After:` He came in and noticed the knife before he noticed her face.

**6. Cut the explanation.** Delete the sentence that tells the reader what the previous sentence meant. It is almost always the sentence after the good one.
`Before:` She set the second plate down, then put it back in the cupboard. It was a way of admitting he wasn't coming.
`After:` She set the second plate down, then put it back in the cupboard.

**7. Replace the dead image.** A simile whose vehicle could attach to anything is not an image. Often the repair is not a better simile but a concrete situation.
`Before:` The silence between them was like a wall.
`After:` They sat in the car with the engine off and let the meter run.

**At the paragraph.** The same four operations apply one size up. A paragraph should carry one thought or one beat; two means split. A single-sentence paragraph inside a run of long ones is a beat landing — check that it lands where the turn is, not where the writer got tired. Check the break, not only the sentence: a break placed after the explanation rather than before it is the commonest way a good sentence is spent. In dialogue, a new speaker is a new paragraph; two speakers in one paragraph make the reader re-attribute a line, and re-attribution reads as confusion even when the line is clear.

**The check.** A repair is not an improvement unless the meaning survives it. Re-read the paragraph, not the sentence: the repair that fixes a sentence and damages its paragraph has failed.

## Copy editing

Four territories, and one rule that outranks the rest.

- **Consistency.** The same fact stated the same way: spellings, invented terms, units, capitalisation, numbers. Build the list as you go; it is the manuscript's memory.
- **Punctuation.** Within the work's own system. If the book uses dashes for interruption, chapter twelve does not switch to ellipses without a reason. Dialogue punctuation follows the language's convention — see `references/english-prose.md` and `references/russian-prose.md`.
- **Fact-checking inside the fiction's rules.** If the tide comes in at six, it comes in at six in every scene. If a character is a doctor, their medical errors must be characterisation, never the author's ignorance. Research the real world far enough that the invented world can be consistent with itself.
- **House style never overrides voice.** A style guide that forbids fragments, or mandates a serial comma, yields to a narrator whose voice depends on the fragment. Apply house style to what the narrator does not control — front matter, chapter titles, running heads, correspondence — and to the text only where the text is silent. If the house style would change the rhythm, flag it and leave it; do not silently normalise.

**What copy editing may not do:** reorder words for elegance, "improve" a deliberate repetition, resolve an intentional ambiguity, standardise a dialect, or add a comma that changes a pause the voice needs.

**Query, do not silently decide.** When an item is ambiguous — a pronoun with two possible referents, a date that may be wrong, a name spelled two ways — flag it with a question rather than picking the likelier reading. The author knows which was meant; the editor is guessing. On a long manuscript a query list is worth more than a clean page, because every silent decision is one the author must later reverse without knowing it existed.

## Dialogue editing

Five passes over one exchange. Each has a test.

1. **Agenda.** Write each speaker's want in the margin above their first line. If both agendas are identical, one speaker is redundant. If neither has one, the exchange is exposition wearing quotes.
2. **Act-reply.** For each reply, name the act it answers: a test, a retreat, a claim of innocence, a bid for reassurance, a change of subject. Replace any reply that answers only the literal content. If no reply answers an act, the scene is a question-and-answer session, not a conversation.
3. **Idiolect.** Cover the names. Can you tell who is speaking? Idiolect is syntax and a small set of recurring words, not accent. Give each speaker a habitual sentence shape: one speaks in subordinate clauses, one in fragments, one answers questions with questions.
4. **Cut the ritual.** Remove greetings, confirmations, and "as you know". Enter after the ritual and leave before the farewell. Keep the logistics that carry: who gives an exact time and date, who refuses to say a name, who pays.
5. **Land the shift.** Find the line where the exchange changes something — a concession, a threat made real, a fact released, an alliance moved. If it is not there, the scene has no turn. If it is the last line, the scene ends correctly. If it is in the middle, everything after it is a farewell and should be cut.

**The surface check.** Subtext needs a surface. If nothing is said, there is no subtext, only absence. The talk must be genuinely about something — the parking fee, the sister's operation — and that thing must matter enough that the characters would discuss it if nothing else were wrong.

## Pacing revision

Do not diagnose pacing by counting words. Word count measures speed; pacing is the rate at which the reader's questions open and close.

**Four diagnostics.**

1. **Question density.** How many questions is the reader holding on this page, and are they being answered faster than they are opened? Pacing is that ratio. A page that closes two questions and opens none reads as an ending even in the middle of a chapter.
2. **Scene-to-summary ratio.** Where is the reader told rather than shown, and is the told material load-bearing or connective? Long summary after a scene reads as anticlimax; long summary before a scene reads as throat-clearing. Connective summary belongs *between* pressures, not inside one.
3. **Placement of revelation.** A revelation lands where the reader has just enough to suspect it and not enough to be sure. Earlier is flat — nothing to wonder about. Later is late — the reader has already guessed and is now waiting for the characters to catch up, which is the most reliable way to make a plot feel stupid.
4. **Length of the approach to the turn.** Count *beats*, not words: how many attempts, exchanges, or obstacles before the scene turns. A long approach with no failed attempt is delay. A short approach with no pressure is abrupt. The approach should contain the attempt that fails in a new way.

**The pacing map.** Before repairing anything, mark each scene `+` (opens more questions than it closes), `−` (closes more than it opens), or `=` (balanced). Read the sequence of marks. Three `−` in a row is a sag that no sentence-level work will fix. Three `+` is a stall: the reader is carrying questions with no relief. A `−` immediately after the climax is the anticlimax; a `+` immediately before it is the stall before the drop. The map is coarse on purpose — it shows shape, and shape is what pacing is.

**Three repairs.**

- **Compress the approach.** Cut the failed attempts that repeat the same obstacle. Keep the one that escalates, and let the earlier ones be a line of summary or a single exchange.
- **Move the turn earlier.** Start the scene at the last attempt. What the reader loses in preparation they gain in pressure, and the earlier attempts can be recovered in dialogue later if they matter.
- **Insert a consequence.** When the sag is not delay but absence of risk, bring the consequence of the previous scene's choice into this one. The reader then has something at stake in the current scene without the scene needing to raise its voice.

**The rule that outranks the repairs:** slowness is not bad pace. A slow scene with a live question is paced; a fast scene with no question is dead. Correcting "slow" by adding events produces a busy text with the same hole in it.

## Scene rewriting

A protocol for the case where a scene must be replaced rather than patched. Six steps, in order.

1. **State the scene's job in one sentence.** "This scene makes the confrontation possible by costing Anna her sister's trust." If you cannot, the scene has no job and the rewrite has no target.
2. **Identify the load-bearing beat.** One moment the scene exists for. If you find two, the scene is two scenes and the rewrite should start by splitting it.
3. **Rewrite from the beat outward.** Write the beat first, in the voice and at the distance it needs. Then write the approach that makes it inevitable. Then the exit that lands on it. Not the reverse — an approach written first tends to produce a beat that the approach has already spent.
4. **Keep the best existing line.** Name one line, image, or gesture from the original that survives. If nothing survives, say so: this is a replacement, and the original should be kept in the file as source material.
5. **Compare on two axes only: state change and cost.** Did the new version change more, and cost more? If it changed less, the rewrite failed regardless of how good the prose is. Prose quality is not the measure of a rewrite; it is the measure of a line pass.
6. **Re-run the scene gate.** Entry and exit, information, voice, modal test. The rewrite is a new scene and gets no credit for the old one's virtues.

**The cost of rewriting.** A rewrite loses the accidental felicities of the draft — the odd detail that arrived by chance, the joke that worked because the sentence was clumsy. Before deleting, copy the original into a separate file. Most rewrites should harvest two or three things from the version they replace.

**When the scene has two jobs.** A scene asked to advance the plot and establish a relationship at equal weight will do neither cleanly, because each job wants a different rhythm. Give the scene the job the chapter cannot do without, and let the other happen as a by-product of the beat: the relationship is established by who is in the room when the plot moves, not by a second agenda running alongside it.

## The paraphrase-survival test

An instrument for deciding which layer a claim belongs to.

**Procedure.** Rewrite a paragraph in your own words, preserving the meaning: the same events, the same information, the same order of information, and nothing added. Then compare the two versions.

- **What survived is structure**: the causal links, the informational order, who wants what, what the change costs.
- **What disappeared is what the prose was doing with surface**: the rhythm, the assonance, the specific image, the syntactic suspense, the length of the sentence at the moment of the turn.

**The rule.** A defect that disappears under paraphrase was a surface defect. Demote it to [L1]–[L3] and repair it there — which may mean not repairing it at all, since surface choices are often deliberate. A virtue that disappears under paraphrase was surface too. That is not a demotion of the virtue: voice lives on the surface, and in some prose — comic timing, lyric compression — the surface *is* the point. The test tells you which layer a claim is about, not which layer matters.

`[hypothesis]` The test is derived from the fingerprinting study's paraphrase results: paraphrase costs the most where the signal was most lexical, and a structural residual survives it [data: Fingerprinting]. Our use of it as a layer-discriminator is an engineering inference, not a measured result.

**Worked example.**

*Original:*

> The rain had stopped by the time she reached the station, and the platform smelled of wet iron and cigarettes. She had not brought an umbrella. It did not matter now. The train was late, which gave her time to decide whether she was doing the right thing, and she decided, as she always did, by not deciding.

*Paraphrase, same events, same order, nothing added:*

> The rain stopped before she got to the station. The platform smelled of wet iron and cigarettes. She had no umbrella, but that no longer mattered. The train was late, so she had time to decide whether this was right, and she decided the way she usually did — by not deciding.

*What survived:* the sequence, the late train, the non-decision, and the causal link between the delay and the evasion. That is the structure, and it is sound: the scene has a mechanism.

*What disappeared:* the rhythm of "wet iron and cigarettes"; the fragment "It did not matter now"; and the parenthetical "as she always did", which in the original sits inside the sentence and makes the habit sound like an aside rather than a verdict.

*What follows:* the structural claim — "the turn is a non-decision and needs a cost attached" — survives, and can be acted on. The stylistic claim — "the prose is doing something with the fragment and the aside" — does not survive, so it is a surface claim; and because the surface is doing deliberate work here, it is not a defect at all. Two claims, two layers, one test.

**Two limits.** The test cannot distinguish a deliberate surface effect from an accidental one — that requires knowing the intention, which the paraphrase cannot supply. And it proves nothing about quality: a passage can survive paraphrase intact and still be bad, because structure can be well preserved and worthless.

## The revision plan format

One page. It is a working document, not a report.

```
# Revision plan — <work>, pass <n>

## Verdict
≤3 sentences: what it is trying to do, whether it does, the one thing in the way.

## Findings
### 1. <defect> · [L#] · <fatal | structural | local | cosmetic>
- Evidence: "<≤12 words>" (¶n)
- Mechanism: because …, the reader …
- Leverage: what else this repair changes
- Repair: the specific action
- Risk: what the repair endangers; what must survive it
- Order: 1

### 2. …

## Deferred
One line per defect found and not reported this pass, with the reason:
budget, dependency on finding 1, or sample too short.

## Not diagnosed
What this sample cannot support. Name the limit; do not fill it with a guess.
```

**Rules.**

- **≤2 structural, ≤3 local** per pass (`references/literary-critique.md` §5). A longer plan is a list, and lists do not get executed.
- **Order by leverage, not by page position.** Finding 1 should be the repair that makes findings 2 and 3 easier or unnecessary.
- **One defect per finding.** Two defects in one entry means the second will be skipped.
- **Risk is mandatory.** A repair with no stated risk is a rewrite in disguise.
- **Carry the deferred list forward.** Do not re-discover the same defects next pass; that is how a revision loop becomes a treadmill.
- **Renumber after every pass.** The plan is not an archive. Old passes go into the file's history, not into the current plan.
- **The plan is a hypothesis list.** If the writer reports that repair 1 changed nothing, re-diagnose instead of proceeding to repair 2. A plan executed in order after its first premise failed is worse than no plan.

# Ghostbuster — structured feature search, and what a detector can teach a critic

> Purpose: what Verma et al. actually measured about machine-written prose, and which of their methods transfer to multi-signal critique.
> Read when: you build or audit the Critique Engine's differential diagnosis, or a user asks the skill to "detect AI text".
> Links: `references/ai-narrative-signature.md` — the defect catalogue that inherits this paper's evidence discipline; `references/literary-critique.md` — quoting evidence instead of emitting a verdict; `methodologies/05-continuity-and-revision.md` — E14, where the search runs; `references/research/03-detectgpt.md` — the curvature approach this paper outperforms.

"Ghostbuster: Detecting Text Ghostwritten by Large Language Models." *Proceedings of the 2024 Conference of the North American Chapter of the Association for Computational Linguistics: Human Language Technologies (Volume 1: Long Papers)*, pp. 1702–1717, June 2024. Computer Science Division, UC Berkeley.

- System: ghostbuster.app · Code and experiment replication: github.com/vivek3141/ghostbuster
- Released artifact: three detection benchmarks (student essays, creative writing, news), plus a paragraph-level benchmark proposal (Appendix F) at github.com/vivek3141/ghostbuster-data

- Method class: supervised binary classification over features derived from per-token probabilities of *weaker* language models. No access to the target model's probabilities required.

## Research question

The paper asks a narrow engineering question with a broad constraint: can a document-level "was this written by a model?" classifier hold up when it is moved off the data it was trained on, without the token probabilities of the model that supposedly wrote the text?

Three pressures motivate the framing.

1. **Black-box targets.** Commercial models (ChatGPT, Claude) do not expose token probabilities. DetectGPT's curvature test needs a scoring model close to the generating model and "is known to perform poorly when the scoring and target models differ" (§5). A detector that only reads the text and a set of cheap surrogate models is deployable; one that needs the generator is not.
2. **Brittleness under distribution shift.** The authors state that existing frameworks "falter on datasets that they were not originally evaluated on" (§1) and cite Sadasivan et al. on paraphrase attacks. They treat generalisation — across domains, prompts, and models — as the actual object of study, not an afterthought.
3. **Harm from false positives.** Students whose genuine work is flagged, and non-native English writers who previous work found "disproportionately flagged as AI-generated" (§1, citing Liang et al. 2023). This is why the paper runs precision-only evaluations on human text, where accuracy equals precision.

Why black-box detection matters at all, in the authors' own terms: authenticity of student work, and trust in informative text when models hallucinate. Both are institutional problems, not literary ones. Hold that distinction — it is the first thing that breaks when the method is carried into a craft skill.

## Methodology

### Task

Document-level binary classification of paired documents: one human-authored, one model-generated from the same prompt (or from a prompt reconstructed from the human text). Paragraph-level detection is discussed as a harder benchmark but is not the modeling target; the authors concede that below roughly 100 tokens "it is difficult to determine whether individual paragraphs of documents were generated by language models" (§7.4).

### Feature extractors: four weak models, not one

Each document is converted into per-token probability vectors under four models, all weaker than the target:

| Extractor | What it is |
|---|---|
| Unigram fertility model | Per-token unigram probabilities over the GPT-3 tokenizer vocabulary, trained on Brown Corpus counts, no UNKing |
| Kneser-Ney trigram | Discount δ = 0.9, backing off to bigram and then to the unigram model; same vocabulary and corpus |
| GPT-3 `ada` | Early GPT-3, no instruction tuning |
| GPT-3 `davinci` | Early GPT-3, no instruction tuning |

The point of the ladder is that no single extractor defines "machine-like". A unigram model knows only lexical frequency; davinci approximates the target's own distribution. The discriminating signal lives in the *relations* between them. Note the count: four extractors. A summary that says "three weak models" is wrong.

### Feature families: 13 operations

The paper defines 13 vector and scalar operations over probability vectors (Table 1). Vector functions take two vectors p₁, p₂ ∈ ℝⁿ and return one vector; scalar functions collapse a vector to a number. Here n is the number of tokens in the document, and T_p is the set of indices holding the 25 *lowest* values of p.

Vector functions:

- `add` = p₁ + p₂
- `sub` = p₁ − p₂
- `mul` = p₁ · p₂ (elementwise)
- `div` = p₁ / p₂ (elementwise)
- `gt` = 1{p₁ᵢ > p₂ᵢ} (indicator vector)
- `lt` = 1{p₁ᵢ < p₂ᵢ} (indicator vector)

Scalar functions:

- `max` = max p
- `min` = min p
- `avg` = (1/|p|) Σ pᵢ
- `avg-top25` = (1/|p|) Σ_{i∈T_p} pᵢ — average of the 25 least probable tokens, i.e. a tail measure
- `len` = |p|
- `L2` = ‖p‖₂
- `var` = (1/n) Σ (pᵢ − μ_p)²

Each scalar is a summary statistic over the whole document: mean predictability, worst-case predictability, spread, length, norm. Each vector op is a *comparison between two models' judgments of the same token*: where does davinci find the token more likely than ada, where does the trigram disagree with both. Nothing here reads style. Everything reads the geometry of probability assigned by surrogates.

### Structured search

Features are generated recursively (Algorithm 1, `FIND-ALL-FEATURES`): from a current feature p, apply every scalar function; and for every other probability vector p′ and every vector function f_v, recurse on f_v(p, p′) with depth + 1. The search runs four times, once from each extractor's vector, with `max_depth = 3`.

A resulting feature therefore combines three probability vectors through vector functions and reduces the result to a scalar. The paper's own example: `var(unigram_probs > ada_probs - davinci_probs)`. Selected features per domain (Figure 5) look like `avg(ada > davinci / trigram)`, `var(unigram > ada - davinci)`, `max(ada / unigram + ada)` — all cross-model contrasts.

Search-space size is reported precisely: commutativity pruning removes about two thirds of the candidates; at depth 3 there are **2,534** selectable features, at depth 2 there are **322** (Appendix C). Selection is greedy forward selection, run separately per dataset, with features "chosen through validation" (App. E). The paper does not name the objective metric being optimized during selection, and does not state how many features the final classifier retains. Treat both as unreported.

### Classifier

Logistic regression, L2 regularization, C = 1, over the selected probability features plus seven handcrafted features (Appendix D):

- number of outlier tokens (pᵢ > 10), average of the 25 largest and of the 25th–50th largest token probabilities;
- the same two averages computed over the vector `davinci − ada`;
- average length of the 25 longest and of the 25th–50th longest words, measured in tokens.

The handcrafted block encodes hunches about machine prose (odd word lengths, uniformly high-confidence tokens). It matters less than the search: see the ablations.

### Datasets

Three new benchmarks, each 7,000 documents per Table 5.

| Domain | Human source | Human | ChatGPT | Claude | Median words (human / ChatGPT / Claude) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Student essays | IvyPanda, high-school and university | 1,000 | 5,000 | 1,000 | 529 / 559 / 442 |
| News | Reuters 50-50 (5,000 articles, 50 journalists) | 1,000 | 5,000 | 1,000 | 498 / 510 / 384 |
| Creative writing | r/WritingPrompts | 1,000 | 5,000 | 1,000 | 455 / 512 / 384 |

Construction details that matter for interpreting the numbers:

- All training data was generated with **gpt-3.5-turbo**; Claude data was evaluation-only.
- Creative writing: top 50 posters in October 2022, last 100 posts each — chosen to predate ChatGPT's public release (30 November 2022) and avoid contamination.
- News and essays had no ground-truth prompts, so ChatGPT first generated a headline or prompt from the human text, then generated the document from that. The paired AI text is therefore conditioned on a summary of the human text, not on the writer's actual intent.
- Length was matched approximately (human word count rounded to the nearest 100) to remove length as a free cue.
- Per domain, the 1,000 same-prompt ChatGPT documents plus the 1,000 human documents were split into train/validation/test; the remaining 4,000 ChatGPT documents (different prompts) and the 1,000 Claude documents were held out for generalisation only. Exact split proportions are not stated in the text available here.

### Baselines and protocol

- **Perplexity only** — linear classifier on document perplexity under davinci; one learned threshold.
- **DetectGPT** — unsupervised perturbation/curvature method, GPT-2 XL as scoring model, per Mitchell et al.
- **GPTZero** — commercial mixture (supervised training, perplexity, perplexity variance, internet search).
- **RoBERTa** — `roberta-large` with a logistic-regression head, fine-tuned with early stopping.
- **Humans** — 6 undergraduate/PhD students with prior experience using text generators, 50 documents each; plus 233 volunteers through a web interface, of whom 17 made ≥ 25 guesses.

Metric is F1 for classification. On human-only sets (non-native-speaker data) the metric is accuracy, which "is equivalent to precision, since there is no corresponding AI-generated text" (Table 3). Oracle thresholding — matching the predicted-label distribution to the test set — was applied to DetectGPT and GPTZero for the prompt and model generalisation experiments, and deliberately not to Ghostbuster or the supervised baselines, nor to the precision-only evaluation (it "would trivially result in perfect accuracy", §5).

## Results

### In-domain

| Model | All domains | News | Creative writing | Student essays |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perplexity only | 81.5 | 82.2 | 84.1 | 92.1 |
| DetectGPT | 57.4 | 56.6 | 48.2 | 67.3 |
| GPTZero | 93.1 | 91.5 | 93.1 | 83.9 |
| RoBERTa | 98.1 | 99.4 | 97.6 | 97.4 |
| Ghostbuster | **99.0** | 99.5 | 98.4 | 99.5 |

99.0 F1 averaged across the three domains — 5.9 above GPTZero, the best prior system, and 41.6 above DetectGPT. The introduction quotes a 23.7 F1 average margin over DetectGPT and GPTZero taken together. The conclusion rounds to "over 98.4 F1 across all datasets". RoBERTa at 98.1 is close; the gap between the two opens under shift.

### Generalisation

| Shift | Ghostbuster | Best baseline | Margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Across domains (train on 2, test on 1) | 97.0 avg | GPTZero (best prior) | +7.5 F1 |
| Across prompting strategies (5 prompts/domain) | 99.5 | RoBERTa 97.4 | +2.1 F1 |
| Across models (Claude) | 92.2 | RoBERTa 87.8 | +4.4 F1 |

Out-of-domain per-domain figures: news 97.9, creative writing 95.3, student essays 97.7. RoBERTa collapses to 88.3 on news and 71.4 on essays out-of-domain, but **beats Ghostbuster on creative writing out-of-domain, 95.7 to 95.3**. That single cell is the most instructive number in the table: the advantage is not universal, and the paper says so.

The authors are explicit that the in-domain comparison is "potentially unfair since these datasets are out-of-domain for GPTZero and DetectGPT" (§6.2) — those two were never trained on this data, so their in-domain and out-of-domain scores are identical. They call the out-of-domain comparison "the fairest". Cite the +7.5, not the +41.6, when you want an honest generalisation claim.

Claude generalisation costs 6.8 F1 against Ghostbuster's own level, which the authors read as evidence that "generalisation to different models without training on data from them remains a more challenging task" (§6.4).

### Ablations

| Condition | All domains, in-domain | All domains, out-of-domain |
|---|---|---|
| Handcrafted features only | 80.5 | 75.8 (news) |
| Search depth 1 | 93.7 | 93.7 (news) / 81.3 (creative) |
| Search depth 2 | 98.3 | 95.9 / 95.2 / 93.1 |
| Search depth 3 (full) | 99.0 | 97.9 / 95.3 / 97.7 |
| Search depth 4 | 98.3 | 96.4 / 95.8 / 97.7 |
| Without ada and davinci | 88.2 | 70.1 / 78.5 / 75.5 |
| Without davinci | 98.8 | 97.3 / 90.3 / 91.9 |
| Without handcrafted features | 98.9 | 97.8 / 93.4 / 97.4 |
| With random features | 97.8 | 94.3 / 93.1 / 94.3 |

Three readings, all supported by the text:

- Depth matters up to 3 and then stops. "Conducting structured search with depth lower than 3 tends to underfit the data, whereas performance plateaus or degrades for search depths greater than 3" (§7.1). Two operations are enough for 98.3; the third buys 0.7.
- The search itself is the main engine. Dropping it for handcrafted features costs 15.9 to 22.1 F1. Random features still reach 97.8, so most of the gain is "some probability feature, well-chosen" rather than "these exact features".
- A neural surrogate is needed for generalisation. Removing ada and davinci costs up to 10.8 F1 in-domain and 27.8 out-of-domain. Removing only davinci costs 0.6 to 5.8 out-of-domain — the authors' reading is that a surrogate closer in quality to the target improves transfer.

### Robustness

- Local perturbations (character flips, added spaces, case changes, adjacent-word swaps, synonym replacement via WordNet): performance degrades smoothly; "numerous edits are typically required to produce a false negative" (§7.2).
- Global perturbations: swapping adjacent sentences or paragraphs has a negligible effect. Repeated paraphrase degrades results; heavy paraphrase is the real attack surface.
- Commercial evader Undetectable AI: recall fell from 99% to 62% on 100 documents per domain — the site still fools the detector "less than half of the time" (§7.2).
- Length (Figure 4): performance improves with length; "may be unreliable for documents with ≤ 100 tokens", plateaus at ≥ 500.

### Non-native English speakers

Two of the three human-only sets are fine, the third is not.

| Model | Lang8 (acc) | TOEFL 11 (acc) | 91 TOEFL essays (acc) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Perplexity only | 98.6 | 98.1 | 13.2 |
| DetectGPT | 98.6 | 100.0 | 63.7 |
| GPTZero | 99.2 | 100.0 | 92.3 |
| RoBERTa | 98.6 | 98.1 | 96.7 |
| Ghostbuster | 95.5 | 99.9 | **74.7** |

Ghostbuster is the *weakest* of the five on the 91 TOEFL essays and second-weakest on Lang8. The authors attribute most of this to length — median 104 and 77 words, against 315 for TOEFL 11 and above 350 for everything else — and note that out-of-domain performance at 100 words ranged 75.6–93.1 F1, close to the 74.7 observed. Length explains much; the paper says "largely, though not entirely" (§7.3). Either way, a claim that this method is safe for non-native writers is not supported by its own numbers.

### Human baseline

Average human accuracy 59% (range 34–80%) on 50-document sets; 58.1 ± 11.1% for the 17 volunteers with ≥ 25 guesses (range 39.7–82.0%). The task is hard for people. This cuts both ways: it validates dataset difficulty, and it means "a human can't tell" is not evidence that a text is bad.

## The three transferable principles

### (a) Structured search over combinations beats any single signal

**Shown.** One signal — perplexity — collapses under shift: 81.5 overall, 49.0 F1 on out-of-domain creative writing, and 13.2 accuracy on the 91 TOEFL essays, which is worse than chance on a balanced set. Handcrafted multi-feature intuition without search reaches 80.5. A search over combinations of four extractors' probabilities reaches 99.0 with 97.0 under domain shift. Depth 1 to depth 3 buys 5.3 F1 in-domain. The gain comes from *contrasts between models*, not from any single statistic: every selected feature in Figure 5 compares at least two models.

**Not shown.** That the selected features are causal or explanatory — selection is discriminative, and random features still score 97.8. That more search is better: depth 4 is flat or worse, and the paper attributes the plateau to overfitting rather than to exhausted signal. That the approach wins everywhere: RoBERTa beats it on out-of-domain creative writing. That the same structure would transfer to non-probability signals, where the 13 operations have no obvious analogue.

### (b) Interpretable, model-independent features allow explanation, not just a score

**Shown.** The feature space is deliberately restricted to a small set of "easily interpretable features" (§4.2). A feature like `var(unigram > ada - davinci)` can be stated in words: the variance, across tokens, of whether the unigram model rates a token above the davinci-minus-ada contrast. That is a sentence a human can argue with. The method also needs no probabilities from the target model, so the explanation does not depend on access the user lacks.

**Not shown — and this is the important gap.** The paper never produces a per-document explanation. It reports which features the classifier weights, not why *this* document was flagged. The conclusion lists exactly this as future work: detectors "could provide additional explanations for classification decisions, so that human users can use their own judgment" (§8). Interpretability here is a property of the feature vocabulary, not a delivered artifact. Anyone citing this paper for "explainable detection" is citing a design property and a hope, not a result.

### (c) Generalisation must be tested across domains, prompts, models, and edits

**Shown.** Four axes, each measured separately: domains (+7.5 F1 over the best baseline), prompts including an explicit "write like a high-school student" evasion prompt (+2.1), a different generator (Claude, +4.4, and a 6.8 drop for Ghostbuster itself), and nine perturbation families plus a commercial paraphraser (recall 99% → 62%). The ablation showing a 27.8 F1 out-of-domain loss without neural surrogates is a generalisation finding, not a performance finding.

**Not shown.** Generalisation to non-English text, to non-Standard varieties (the authors concede the data is "predominantly British and American English"), to human-edited machine text, to adversarial paraphrase (deferred to future work in §1), to paragraph level (unreliable under 100 tokens), or to generators released after the paper. Generalisation is demonstrated on a 2023-era slice, and the paper's own limitation section says the method's errors are most likely exactly where the shifts get large.

## Limitations

**The paper's own.**

- Datasets "are not representative of all writing styles or topics", and errors are "particularly likely for text that represents a distributional shift" from training (§9).
- Named failure zones: shorter text; distant domains (the example given is text messages); varieties of English beyond Standard American/British; non-English; non-native speakers; AI text edited or paraphrased by a human; human text paraphrased or adjusted by a model.
- Paragraph-level and mixed-authorship detection are unsolved at the token lengths involved.
- In-domain comparisons against unsupervised baselines are unfair; the authors say so and lean on out-of-domain numbers instead.
- The method's inputs are GPT-3-era surrogates. Nothing in the paper establishes behaviour against later generators.
- Ethical: "we strongly discourage incorporation of Ghostbuster into any systems that automatically penalize students or other writers" without human supervision (§9).

**Added here.**

- **Detection accuracy is not a quality measure.** The label is authorship, not merit. A 99.0 F1 detector says nothing about whether a text is good, bad, generic, or worth revising. Every number in this dossier is about provenance.
- **The feature search is supervised and needs labels we do not have.** Forward selection over 2,534 candidates with a validation set requires thousands of paired human/machine documents per domain. Narraith has none, and cannot manufacture them.
- **The creative-writing subset is one genre from one platform.** r/WritingPrompts is short prompt-driven genre fiction by a self-selected online community, collected in October 2022. Treat "creative writing" in Table 2 as that, not as literary fiction.
- **False positives carry real harm**, and the paper's numbers show the risk is not hypothetical: 95.5 accuracy on Lang8 means roughly one human document in twenty was flagged. A craft skill that says "this reads like AI" inherits that error rate with none of the calibration.
- **The pipeline is not reproducible inside a chat context.** Unigram/trigram counts over Brown, two GPT-3 checkpoints, 2,534-candidate greedy search, logistic regression. We can borrow the shape of the procedure and nothing else.

## What transfers to Narraith, and what does not

**Transfers: the shape of the reasoning, not the machinery.**

1. **Multi-signal diagnosis with explicit evidence instead of a verdict.** Ghostbuster never asks "does this feel machine-written?" It computes several distinct measurements and asks which combination separates the classes. Narraith's equivalent: never "this scene is weak", always "this scene fails L5 because the turn is caused by an arriving letter rather than by a choice, evidence: ¶3 last sentence; the same defect appears at ¶7". The measurement differs; the discipline is identical.
2. **A structured search over candidate explanations.** The deepest transferable idea. Ghostbuster enumerates 2,534 combinations rather than accepting the first plausible feature, then keeps a small set that survives validation. Narraith's critique should enumerate candidate *defects* — agency failure, information leak, unmotivated turn, flat scene, false ambiguity — and then ask which minimal combination explains all the observed symptoms, rather than listing every defect the checklist can name.
3. **Ablation thinking: does the diagnosis survive removal?** Ghostbuster checks whether performance survives dropping davinci, dropping handcrafted features, restricting depth. The critique analogue: if deleting the quoted sentence removes the defect, the defect was local and fixable; if it persists, it is structural. And if the diagnosis evaporates when the passage is paraphrased, you diagnosed the surface, not the cause.
4. **Shift testing.** The paper's four axes map onto four questions a critique should answer before it is trusted: does this judgment hold in another genre, under another narration, for another reader, after one edit pass? A defect that exists only under one framing is a preference.
5. **Length discipline.** "Unreliable below 100 tokens" becomes: do not diagnose structure from a fragment. Narraith should refuse L5–L7 verdicts on passages too short to contain a turn, and say so.

**Does not transfer.**

- **We cannot claim detection, and must not imply it.** No logprobs, no surrogate ladder, no classifier. Saying "this is AI-written" is a factual claim we cannot support and a harm the source paper explicitly warns against.
- **No labelled corpus.** Supervised feature selection is unavailable; any "search" we run is over qualitative hypotheses, scored by argument, not by F1.
- **No F1 as evidence.** A detector's 99.0 does not show that a critique method improves writing. Our evidence for the skill has to come from judged revisions, not from a borrowed classifier score.
- **No feature transplant.** `var(unigram > ada − davinci)` has no prose-level reading. Translating selected features into rules like "avoid short sentences" or "avoid high-frequency words" is invention, and the paper's random-feature ablation (97.8) shows such translations would not even be faithful to the mechanism.

## Engineering hypotheses

1. `[hypothesis]` A differential-diagnosis critique that generates candidate defects and then tests them against quoted evidence will produce higher agreement between two independent judges on *which* defect is present than a flat checklist pass over the same texts. Falsified if agreement is equal or lower on a 30-text sample.
2. `[hypothesis]` Findings anchored in a quoted span survive a paraphrase of that span better than findings stated as impressions: after paraphrasing, a second pass will relocate ≥ 70% of quoted findings and < 40% of impressionistic ones. Falsified by the reverse ordering.
3. `[hypothesis]` Requiring two independent signals before asserting an L5 structural defect will cut unfounded structural critiques by half while cutting true ones by less than a quarter. Falsified if recall falls proportionally.
4. `[hypothesis]` Critique quality degrades sharply below ~150 words of source text, mirroring the paper's 100-token floor. Falsified if short-passage diagnoses are judged as accurate as long-passage ones.
5. `[hypothesis]` A revision plan capped at three ranked defects yields higher uptake in the revised draft than an exhaustive ten-item list, measured as the fraction of named defects actually fixed. Falsified if the exhaustive list produces more fixes per defect named.
6. `[hypothesis]` Diagnoses that name the genre frame before judging will be judged more accurate on genre fiction than frame-free diagnoses, because several "defects" are genre conventions. Falsified if frame-free scores equal or higher.

## Misapplication risks

1. **Reading the score as a verdict on quality.** The classifier separates provenance, not merit. A text can be machine-typical and good; human-typical and bad. Any sentence in Narraith that fuses "reads like AI" with "is weak" commits this error.
2. **Borrowing the F1 as validation.** "99.0 F1" is evidence about a 2024 detector on three 2022–2023 datasets. It is not evidence that a critique method, a revision plan, or this skill improves prose. Quoting it in a README as support for the skill is a category error the paper's own framing would reject.
3. **Translating features into style rules.** The selected features are cross-model probability contrasts. Turning them into "avoid adverbs", "vary sentence length", "cut em-dashes" produces a blacklist with no mechanism — exactly what the brief forbids — and is not what the paper measured.
4. **Treating selected features as causes.** Forward selection keeps what discriminates, and random features still reach 97.8 F1. The chosen set is one survivor of a search, not an explanation of why machine text differs.
5. **Accusing the user's text.** Running any "AI-ness" judgment on a writer's draft, especially a non-native writer's, reproduces the harm the paper documents — 95.5% accuracy on Lang8 means real false accusations — without the human-supervision safeguard the authors demand.

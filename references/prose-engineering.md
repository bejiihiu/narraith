## The unit of prose is the sentence, the unit of effect is the paragraph

A sentence can be correct on its own and wrong in its place. The paragraph is the smallest span a reader holds as one thing — they cannot keep eight sentences of qualification in mind, so they hold the shape, the emphasis, and the last word. That makes the paragraph the smallest unit you can revise as a whole and the only unit in which rhythm, information order, and emphasis can be judged together.

Two consequences, both practical:

- **Revise paragraphs, not sentences.** A sentence-level pass produces locally elegant sentences that do not add up, because each one was optimised against nothing. Mark the paragraph boundary first, state what the paragraph is for in four words, then judge every sentence against that.
- **A paragraph with two jobs is a paragraph with none.** If you cannot name the job, the paragraph is a transit zone between two places the reader needed to be. Merge it, cut it, or split it and give each half a job.

The sentence is still where the work happens. It carries one move — one assertion, one perception, one turn — and a sentence that carries two moves is usually a sentence that will be reread. When a sentence needs to carry two, the second one gets a colon, a semicolon, or its own sentence. Punctuation is a budget for how many moves a reader will accept in one breath.

## Rhythm

Reading is subvocalised: the reader hears durations even when they do not move their lips. Rhythm in prose is therefore not decoration but the channel through which the text says *this matters more than that*. A paragraph of sentences within three or four words of each other in length has no channel left: every beat is equally weighted, so nothing is emphasised, so the reader has no instruction about where to put attention. That is the mechanism behind the flatness of machine prose — not that the sentences are bad, but that they are interchangeable, and interchangeability is what a default produces.

Sentence length is not the same as stress count. Read for stresses, not words: *He bought the house* is four words, two stresses, four syllables. *He completed the acquisition of the property* is seven words, three stresses, fourteen syllables — the words are short on stresses and long on unstressed filler, so the line drags while carrying less. Vary by ear, then count words only as a check.

### The four functions

Every sentence in a paragraph is doing one of four jobs. Name the job before you judge the sentence.

| Function | Typical shape | What it must do | Failure when miscast |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carry** | 15–35 words, one main clause plus one or two subordinates, linear | Move information the reader needs to reach the next beat | Becomes a list of equal facts; the reader cannot tell which fact is load-bearing |
| **Land** | 2–8 words, often a fragment or a single clause | Change something: a verdict, a refusal, an arrival, a stop | Lands on nothing new, so it reads as a pause rather than a blow |
| **Turn** | 10–25 words, usually with *but*, *until*, *except*, or a negation placed late | Reverse the direction the paragraph was travelling | The turn is announced ("however") rather than performed by the content |
| **Dwell** | 30–70 words, subordinate clauses, appositives, a delayed main verb | Slow the reader down inside one perception, one memory, one decision | Dwells on material the scene has not made worth dwelling on |

Two rules follow, and they are the whole of applied rhythm:

- **A long sentence must be doing something** — accumulating (adding instances until the weight is the point), qualifying (narrowing the claim as it goes), or delaying (withholding the main clause so the arrival lands). A long sentence that is merely long is a sentence with slack in it, and slack reads as an author who did not decide.
- **A short sentence must change something.** Length alone is not emphasis. A three-word sentence that restates what the reader already knew is a drumbeat with no drum.

### One paragraph, two distributions

Same content — a man returns to the house where his brother is dying — first in the default distribution, then revised.

**Default:**

*The taxi left him at the end of the lane. The gate had come off its hinges and leaned against the wall. The grass in the yard had grown past his knees. A dog came around the side of the house and stopped. It looked at him the way it looked at strangers.*

Five sentences, 10, 12, 11, 11, 10 words. Every one is subject-verb-object, every one is a separate observation, every one gets the same duration. The paragraph is an inventory: the reader is told that five things exist and given no reason to rank them. The dog is the most important thing in the scene and it arrives with the same weight as the gate.

**Revised** (same content, chosen distribution):

1. *Everything after the gate he had to do on foot, through grass that came past his knees and wet his trousers to the shin, past the drum where his mother had burned the letters, so that by the time the dog came round the corner of the house he was breathing like a man who had been running.*
2. *The dog stopped.*
3. *It had known him eleven years.*
4. *It did not come closer.*
5. *It looked at him the way it looked at the meter man.*

**Annotation.**

| # | Function | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dwell, 58 words | Accumulates (grass, drum, letters) and delays the dog to the end of the sentence, so the animal arrives *after* the reader has walked the yard. The delay is characterisation: he is not hurrying, though his brother is dying. |
| 2 | Land, 3 words | The event. Length is the emphasis: three words against fifty-eight. |
| 3 | Turn, 5 words | States a fact that makes the next sentence a reversal. Without it, sentence 4 is just a dog being cautious. |
| 4 | Land, 5 words | The change: recognition refused. |
| 5 | Carry-and-land, 13 words | The comparison does the work an adjective would have done badly ("unrecognising"), and it ends on *meter man* — the reader carries "stranger who has a right to be here" into the next paragraph. |

Two things were added and one removed. Added: a causal chain (the walk produces the breathing produces the dog's hesitation) and a fact (eleven years) that gives the refusal a cost. Removed: the gate as a separate beat — it is now a prepositional phrase, because the gate is not the news.

The revision is not "better writing". It is the same writing with a decision in it: the dog is the event, so the paragraph is built to deliver the dog.

## Information structure inside the sentence

Three principles, all from the same fact — the reader integrates a sentence by attaching new material to material already held.

- **Given before new.** Open with what the reader already has (a person, an object, the previous sentence's topic), close with what they do not. A sentence that opens with new information forces the reader to hold it while reading the rest, and they will drop it.
- **End-weight.** The heaviest constituent — the long noun phrase, the clause with the qualification — goes last, because the end of the sentence is where the reader has the most capacity and where the memory is freshest.
- **End-focus.** The last stressed word is the one the reader carries into the next sentence. Whatever you put there is, functionally, what the sentence was about.

The three can conflict, and when they do, focus beats weight: a short, light word in final position outranks a long clause before it. *What she could not forgive was the letter* ends on the object of the grudge; *She could not forgive the letter he had written and then not sent* ends on the act of not sending, which is a different sentence.

**The test.** Cover the last three words. If the sentence still means the same thing and loses nothing, the ending is dead weight — the payload is somewhere in the middle, and you have spent your strongest position on a prepositional phrase.

**Focus is a choice about the question.** One fact, three sentences, three questions answered:

- *The letter was from her mother.* — answers *who was it from?*; *her mother* is the news.
- *Her mother's letter came on the Tuesday.* — answers *when?*; *the Tuesday* is the news.
- *On the Tuesday, her mother's letter came.* — answers *what happened?*; the arrival is the news.

None is more correct. Each commits the paragraph to a different next sentence. Write the sentence whose final word is the thing you want the reader to be holding when they start the next one.

**The paragraph's last word is the reader's carry-over.** This is the cheapest structural instrument in prose and the least used: end a paragraph on the object, image, or word you want live in the reader's hand as they cross the white space. If the paragraph ends on *and then she went to bed*, the reader carries bedtime; if it ends on *the second fold in the envelope*, the reader carries the fact that someone opened it before her.

**A pair, same content.** The information is: the workshop has been sold; the buyer is a man named Fyodor; the narrator's father does not know yet.

*There were a number of reasons why the sale of the workshop, which had been in the family for three generations and which her father still spoke of as though it were operating, had been handled quietly and through a lawyer in the regional capital, and one of those reasons was the identity of the buyer.*

One sentence, 56 words, and the payload — the buyer's identity — never arrives, because the sentence spent its whole budget apologising for its own length. The reader reaches the full stop holding a promise.

*Her father still talked about the workshop as though it were open. It had been sold in March, through a lawyer in the regional capital, to a man called Fyodor.*

Two sentences. The given (the father's belief) comes first; the new (the sale, the date, the buyer) arrives in the order the reader needs it; the paragraph ends on the name, which is the sting and the carry-over into the next paragraph. Nothing was added; the material was ordered.

**Russian and English distribute this differently.** English has articles, a fairly fixed order, and strong sentence stress, so it can put new information last almost mechanically and can use cleft constructions (*it was the dog that…*) when it cannot. Russian has no articles and free word order, so the final position is the focus position by default and moving a word there *is* the emphasis — which means an English-shaped sentence order in Russian reads as unmarked where the writer intended stress, and vice versa. Do not translate the distribution. `references/russian-prose.md` §2 is the operational version.

## Paragraph shape

A paragraph is a unit of attention, not a unit of length. It has four positions, and a paragraph that omits one of them usually feels either abrupt or slack:

- **Entry** — the first sentence, which sets the contract. It tells the reader what kind of attention is required (a scene? a summary? an argument?) and whose terms the paragraph will use.
- **Development** — the middle, which delivers what the entry promised.
- **Turn** — the point at which the paragraph knows something the entry did not. Not every paragraph needs a reversal, but a paragraph with no development past its entry is a paragraph that has not earned its length.
- **Exit** — the last sentence, which pays the contract and hands the reader a carry-over.

**The commonest structural fault is an entry that lies about the paragraph.** A paragraph that opens with a general statement and then becomes a scene makes the reader read the first sentence as summary, then re-read it as scene-setting when the dialogue starts. The cost is a stumble the reader cannot name. Fix by making the entry announce the mode: a time, an action, a voice, or a colon.

### Four paragraph types

| Type | Job | Time | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scene** | Play a moment at near-real speed; bodies in a space, speech, action | Story time ≈ text time | Scene inflation: a scene that changes nothing because "scene" was mistaken for "good" |
| **Summary** | Cross ground; deliver information the reader needs to make the next scene legible | Story time ≫ text time | Summary used where the reader needed to be present; the draft reports the story instead of playing it |
| **Thought** | Render a mind working; interiority, deliberation, memory | Subjective, often dilated | Thought that restates the scene's meaning instead of changing the character's position |
| **Description** | Fix a place or an object in the reader's attention | Paused | Inventory: objects listed in the order they occur to the author, not the observer |

**Do not mix types without a seam.** A paragraph that begins in summary and slides into scene without a marker forces the reader to re-derive the mode mid-sentence. Seams are cheap and visible: a line break, a change of tense, a colon, a named time (*By six she was in the yard*), or a shift into direct speech. A seam the reader can see is not an interruption; an invisible seam is.

**The one-sentence paragraph is a tic when it is a habit.** It works by contrast — a single sentence between two long ones gets the weight of a paragraph break. Use it where the content deserves a paragraph's worth of silence, which is roughly once or twice a scene. Used every third paragraph, it stops being emphasis and becomes layout: the prose equivalent of putting every other word in bold. The diagnostic is mechanical — count them. More than one per page of continuous prose, and the emphasis has been spent.

**One paragraph, all four positions, annotated.** A woman is waiting in a room where her husband is being interviewed by a committee.

*They had given her a chair in the corridor and told her someone would come. The corridor was the kind with windows only at one end, and the light reached about as far as her shoes. She had counted the doors twice. Nobody had said what time the interview took, which she understood now to mean it took as long as they wanted it to. Across the corridor a man in a cleaning tabard was eating bread from a bag, and the bag was a good one, folded and clipped, and he ate with his back to her.*

- **Entry:** a fact and a promise of procedure. The contract is that the paragraph will wait with her.
- **Development:** two details, both about duration, neither about the committee.
- **Turn:** a deduction rather than an event — it changes her position from passive to comprehending, and it is the only sentence in the paragraph that makes a claim.
- **Exit:** an object that is not the committee. The carry-over is the question of who in this building has a right to be here.

The middle two sentences are where a weaker version would have put a description of the committee room, which she has not seen and cannot report.

**Seams in practice.** The four commonest markers, in order of how invisible they are: a change of tense (summary is past, scene can be past-into-present or past-into-something-faster), a colon (the colon is a promise that what follows is an instance), a named time (*By six*, *On the Thursday*), and a line break. The line break is the strongest and the most expensive; the colon is the weakest and the cheapest. If you find yourself writing *Meanwhile* or *And so*, the seam is probably missing and the word is a bandage.

## Concrete and abstract

Abstraction is the default failure at [L1] and [L4] for the same reason in both places: abstract nouns are the highest-probability continuation. They are safe (nobody can be wrong about *grief*), they summarise (which feels like progress), and they do not commit to an object that might be the wrong object. A model reaching for the next token finds *a sense of loss* before it finds *the second cup still on the table*, and the first is always available.

**The object test.** Can you photograph it, or point at it in a room? *Grief* — no. *The second cup still on the table* — yes, and the cup carries the grief without naming it. The test is not "use only physical nouns"; it is "if the sentence is about a state, find the object the state has arranged, and describe the arrangement".

Application, at three levels:

- *She was devastated by the news.* Nothing to photograph, nothing to infer; the reader is told the conclusion.
- *She read the letter twice and then went to the kitchen and put the kettle on and stood at the counter until it boiled dry.* The state is not named; the sequence is. Note that this is not "showing" in the somatic sense — there is no trembling hand — it is behaviour with an outcome, and the outcome (the dry kettle) is the measurement.
- *She read the letter twice. The kettle boiled dry.* Same events, different distribution: the second version makes the dry kettle the event, which is a claim about what the scene is about.

**Specificity is not trivia.** A detail earns its place if it is **load-bearing** (it changes what the reader can infer), **characterising** (it tells you who would notice it), or both. A detail that is neither is trivia, and trivia signals that the writer was guessing.

*He was tall, with brown hair, a scar above his left eyebrow, a habit of clearing his throat, and a fondness for cardamom.* Five details, none load-bearing and none characterising: any of them could be swapped for any other and the character would be unchanged. Now: *He cleared his throat before he said his wife's name, and only before her name.* One habit, and it is a fact about the marriage.

**The one true detail rule.** One precisely right detail outperforms four plausible ones, because four plausible ones are visibly a sample from a distribution — the reader feels the writer reaching — while one exact detail is a report from a world. When you have four, keep the one that costs something to know, and let the other three go. If you cannot tell which one costs something, none of them do yet.

## Image and metaphor

A metaphor has two parts: the **tenor** (what is being described) and the **vehicle** (what it is compared to). Craft attention goes almost entirely to the vehicle, because that is the visible choice; but the tenor decides whether the comparison is doing anything. *Her marriage was a prison* has a tenor with no specificity — whose marriage, what kind of prison, for whom — so no vehicle can rescue it.

**The dead-simile test: does the vehicle belong to the character's world?** A comparison is alive when the thing compared to is something this observer could plausibly have in their head — from their work, their town, their reading, their body. *Her fear was like a cold hand* fails: nobody's hand, no temperature, no owner. *Her fear was like the moment before the inspector reads the meter* works for a character who has been hiding something from an inspector, and for nobody else.

**A metaphor must be earned by the scene's material.** The strongest version of the test: the vehicle should already be present in the scene, so importing it costs nothing and the comparison feels discovered rather than applied. If the scene is in a bakery, the comparisons available are flour, heat, proving, ovens, the sound of the door. A comparison to a cathedral in a bakery scene has to be paid for, and the payment is usually a sentence of justification the scene did not want to spend.

**Extended metaphor becomes an obligation.** Once you commit to a vehicle, the reader tracks it. Return to it and it must *develop* — the second return must know something the first did not, or it is repetition with extra words. Three returns is the practical maximum before the metaphor is running the paragraph instead of serving it. If you cannot say what changes between return one and return three, collapse it to one.

**Metonymy is cheaper and often better.** Instead of asserting a resemblance, name the associated thing and let the association carry the meaning: *The ring came off before the papers were signed.* No comparison is made, nothing is claimed about what a marriage is like, and the reader does the work. Metonymy's advantage is that it does not ask the reader to accept a proposition; it asks them to notice a fact. That is a lower price and usually a better trade, especially at [L4], where the failure is meaning stated rather than built.

### Three images

**Dead.** *The silence between them was a wall.*

The vehicle is the first thing anyone reaches for, which means it has been used on every silence in print. It asserts nothing checkable, belongs to no observer, and can be moved into any story about any two people unchanged. Portability is the tell: an image you could relocate is not an image of this scene.

**Competent.** *The silence between them had the quality of a room where the furniture has been covered.*

Specific, visual, faintly uncanny; it does perceptual work the word *silence* cannot, and it implies a household in abeyance. Its limit is that it is still an observation from outside: it could belong to any couple in any hiatus, and it tells us nothing about who is noticing or what they stand to lose.

**Good.** *The silence between them was the silence of two people reading the same letter and pretending not to.*

This one imports an event. It presupposes a letter, a shared object, a deception, and a prior scene in which the letter arrived — so it can only exist here. It also carries a stake: the pretence is a choice someone is making. The cost is that it needs the letter to exist; used in a story without one, it is a lie about the material.

The difference between the three is not elegance. It is how much of the specific world each one requires in order to be true.

**When not to use a metaphor at all.** Comparison is an interruption: it stops the scene to make a claim about it. That is worth it when the comparison changes what the reader understands, and not worth it when it only confirms what the sentence already said. Three cases where the right choice is none:

- **The action is the meaning.** *He put the key under the mat and walked to the station.* A metaphor here would tell the reader how to feel about a departure that is already doing its work. Transparent prose is not a failure of imagination; it is a decision that the event is strong enough to carry itself.
- **The register is a report.** A witness statement, a log, a doctor's notes — the absence of figuration *is* the voice, and one simile breaks it.
- **The vehicle would be doing the author's work, not the narrator's.** If the comparison comes from the writer's reading rather than the character's life, it is a visiting ornament, and readers register it as the author leaning in.

**Mixed and semi-mixed vehicles.** *The argument was a house of cards that had run out of steam* fails because the reader is asked to hold two physical models at once, and the second cancels the first. The subtler fault is the vehicle that is coherent but *not finished*: a comparison that promises a mechanism it never delivers — a machine introduced for one clause and never used again. If the vehicle has parts, at least one of them must do something.

## Description and attention

Description is not a record of a room. It is a record of what a particular consciousness notices, in the order it notices it, given what that consciousness wants and what it is doing in the room. That definition does the work that "be vivid" cannot: it makes every object in a description answerable to a perceiver.

**The POV-filter rule.** Every noun in a description must pass through three questions: would this observer notice it, would they name it this way, and would they notice it *now*, given what they are in the middle of? A plumber entering a flooded kitchen notices the stopcock, the water line on the cabinet, the smell; they do not notice the colour of the tiles unless the tiles are the reason the water is where it is. The same room through a buyer's eyes is a list of costs.

**How to describe without inventory.** Four working methods:

- **Use the room.** Let the character act in it and let the objects appear as obstacles, tools, or evidence. The reader gets the space from the friction.
- **Occlude.** Describe what the observer's body blocks, or what they must move to see. Attention has a geometry, and partial views are more real than panoramas.
- **Describe a change.** A room described twice — before and after, or as the light moves — carries time, and time is what an inventory lacks.
- **Describe by refusal.** What the observer will not look at is often the strongest description available, because it is characterisation and setting in one move.

**Ninety words, and a diagnosis.**

*The bench was where he left it, under the window, and the window was still taped at the corner where the draught came in. She did not look at the tools. She looked at the floor around the bench, at the pale shavings under it, and at the way the shavings stopped in a line, as if someone had swept up to a point and then been interrupted. The drawer was open two inches. She could see the corner of a brown envelope, and the corner had been bent back and smoothed flat again.*

**Diagnosis.** The attention is forensic and retrospective, and it is an attention, not a camera. She registers *baseline deviations*: the tape is *still* there (she knows the room well enough to have a before), the shavings *stop in a line* (a process interrupted, not a mess), the envelope was opened and re-closed (someone covered a trace). She does not look at the tools — an active avoidance, which tells us the tools are the dead man and she cannot afford him yet. There is no inventory: no list of chisels, no dimensions, no quality of light. The room is delivered entirely as evidence, which tells us she has come here to find something out and does not trust what she will find. Change one thing — let her notice the smell of linseed — and the observer changes from an investigator to a daughter, and the paragraph would need rewriting.

That is the test to apply to your own descriptions: name what the attention pattern reveals about the observer. If it reveals only that the author has a camera, the description is inventory with better vocabulary.

**How much description is enough.** Two questions, and both must be answered before you keep a descriptive block:

- **What decision does the reader make with this?** Description that changes what the next scene means — where the exits are, what the father's hands are doing, whether the money is on the table — is structural and can run long. Keep a descriptive block only if it answers a question the next two scenes ask: if it answers none, cut it to one sentence; if it answers one, cap it at a paragraph.
- **What is the observer doing while they look?** A character standing still to admire a room is a character the reader has left alone with the author. If the observer is walking, searching, waiting, hiding, or being watched, the description has a reason to stop and start, and that reason is what makes it feel *seen* instead of *supplied*.

## Sensory channels without the checklist

The five-senses checklist ("include sight, sound, smell, touch, taste") is a distribution imposed on material that has its own logic. Sensory balance is not a property of good description; it is a property of a form. A chase has almost no taste. A deathbed has almost no smell until it does, and then the smell is the whole scene. Write the channel the moment forces and skip the rest — a paragraph with one sense used exactly is stronger than four used decoratively.

`[data: StoryScope]` The measurement is worth knowing because it inverts an intuition: AI stories deploy somatic and olfactory imagery *more* than human ones. Emotion is conveyed through physical sensation and bodily metaphor in 81% of AI stories against 38% of human ones, and smell is a dominant modality in 82% of AI stories against 57% of human ones. The direction is what matters: the over-deployment runs toward the body and the nose, not away from them.

The implication is not "use fewer senses". It is that sensation is being used as a substitute for event. When a scene has nothing happening, a smell arrives to make it feel inhabited; when a character has no decision, the body performs the feeling instead. The proof is that the over-deployment is concentrated in the *least* default literary channel — smell is the sense prose uses least, so its frequency is a signature of a writer reaching for vividness rather than for information.

**Smell and touch are proximity senses.** You cannot smell something across a room; smell means you are close enough to be affected, which makes it an instrument of exposure and intrusion. Touch means contact, which means consent or its absence. That is why they carry more charge per word than sight: sight is the sense of the observer at a distance, and most narration is already at a distance. Use them where proximity is the point — the crowded train, the hospital, the embrace that should not be happening.

**The operational rule.** Every sensory detail answers a question the scene has already asked: *is the milk off? who was here? is he afraid? is she lying?* A detail that answers no question is atmosphere, and atmosphere is a legal choice — but it is a choice, and it costs pace. Name the question, then decide whether the detail is the cheapest way to answer it.

## Emotion on the page

Three channels, and the choice among them is a decision about scene function, not about virtue.

| Channel | What it does | Works when | Fails when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Labelled** | Names the state: *she was afraid* | The character is articulate about feeling, and the articulation characterises them; or the gap between label and behaviour is the irony | The label replaces the scene; the reader is told the conclusion they were supposed to reach |
| **Enacted** | Behaviour, speech, and the body: what the character does, says, and cannot help doing | The body is the site of the conflict — pain, exhaustion, desire, fear that overrides speech | It becomes the somatic reflex: tight chest, cold sweat, breath catching, for every emotion in the book |
| **Oblique** | Juxtaposition, displacement, omission: the text attends to something adjacent and lets the reader infer | The feeling is unspeakable, or the POV is defended, or the character will not look at it | Nothing is on the page to infer *from*; the reader gets absence, not pressure |

`[data: StoryScope]` The channels have very different base rates in the two populations: AI stories label emotion explicitly in 8% of cases, human stories in 29%. Combined with the 81% vs 38% embodiment figure in §8, the pattern is a near-total substitution — the model enacts because it has been told to show, and almost never names, because naming is what the rule forbids. That rule, applied as dogma, produces the machine signature.

**The calibration rule.** Choose the channel by what the scene needs the emotion to *do*:

- **Labelled** when the character's relationship to their own feeling is the subject — someone who can name it precisely is characterised by the precision, and someone who names it wrongly is characterised by the error. Also for comic or ironic distance, where the gap between the label and the visible behaviour is the joke.
- **Enacted** when the body is where the conflict is being fought, and speech would be a lie the scene cannot afford.
- **Oblique** when the feeling cannot be spoken by this person in this room, or when the POV's defence is the point: the narration looks at the coffee ring because looking at the coffee ring is what the character is doing instead.

**One beat, three ways.** A man is told in a meeting that his sister has died.

- **Labelled:** *He was not surprised, which was the worst of it, and he said thank you for telling me in the voice he used for clients.* The label (*not surprised*) is the event, and the voice is the evidence.
- **Enacted:** *He found he had stood up. His chair went back and hit the radiator, and he stood with his hand flat on the table where the report was, and the room waited.* The body acts before the mind authorises it; the room's waiting is the cost.
- **Oblique:** *The report had a coffee ring on page four. He turned to page five and asked whether the second column included the quarter they had written off, and someone said it did, and he said good, and they went on.* Nothing is named; the reader infers from the fact that a man has been handed a death and is discussing a write-off. The coffee ring is the only thing in the paragraph that is not business.

**The failure is stacking.** The characteristic bad version is all three at once, in descending order of abstraction: *She felt a wave of grief wash over her, her chest tightening, as if the world had stopped.* Label, soma, and gloss, each one telling the reader what the previous one already said. Pick one channel, and let the other two be absent. Absence is what gives the chosen channel its authority.

## Point of view and distance

POV is not a pronoun choice. It is a decision about what can be known, by whom, and at what distance — and each option buys something and costs something.

| Mode | Buys | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **First person** | Voice, immediacy, a built-in unreliability (the narrator is a character with interests) | Knowledge is bounded by one person's presence; the narrator must plausibly have learned everything they report |
| **Third close** | Access to one mind plus the narrator's freedom of phrasing; the standard vehicle for free indirect discourse | The boundary must be held; every sentence that knows too much breaks it |
| **Third omniscient** | Range, irony, the long view; the narrator can comment | Suspense is hard, because suspense needs a gap and omniscience closes gaps unless it withholds on purpose |
| **Second person** | Complicity; the reader is positioned, not invited | It reads as instruction or accusation, and it fails completely when the *you* has no specificity — a generic you is a horoscope |
| **External / camera** | The reader does the inferring; behaviourism as a deliberate opacity | Sustained for long, it starves the reader of the one thing prose does better than film: access |

**The distance ladder.** Gardner's psychic distance is a ladder of seven rungs, not a switch, and the rule is one rung at a time except at a section break or one deliberate shock. The rungs, the legal two-rung move, and the illegal jump are set out in `references/narrative-theory.md` §4; use that table rather than a summary of it.

**Head-hopping.** The pronouns can stay constant while the focalizer changes, and that is the version readers feel without being able to name. The three-question test in `references/narrative-theory.md` §3 locates the actual focalizer: whose sensory access limits the description, whose vocabulary appears in an evaluative word, and what the narration refuses to state. When the three answers disagree, you have hopped, whatever the grammar says.

**The camera fallacy.** "Cinematic" is often used to mean neutral, and there is no neutral sentence. A camera has no interests; a sentence always has a selector, because someone chose to put this noun before that verb. Trying to write without a selector does not produce objectivity, it produces the inventory — objects listed in the order they occur to the author, with the author's taste as the only organising principle and no observer's attention to justify it. If you want the camera effect, commit to it: an external focalization that reports only what a body in the room could see and hear, with the inferences left out. That is a decision. Listing is not.

**POV is a suspense machine.** Suspense is a gap: the reader knows something the character does not (dramatic irony), or the character knows something the reader does not (mystery), or both know something is coming and neither can stop it (suspense proper). Close third generates the first and third for free, because the reader is inside a mind that is about to be wrong. Omniscience generates none of them unless the narrator chooses to withhold, and a narrator who withholds must have a reason the reader will accept — which is why omniscient thrillers so often read flat: the mode has spent the gap before the first scene.

## Voice

Voice is not a style. It is a system of recurring choices that a particular narrator could not help making, and it is detectable because the choices cohere. Six components, and a voice needs a position on all six:

- **Attention** — what this narrator notices, and in what order. A narrator who notices hands, or money, or the exits, is characterised before they say anything.
- **Syntax** — the sentence shapes they default to: paratactic and clipped, or hypotactic and qualifying; fronted objects, tag questions, sentences that end early.
- **Lexicon** — the word-field they draw on. Not "fancy words" but *which* field: legal, agricultural, medical, liturgical, mechanical.
- **Figuration** — what they compare things to. This follows from lexicon and attention, and it is the fastest way to detect a voice that has been assembled rather than inhabited: the comparisons will come from the author's reading, not the narrator's life.
- **Attitude** — what they approve of, disdain, find funny, refuse to take seriously. Attitude is carried by evaluation, and evaluation is carried by single words: *predictably*, *at last*, *of course*.
- **Refusal** — what the narrator will not say. The negative space is the strongest evidence, because it cannot be faked by adding mannerisms: a narrator who never names their father, or never uses the word *home*, has a shape.

**Build a voice contract.** Six lines, and they belong in the decision ledger, not in the prose: POV and distance; time of narrating; register; the narrator's attitude to the story they are telling; three obligations (things this voice always does); three prohibitions (things it never does). `templates/decision-ledger.md` has the fields.

A filled example, for a first-person narrator who runs a ferry:

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| POV, distance | First person, subsequent, close; distance moves one rung only, at the section break |
| Time of narrating | Years later, from a different town; knows the ending and is not saying |
| Register | Spoken, paratactic, technical about water and engines, no literary allusion |
| Attitude | Contempt for land people, tenderness he would deny, suspicion of anyone who is grateful |
| Obligations | Always gives a time or a distance; always names a part before a person; always understates a loss |
| Prohibitions | Never uses an abstract noun for a feeling; never quotes anyone directly except his father; never explains a joke |

The prohibitions are the load-bearing part. Obligations make a voice recognisable; prohibitions make it a *person*, because a person is defined by what they will not do. A contract with six obligations and no prohibitions produces a narrator who can say anything, which is to say no narrator at all.

**Test it two ways.** First, the transplant: take a neutral paragraph of reported fact — a weather report, a police log — and rewrite it in the voice. If the result is indistinguishable from any competent literary narrator, the contract is not specific enough to constrain anything. Second, the blind sample: give a reader a hundred words of narration with the names removed and ask whose head they are in. If they cannot tell, the voice is a register, not a person.

**Voice is not quirks.** A quirk is a tic: the narrator always mentions birds, or uses one archaic word, or swears every third sentence. Quirks are detectable and portable — they can be added to any text, which is exactly why they are not voice. Voice changes what the text *notices* and what it *refuses*; a quirk changes only its surface. The test: remove the quirk and ask whether anything about the narrator's relation to the world has changed. If not, you removed decoration.

## Repetition

Repetition is structure when the second occurrence knows about the first, and an accident when it does not. That single test separates the two categories, and it is answerable by looking at the two instances side by side.

**Deliberate:**

- **Motif** — an object, gesture, or phrase that recurs with a shifting meaning. It must recur at least twice, and the valence must change between occurrences, or it is just an object mentioned twice. A wedding ring appearing in chapter one and chapter nine is continuity; the same ring appearing on a different hand is a motif.
- **Refrain** — a phrase repeated at intervals, usually at section ends, where the repetition measures how far the story has moved. The refrain's words stay the same; the context makes them mean something else. If the context has not changed, the refrain is a chorus, which is a different (and legitimate) device.
- **Anaphora** — the same opening repeated across successive clauses or sentences, to build pressure. It works at three; at two it is a pair, at four it is usually one too many unless the fourth varies the pattern.

**Accidental:** the same adjective twice on a page, the same verb of motion three times in a paragraph, the same sentence shape in consecutive sentences. These read as slips because the reader assumes the writer would have noticed. The mechanism is not that repetition is bad — it is that unintentional repetition signals inattention, and inattention is contagious: the reader stops trusting the details.

**The distinction is not frequency, it is legibility.** A word can appear five times in a page deliberately if each occurrence is *marked* — placed in the same syntactic position, or given the same surrounding words, so the pattern is visible as a pattern. The same word twice, unmarked, in different contexts, reads as a failure of vocabulary.

**Diagnostic.** List the content words that appear more than twice on a page, then classify each: is the repetition doing structural work (a motif, a refrain, a marked return), or is it the writer's ear failing? Cut or vary the second category. Do not cut the first — the strongest prose in a story is often a word that has been made to carry two meanings by being repeated at the right distance.

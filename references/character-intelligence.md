# Character Intelligence: Designing Pressure, Not Personality

> Purpose: build characters as systems of desire, resistance, self-deception and cost, so that behaviour produces inference instead of requiring explanation.
> Read when: you are casting a story, a character is described well but inert, a scene's turn depends on someone acting "in character", or dialogue reads as two mouths and one mind.

## The problem with "make them relatable"

Relatability is an effect. It is what a reader reports after the fact — *I felt for her, I knew her* — and like every reader-side report it is a measurement, not a lever. You cannot put relatability into a draft the way you put in a sentence. You can only put in the things that sometimes produce it, and then find out.

Designing for it directly produces three recognisable failures.

**The sympathy checklist.** The character is assembled from approved vulnerabilities: loves a pet, worries about money, is clumsy, was bullied. Each item is a claim on the reader's goodwill and none of them is a decision. The result reads as a defence brief for someone the reader has not met.

**The universal wound.** A backstory so general it could belong to anyone — the absent father, the lost child, the town that never accepted them. Universality is not specificity's shortcut; it is its opposite. A wound that half the population shares explains nothing about what this person does at four in the afternoon.

**The plea disguised as a trait.** *She just wants to be loved. He never learned how to ask for help.* These are not traits. They are theses about the character's need, stated in the character file and too often restated in the prose, and they convert the reader from a participant into an audience for a diagnosis.

Relatability also fails exactly where the interesting work lives. A torturer, a billionaire, a god, a nine-year-old, a woman who has just done something unforgivable — the sympathy route is closed and the writer is forced onto the real surface. That is not a coincidence. It is evidence that sympathy was never the mechanism.

What is actually on the design surface is four forces:

- **Desire** — what is pursued, and what is spent pursuing it.
- **Resistance** — what blocks the pursuit, and why the block is right from inside itself.
- **Self-deception** — the belief that makes the pursuit feel necessary rather than chosen.
- **Cost** — what is lost by acting, and what would be lost by succeeding.

A character built from those four is not necessarily likeable and does not need to be. The reader's experience is not *I like her* but *I need to know what she will do*, which is a stronger and more durable attachment, and the only one that survives a character doing something appalling.

Two versions of the same father, one sentence of each:

> He had always worked hard for his family, and though he never said it, he loved them more than anything.

> He came in at nine and put the pay packet on the table where she would see it, and then went out to the car to listen to the end of the match.

The first is a claim about the character. The second is a set of actions with a shape: he needs the payment witnessed, and he cannot stay in the room where it is witnessed. Nothing in the second sentence asks for sympathy, and it earns more of it.

Retire "sympathetic". Keep **legible** — the reader can reconstruct why this person did that — and **charged** — the reconstruction matters to them. Both are designable. Relatability is the residue.

## The three axes

Want, need, lie. Three sentences, each with a verb, each testable against a scene. The axes earn their place only if they are in tension: the want must be reachable only by obeying the lie, and the need reachable only by breaking it. Three true facts about a person are a description. Three forces pulling against each other are a character.

### Want

One clause, and blockable. *Get the boat through the inspection. Keep the shop until spring. Be the one who is called.*

- **In behaviour:** allocation under constraint. A real want takes resources from something else — hours, money, credit with other people, the last of a supply — and the transfer is visible. If nothing else in the character's life is being starved for it, the want is a hobby.
- **In dialogue:** return and variation. Count how often a speaker brings the conversation back to their own subject, and how they disguise the return. The want shows as repetition with a new approach each time.
- **Fails when stated:** "I want to be respected" is not a want. It cannot be blocked by a person in a room. It is a need wearing a want's clothes, and stating it turns the scene into an interview about the character's psychology.

### Need

What would repair them, invisible to them, frequently opposed to the want.

- **In behaviour:** the arrival test. The thing they need is offered — free, unattended, at no cost — and they decline it, or take it and cannot hold it. The refusal is the evidence. A need is never proven by the character wanting it; it is proven by what they do when it is handed over.
- **In dialogue:** the subject they cannot leave alone once the defences are down — drunk, furious, four in the morning, in a car. Not a confession. A return, to the same few sentences, in the same order.
- **Fails when stated:** it becomes therapy language, and the story becomes a treatment plan. Worse, if another character names the need accurately and the protagonist accepts the naming, the plot is over — the reader has been handed the ending as a diagnosis.

### Lie

A proposition, false in the world of the story, that makes the want feel necessary. *A thing is only yours if you can repair it yourself. Anyone who stays is being paid to stay. Being owed is the only safe form of being loved.*

- **In behaviour:** arrangement, not assertion. The character builds a life in which the belief is never falsified — chooses the job that confirms it, the partner who will not test it, the room with the door that locks. The lie is most visible in what they do **not** do: the letter not opened, the number not dialled, the question not asked when asking is free.
- **In dialogue:** the short, over-emphatic sentence that arrives when the character is defending something nobody attacked. It is usually a proverb, a family saying, or a joke they have made before.
- **Fails when stated:** two ways. The narrator states it and it becomes the theme. Another character states it and is right, and it becomes a diagnosis. Both remove the reader's work, and the work was the whole experience.

### A character in behaviour

> On the first cold morning Maren took the outboard apart on the slipway and laid the parts out in the order she had taken them off. Her brother came down with two coffees and stood holding one. She said the gasket was fine. She said it twice. When he reached for the housing bolt she moved the tray six inches away from his hand without looking up, and asked whether the harbour office had called. He said it had, twice, and she said she would deal with it, and went on laying out the parts, though the parts were already laid out. At noon she ate standing. The coffee went cold on the rail; at four she poured it into the gravel and rinsed the cup.

### Autopsy

**Want.** Get the engine back together before the inspection. It is in the work, in the deadline named in dialogue, and in the fact that every sentence is labour. Blockable by a person (the brother), by an institution (the harbour office), and by time.

**Lie.** *A thing is only yours if you repair it alone.* It is in the tray moved six inches, and in the re-laying of parts already laid out — a ritual of self-sufficiency that has no mechanical function. The lie is never argued. It is performed twice, once by moving the tray and once by redoing work already done.

**Need.** To let someone hand her something. It is in the two coffees and the standing brother, in the four hours she keeps the cup on the rail, and in the fact that she destroys it herself rather than let it sit there overnight. She keeps the offer, then removes the evidence of keeping it.

**Cost.** Visible and unremarked: the brother's morning, the inspection she has not called about, the coffee. The story does not tell you these are losses. It shows a woman spending them.

**What is absent.** No stated feeling, no backstory, no simile about her father, no adjective for her pride. Written this way, the passage can only be entered by inference.

**The false inference it permits.** A reader can take the tray move as dislike of the brother — a cold woman, a family in trouble. The cup kept all day contradicts that reading, and the contradiction is the reader's reward for paying attention. In the one passage that carries the character's contradiction, name the wrong reading in a sentence and the detail that refutes it. One passage per character, not every passage: a text that can only be read one way is an explanation with scenery. `[hypothesis]`

**Why it holds.** Every sentence is an action with a price. Nothing in it is a report about a person. That is the whole method: replace adjectives with expenditures.

## Contradiction

Two conditions, both mandatory.

**Costly.** State what the contradiction makes the character lose, and to whom, in which scene. A trait pair on its own loses nothing: *she is ferociously organised at work and cannot keep an appointment with her sister.* Price it and it becomes a contradiction: *she has reorganised the rota twice to sit with a colleague at a scan, has never once been at her sister's, and her sister has stopped asking.*

**Repeated.** Once is a detail, twice is a pattern, three times is a fate. Vary the stakes between occurrences, never the contradiction. A contradiction that appears once can be read as an accident of the scene; the reader needs the second occurrence to know it is load-bearing.

Four kinds, each generating a different scene.

### Value conflict

Two goods the character actually holds, incompatible in this situation.

> She has never lied under oath in twenty-two years on the bench, and she has been lying to her sister about the scan since March.

**Scene type:** the room where both audiences are present. A dinner, an awards evening, a hospital corridor where a colleague and the sister arrive within a minute of each other. The scene is not about the lie; it is about the cost of maintaining two selves in one room.

### Temporal contradiction

Who they were against who they are, with the earlier self still causally present.

> At seventeen he held a boy's head under the water in the quarry until the laughing stopped. At forty he teaches conflict resolution to year nines and is good at it.

**Scene type:** the return. A reunion, a parents' evening, a new colleague who turns out to be the boy, a son who comes home with the same story. The earlier self is not backstory; it is an unpayable debt that walks into the room.

### Performative contradiction

What they claim against what they do, where the claim is sincere.

> He tells the table that rank is a fiction. He has counted, in order, who will speak next, and how long he can hold the floor before the woman on his left is permitted to disagree with him.

**Scene type:** the request that would cost the claim. A favour asked in public, a correction from a subordinate, a toast that requires him to yield the floor. The scene works only if the claim is genuine — hypocrisy the character knows about is a much smaller thing than a belief their body has already abandoned.

### Somatic contradiction

The body against the speech, where the body is the more accurate witness.

> She says she is fine, and her hand goes to the lock of the door behind her, testing it, three times in a minute.

**Scene type:** the interview, the appraisal, the first night in the new house. Anywhere a person who reads bodies is present and the character cannot leave. The somatic tell must be small and repeatable; a trembling hand is weather, a hand that checks a lock three times is a person.

### Why "loves kittens, kills people" is decoration

It is the most common substitute for contradiction in generated prose, and it fails on four counts.

It **cannot be dramatised**. Name the scene in which the tenderness and the violence cannot both be honoured — where the character must choose, and either choice costs. For the kitten-lover there is no such scene. The two traits never meet, so no decision is ever forced.

It **costs nothing**. Complexity that does not extract a price is a costume. The reader registers it as an interesting surface and stops there, because there is nothing underneath to reach.

It is **symmetrical and aesthetic**. Gentleness and cruelty are a pleasing pair. Pleasing pairs are a compositional effect, like a rhyme; they are chosen for how they look in a list, not for what they do in a scene.

It lives **in the character sketch rather than in the manuscript**. That is the tell. Quirk-contradictions are almost always *told* — in a bible, a query letter, a first-page description — because they have no behaviour that expresses them. When you find one in a draft, ask for the scene. There usually isn't one, and its absence is the diagnosis.

The market reason matters too: this is the cheapest kind of complexity to generate. Symmetrical trait pairs are abundant in the space of plausible character descriptions, so a model asked for "a complex character" will produce them by default. Recognising the pattern is a defence against your own first draft.

## Motivation without explanation

Separate two things the word "motivation" hides.

**Motive** is the causal account of why the character acts — the author's knowledge. **Reason** is the account the character gives, to themselves or aloud — the character's knowledge. Fiction runs on reasons. A motive is what you need in order to write a reason that is interesting, wrong, or incomplete.

Explaining a motive removes the reader's participation. The reader's work is to construct a motive from three sources: what the character does, what they say about it, and what it costs them. Hand over the construction and the character becomes a mechanism the reader watches instead of a person they assemble. This is [L6]'s characteristic failure — motivation explained instead of dramatised — and it is also [L4]'s, because the explanation states meaning the arrangement should have produced.

Three techniques. They can be combined, and usually are.

**The displaced motive.** The character names a small, true reason while a large one is visible in behaviour. Small reasons are credible, because people do give them.

> He said the trains were unreliable. He had checked the timetable twice the night before, and once again at the barrier, and he was forty minutes early.

The stated reason is unremarkable and probably true. The checking is the large reason, and it is never named. The reader now knows something the character has not admitted, which is the most comfortable position a reader can be put in.

**The disproportionate reaction.** The reaction reveals the wound, provided the disproportion can be *closed in retrospect*. A reaction that stays unexplained is a mood disorder; a reaction whose cause arrives two scenes later is a plant.

> The waiter set the table and one leg was short, so the table rocked when Ilse put her glass down. She stood, put her coat on, and left. Outside she apologised for eleven minutes, to the waiter, for leaving.

Two disproportions, opposed: leaving over a wobble, then over-apologising for leaving. Neither is explained. The scene that follows can supply a table that rocked in another house, once, and the reader will do the arithmetic — and will believe it, because they performed it.

**The repeated avoidance.** The character approaches the subject three times and deflects it three ways.

> At the door she asked about his back. In the car she asked about the traffic on the coast road. At the table, when he said his mother had asked after her, she said the soup needed salt and went to the kitchen, and stayed there long enough for him to hear her open and close two cupboards.

The wound is the shape of the hole. Three different manoeuvres — a body part, a road, a seasoning — tell the reader the subject is one subject, without anyone naming it. Avoidance is also cheaper to write than confrontation, and it is how most families actually behave.

**When explanation is legal.** Three cases. When the explanation is the character's own and demonstrably wrong — that is characterisation, not exposition, and it is the basis of E15. When the genre's contract requires it: procedural fiction, some YA, some historical, where the reader has bought a guided tour. And in the sequel, after the cost has been paid, where the character is processing rather than deciding. Outside those three, an explained motive is the author talking.

## Agency

Agency is the capacity to make a costly choice that changes the situation. Not admirable, not competent, not in control. A character can be agentic and catastrophically wrong; that is the normal case.

The audit, per scene, four columns:

| Who chooses | The options, priced | What the choice costs | What happens if they don't |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maren | call the office now and lose the morning's work, or finish and risk the inspection | the inspection, or the engine | the brother calls, and the choice is made for her |

Run it on every scene. Two answers are failures: **nobody chooses** — the turn arrives by accident, message, or new arrival; and **the chooser is not the protagonist** in a scene that turns the story's main value, unless that displacement is the point and is paid for later.

### Agency is not puppetry

Puppetry is a choice the plot required. It has three signatures, and any one of them is enough to convict.

1. **Substitutability.** A different plausible character in the same position would do the same thing. Then the choice belongs to the situation, not the person.
2. **Non-necessity.** The plot would work just as well if they chose otherwise. Then the character is not load-bearing; they are decoration with dialogue.
3. **Fresh price.** The cost they pay was invented in the scene that charges it, rather than built across the story. Then the price is a fee the author paid on the character's behalf.

`[data: StoryScope]` measures a binary feature — whether resolution is driven by protagonist choice or by external events — and finds it in 69% of AI-written stories against 46% of human ones. Read the feature precisely: it cannot tell a costly choice from a costless one. So the figure says something narrower and more useful than "AI characters have too much agency". It says the *presence* of a protagonist choice at the resolution is a default, and defaults are what you have to inspect rather than trust. `[hypothesis]`

### "The character decides to accept"

This is the cheapest way to satisfy the pattern, and one of the weakest endings available. The character's want dissolves rather than being paid for; the dramatic question is answered by removing the terms of the question; and the cost is converted into wisdom. The reader is handed a lesson — [L4] failure — and the character is handed nothing they had to spend.

Acceptance is agentic only when it is a **refusal of something available**. The character must be able to name what they are turning down, someone must have offered it, and the turning down must foreclose a possibility the reader wanted. Then acceptance is a choice with a corpse in it. Otherwise it is the plot putting itself away.

The same test applies to passivity. A passive protagonist is legal when the story is *about* the impossibility of choice — but then the passivity must be chosen against alternatives, and the text must price it. Passivity that is merely convenient for the plot is not a theme. It is an absence.

## Moral ambiguity

Ambiguity is built from **competing goods**, not from a villain's sympathetic backstory. A backstory explains how someone became what they are; it does not make their position good. Two characters who are each partly right about incompatible things produce ambiguity. One character who is wrong but was sad as a child produces sympathy, which is a different and much cheaper effect.

**The rule.** Both sides must be able to state the other's argument in good faith — and better than the other states it. Test this by writing each side's best case in the other's voice, then cutting both passages from the manuscript. What remains should be behaviour that only makes sense if both arguments are live.

Three structures.

### The double bind

Two obligations, each real, which cannot both be met in this situation.

> The witness's account is only true in her own dialect, and the court will not accept a translation that keeps it. Render it faithfully and she is discredited; render it cleanly and she is convicted on a lie.

**Scene type:** the decision scene where the character must choose which harm to authorise, and there is no third option available in the room. The scene fails if a third option turns up at the last minute.

### Dirty hands

Every available action requires doing something wrong. The question is not whether to get dirty but which wrong, and who pays for it.

> The load log is already two tonnes over what the crane will take. Falsify it and the crew gets paid on Friday. Refuse and eight men go home to explain it.

**Scene type:** the negotiation in which the character tries to buy a third option and fails — calls someone, offers something, trades a favour — and then chooses the least bad wrong in front of a witness.

### The loyalty trap

Obligation to a person against obligation to a principle or to another person, where loyalty is the stronger emotional claim and the weaker moral one.

> His brother asks him to say he was there that night. He was not there. He has been the one who was there for thirty years.

**Scene type:** the request scene, which is simultaneously the test of the relationship (E5). The character's answer re-prices a ledger row, and the reader can see the row move.

### Keeping it from collapsing into "everyone is bad"

Five devices.

- **Each side must win something the reader wants.** If one side only takes, the ambiguity is a delay before the verdict.
- **Show the cost on the side that loses, concretely.** Not "she was devastated" — the empty chair, the returned keys, the name removed from a list.
- **Do not distribute vice evenly.** Even-handedness is not the same as equal guilt, and a text that apportions fault symmetrically reads as cowardice. Let one side be more wrong and still legible.
- **Give one side a factual advantage you find uncomfortable.** The character whose politics you dislike should be right about the thing that is checkable. This is the single most effective anti-collapse device, because it forbids the reader from dismissing them.
- **Do not adjudicate.** No closing paragraph that tells the reader who was right, no narrator's aside, no final image that resolves the argument into weather. If you must resolve, resolve the *plot* and leave the *judgement* to the reader.

The target is not "no one can judge". It is: the reader can reach a verdict and cannot keep it. Ambiguity that prevents judgement is nihilism; ambiguity that makes judgement necessary and insufficient is the thing worth building.

## Character change

Change must be visible in behaviour under pressure. Not in a quiet scene, not in a statement, not in a final paragraph where the character looks at the sea and understands something.

The test is the **same-situation return**. Put the character back into a concrete situation from earlier — the same room, the same request, the same offer — and observe what they do. Identical behaviour means one of three things: nothing changed; the change is in the reader's knowledge rather than the character (revelation); or the change is reported but not written. Name which, because each has a different repair. `[hypothesis]`

Four types.

**Reversal.** A value inverts. Loyalty becomes betrayal, or the other way. The strongest reversals are prepared and invisible: every earlier scene must be re-readable as the opposite of what it seemed.

**Revelation.** The character does not change; the reader's model of them does. Information arrives that re-prices everything they have already done. This is the most common type in literary fiction and the least discussed, because "arc" language has no slot for it. It can carry a whole novel without a single change in behaviour.

**Hardening.** The value holds; the character becomes less flexible and pays a higher price for it. Not growth. The same person with fewer exits.

**Refusal.** The character is offered the change — clearly, credibly, by someone who loves them — and declines. The world changes instead. This is underused and often stronger than any growth arc, because it refuses the therapeutic shape and keeps the cost on the page. The reader's disappointment is the story's engine, and disappointment is more durable than uplift.

**What "arc" talk obscures.** Four things.

*Shape versus causation.* An arc implies a curve, which invites scheduling beats along it. The curve is a description of the result; it is not a cause. Change comes from events, and an event that is not in the story cannot cause anything.

*Growth as a hidden value judgement.* Arc language assumes the destination is improvement. Hardening and refusal are not failures of an arc; they are different, complete figures.

*The reader's change.* Arc language locates all movement in the character. Often the movement is in the reader's understanding, and the character is deliberately static — the point being that this is what such a person is like, and always was.

*The bill.* Arc language makes change sound like an achievement. Change is a purchase. Name what it cost, in what currency, paid to whom. A change that costs nothing is a mood.

## Relationships

A relationship is a pattern of exchange, not a feeling between two people. Feelings are what the pattern produces. Write the pattern and the feeling arrives uninvited; write the feeling and the pattern has to be invented later, usually as a scene of explanation.

**The ledger.** Five rows per pair.

| Row | Question | Example (Ada and June, after their mother died) |
|---|---|---|
| Initiative | Who starts contact, proposes, calls, arrives? | June calls every Sunday. Ada answers and says she is busy. |
| Permission | Who may touch, ask, refuse without explaining, enter without knocking? | Ada may walk into June's house. June may not ask about the money. |
| Debt | What is owed, by whom, in what currency, and is it ever mentionable? | June paid for the funeral. The figure has never been spoken. |
| Taboo | What cannot be raised, and what happens in the room when it is? | The last week of their mother's life, and who was there. |
| Physical grammar | Distance, touch, who occupies the doorway, who handles the other's things | They sit at opposite ends of the sofa and pass objects hand to hand rather than put them down. |

Two things to notice. First, the rows are **independent**. Any one can move while the others hold, which is what makes a relationship trackable across a long work — and what makes it a continuity instrument as well as a dramatic one (E13). Second, **power lives in permission**, not in who talks loudest. A character who may enter without knocking holds more of the relationship than one who may not, whatever the dialogue says. `[hypothesis]`

**Change one row.** The scene writes itself from the change.

> June names the funeral figure at the table, in front of Ada's husband. Nothing else about the afternoon is different: same sofa, same Sunday, same two cups.

The debt row moves from unmentionable to spoken, and three other rows are now under pressure. Permission changes — Ada can no longer walk in, because the thing she was permitted not to discuss has been said. Initiative becomes contested, because the Sunday call now has a subject. Physical grammar tightens, because neither of them will pass anything hand to hand again this year. One change, four consequences, no statement of feeling.

**Rules.**

- A relationship scene must change a row. If it confirms the existing pattern, it is a status report; fold it into a scene that moves one.
- Show the change through a **physical or logistical detail**, never through a character's summary of the relationship. The doorway, the extra chair, the name used or not used, who drives.
- Name the direction of the change and who pays for it. A row that moves costs someone; if nobody pays, the change is cosmetic.
- Do not move two rows in one scene unless the second movement is caused by the first.

## Character voice in narration

In third person limited, the narration is still the character's mind, filtered. The character's wants, vocabulary, and habits of attention decide what the sentence notices, what it names, what it compares a thing to, and what it leaves out entirely. That is voice.

**Voice is selection. A verbal tic is a surface feature.** A tic is a catchphrase, a favourite adverb, a sentence-opening formula, a habit of swearing. Tics are audible and cheap; they are also the first thing a model reaches for when asked for "a distinctive voice", which is why they cluster.

The tests separate them.

- **Strip the tics.** Delete every catchphrase and every signature construction. Does the paragraph still belong to this mind? If it could be anyone's, you had tics and no voice.
- **Swap the tics.** Give the same paragraph a different set of surface habits. Is it still the same person? If yes, the voice is structural and the tics were decoration.
- **Ask what is not noticed.** Voice is most legible in the omission. A character who never describes anyone's clothes, or who describes every room by its exits, or who cannot mention a price without also mentioning who paid — that is voice, and no tic is required.

The same room, two minds, third person, no interiority stated.

> The bench was clean, which meant nothing, because varnish hides a burn until the light moves. A ring, four inches across, the finish lifted at the rim. Under a hot pan it would go on lifting for a week. Sand it back, build it up, and it would still read as a repair, because the grain never comes back the same.

> She had cleaned the bench and someone had put a hot pan on it and the ring was still there. She kept her coat on. The ring was the size of a cup. He always put things down wherever, and came back for them later; now the ring was the only thing in the house his hand had left.

Same bench, same ring, same absent person implied. The first mind sorts the world into failure modes and repairs; the second sorts it into who did what and what remains. Neither narrator tells you who is speaking, and you can tell anyway. That is the whole test.

## Character diagnostics

Ten questions. Each exposes a specific flatness, and each has a repair that is a scene, not a note in the bible.

1. **What does this character do with money, and does anyone in the cast do it differently?** Flatness: the whole cast has the same relationship to resources, because the author has never had to think about it.
2. **Whose permission do they need before they can act?** Flatness: autonomy without social dependency — characters who are free in a world where everyone should be constrained.
3. **What was the last thing they lied about, and to whom?** Flatness: honesty as a default. A character with no active lie has no friction with the people closest to them.
4. **What would they refuse even at the cost of the plot?** Flatness: characters who always do what the story needs. If nothing is refused, the plot is driving and the character is cargo.
5. **What do they do with their hands while losing an argument?** Flatness: bodies that only emote. Hands that tidy, check, count, or reach for a pocket are character; hands that tremble are weather.
6. **Which of their beliefs is shared by everyone in the book?** Flatness: one ideology distributed across several mouths. A cast that agrees about the important thing cannot dramatise it.
7. **What do they notice first in a room, and is it what everyone else notices?** Flatness: a single sensorium. The strongest cast has three incompatible definitions of what is worth describing.
8. **What have they stopped asking for?** Flatness: desire without a history of defeat. What a person no longer requests is a record of what they were refused.
9. **Whose imagined judgement shapes their behaviour when they are alone?** Flatness: no internalised audience. Everyone performs for someone, living or dead, present or not.
10. **What would it take for them to change their mind, and has anyone tried?** Flatness: change without resistance, or a refusal with no offer behind it. Both make the ending arbitrary.

Two rules for using the list. Answer for the two or three characters who carry the story; a full cast of answered characters is a cast where no one is legible at a glance. And convert every answer into a scene or delete it — an answer that lives only in the character file has been planned, not written.

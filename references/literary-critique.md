# Literary Critique: Diagnosis, Not Taste

> Purpose: turn "this doesn't work" into a located claim that names a level, a mechanism and the smallest repair that fixes the most.

## The critic's job

A critique is a diagnosis. Diagnosis means: name the defect, locate it, explain the mechanism, rank it, and stop. It does not mean rendering a verdict on whether the text pleases you. The two are different speech acts with different truth conditions.

"I didn't like it" is a report about the critic. It is unfalsifiable, unactionable, and it tells the writer nothing except that one reader's taste differs from theirs. "This fails at [L5] because the turn is produced by a letter arriving rather than by a choice, and the choice it displaces was the scene's only cost" is a claim about the text: it can be checked against the page, argued with, and repaired. Only the second kind belongs in a critique.

**Why ten problems is a failure, not thoroughness.** Three reasons, all practical:

1. **The revision budget is small.** A writer can hold one structural repair in mind while rewriting a scene. Give them five and they will attempt all five at once, produce a compromised scene, and conclude the advice was wrong.
2. **Defects interact.** A structural repair rewrites the sentences a line note was about. Reporting both does not double the value; it delays the structural fix behind local work that will be thrown away.
3. **A long list usually counts one cause several times.** Ten symptoms with one cause is not ten findings. The critic who reports all ten has not yet done the diagnostic work of finding the cause.

The output contract follows from this: **at most two structural findings and three local findings per pass** (§5). Everything else goes on a deferred list, one line each, so it is not lost and not acted on yet.

**The critic does not own the story.** You may say the story's question is not being served. You may not say it should be a different question. The moment a critique requires the writer to care about something else, it has stopped being criticism and become a competing draft.

## Evidence rule

Every claim carries three things, in this order:

```
[L5] Claim. Evidence: "≤12 words" (¶n). Mechanism: because …, the reader …
```

- **Evidence** is a quotation of twelve words or fewer, with a location. The cap is not decoration. A long excerpt lets the critic hide the argument inside the quote, and it makes the defect hard for the writer to find. If twelve words will not show it, the claim is about a pattern — then quote two instances, or count them.
- **Level** comes from the L1–L7 grid. Naming the level is what prevents the classic error of rewriting sentences to fix a structural problem.
- **Mechanism** is the causal sentence: *because the sentence stacks three participles before its verb, the reader holds three unresolved modifiers and the main action arrives late.* "Awkward", "flat", "unclear" are symptoms, not mechanisms. A mechanism can be disagreed with; an adjective cannot.

**No claim may rest on the critic's authority.** These are all failures of the rule:

- "As a reader, I felt distanced." — a report of a feeling, with no location.
- "Good writers never do this." — an appeal to a canon that is not cited and would not settle the question if it were.
- "This is objectively weak." — objectivity asserted, not demonstrated.

**A claim you cannot evidence is a hypothesis, and must be labelled as one.**

```
[hypothesis] The middle third sags. I cannot locate the sag without the full manuscript; on the
sample, the suspicion rests on two scene endings that both resolve rather than open.
```

Labelling is not a weakness. It is the difference between a critic who knows the limits of the sample and one who does not. An unlabelled guess contaminates the revision plan; a labelled one is a question the writer can answer in a sentence.

**Two further disciplines.**

- *One instance proves the instance, not the pattern.* "This sentence is vague" needs one quote. "The prose is vague" needs two or more instances, or a count ("eleven of the first twenty sentences end on an abstract noun"). Without that, the pattern claim is a hypothesis.
- *Quote what is on the page, not what you remember.* Misquotation is the most common way a critique becomes unfalsifiable: the writer cannot find the passage, and the finding evaporates.

## Differential diagnosis

Borrowed from structured search: do not accept the first plausible explanation. Enumerate candidates, find the evidence that *separates* them, then keep the smallest set that explains every symptom. The method is Ghostbuster's shape without its machinery — a search over hypotheses scored by argument, not by F1 ([what transfers and what does not](research/02-ghostbuster.md)).

**Procedure.**

1. **Collect symptoms, not diagnoses.** Write what a reader experiences: *I skimmed the middle; the ending landed but did not move me; I could not say what the scene was for.* Symptoms are data. "The scene is flat" is already a hypothesis.
2. **Enumerate three to seven candidates**, spread across levels, not clustered in one. Fewer than three means you stopped at the first idea. More than seven means you are listing rather than diagnosing.
3. **For each candidate, state the discriminating evidence**: what would be on the page if this cause were true, and absent if it were not. A candidate with no discriminator is not a hypothesis, it is a mood.
4. **Look.** Quote. Record what you found, including the candidates the evidence kills.
5. **Keep the smallest set that explains all the symptoms.** If two candidates explain the same symptom equally well, prefer the one with the stronger discriminator — not the one you thought of first.
6. **Ablate.** Remove the cause in imagination and repair only it. Do the symptoms go? If the scene is still flat after the repair, the diagnosis was wrong or incomplete.

**Symptom → candidate families.** A starting grid, not a checklist.

| Symptom | Candidates to consider first |
|---|---|
| "I skimmed" | no state change [L5]; nothing at risk [L6]; approach longer than the turn [L5/L2] |
| "I was confused" | missing information [L4]; too many names at once [L1]; temporal order withheld without payoff [L7] |
| "I didn't believe it" | unearned turn [L5]; motive explained not shown [L6]; emotion asserted without cost [L6] |
| "It felt flat" | see the worked example below — five candidates |
| "It felt fast / thin" | consequence missing [L5]; summary where scene was needed [L5]; turn with no approach [L5] |
| "The voice is off" | register drift [L3]; narration at the wrong distance [L3]; a borrowed genre voice [L3] |

### Worked example: "the scene feels flat"

**Symptom report.** A reader says: the dinner scene with the sister is flat. I didn't skim it — I read every line — but nothing landed. The scene: Anna's sister offers to cover their father's debt; Anna agrees; the scene ends with the dishes.

**Candidates and their discriminators.**

| # | Candidate | Present if… | Absent if… |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | No state change [L5] | you cannot say what is different at the end; the scene could be cut without the next one changing | something is different — an agreement exists that did not |
| B | Unearned turn [L5] | the change is produced by an arrival, a message or an accident; the protagonist's last choice is unrelated to the turn | the change follows from a decision Anna makes, even a bad one |
| C | Wrong POV distance [L3] | the decision is reported, not rendered: "she had decided to accept"; no access to what it costs her | the decision is dramatised at a distance where the cost is perceptible |
| D | Flat rhythm [L2] | the turn carries the same syntactic weight as the approach; sentence lengths within a few words of each other; breaks fall arbitrarily | the turn is marked — by a short sentence, a paragraph break, a change of pace |
| E | Missing information [L4] | the change is legible but its weight is not; the reader asks "why does this matter"; the fact that would give it weight arrives later | the reader can evaluate the cost at the moment of the choice |

**What the page shows.**

- There is a state change: "she said she would take it" — a debt is now Anna's. A is refuted.
- The change is caused: Anna chooses, against her own interest, and the choice is shown as a choice. B is refuted.
- Sentence lengths vary across the scene, and the agreement lands on its own short paragraph. D is refuted.
- The decision is given in summary: "she had decided to take the loan." No interior access at the moment of choice. C is supported.
- The fact that makes the loan unbearable — that it is their father's debt, and Anna has spent nine years refusing to pay it — appears two scenes later, in a conversation with the mother. E is supported.

**Verdict.** Two candidates survive, and they are not equal. **E is primary**: the scene's change has no weight on the page because the information that gives it weight is placed after the reader needed it. **C is the mechanism by which E goes unfelt**: even with the fact present, a decision reported in summary gives the reader nothing to watch.

**Repair order.** Move the father's-debt fact before the offer (E), so the offer is a trap the reader sees before Anna does. Then render the choice at a distance where her calculation is visible (C). Do not reverse the order: rendering a decision whose stakes the reader cannot yet evaluate produces a vivid scene about nothing — the exact failure that makes "show, don't tell" advice so often damaging.

**What this example is for.** A critic who saw only D would have rewritten sentences and the scene would still be flat. A critic who reported A through E would have buried the one repair that matters under four that do not. The differential is what makes the critique usable.

## The twelve axes

The rubric behind `tests/rubric.md`, in prose. Each axis is scored 1–5; **5** means the property is doing structural work — remove it and the story changes. **3** means present and competent but unremarkable. **1** means absent: not weak, missing. A judge who cannot find the property at all must use the 1 rather than reaching for a charitable 2, because absence and weakness need different repairs.

Every axis has a characteristic false positive — the thing critics mistake for strength. That is the most useful line in each entry.

### 1. Narrative coherence — [L5/L7]

**A 5:** removing any scene breaks something downstream; subplots touch the spine at two or more points; the ending's answer is the question the first pages opened.
**A 2:** events are sequential but interchangeable; the through-line exists only in the synopsis; a subplot could be deleted with no loss.
**False positive:** tightness mistaken for coherence. A story with no loose material may be *tidy*, not coherent — connection is what counts, and a single track has few connections to make. Tidiness also reads as control, which critics reward.
**Ask:** which scene, if cut, would force a change three scenes later?

### 2. Character depth — [L6]

**A 5:** the character does something that contradicts their stated value, and the reader can see the defence that makes it possible; two incompatible wants coexist without either being explained away.
**A 2:** traits are listed and consistently applied; the contradiction is announced ("she was both fierce and fragile") rather than enacted; behaviour is legible only because the narration glosses it.
**False positive:** consistency mistaken for depth. A character who always behaves according to type is *reliable*. Depth is what the type does under pressure it cannot absorb.
**Ask:** what does this character want that they would not admit to wanting, and where does the text show it without saying it?

### 3. Character agency — [L5/L6]

**A 5:** the decisive turns are produced by choices that cost the chooser something they valued; the choice is between two losses, not between a loss and a shrug.
**A 2:** the protagonist reacts; events arrive; the choice has an obvious right answer, so selecting it is not a decision; or the character acts constantly and nothing they do has a price.
**False positive:** busyness mistaken for agency. A character who acts on every page but never chooses at cost is *active*. So is a decisive character whose decisions always succeed — competence is not agency.
**Ask:** name the last decision this character made that cost them something they wanted.

### 4. Causal consistency — [L5]

**A 5:** every effect traces to a cause inside the fiction's own rules; coincidence is spent once, early, to start trouble, and never to end it.
**A 2:** the plot requires an event with no cause but authorial need — a rescue, a revelation, a forgotten object that appears when useful; or a cause is supplied after the effect, as rationalisation.
**False positive:** density mistaken for causality. Many events, each explained, can still be a chain of conveniences. Explanation after the fact is not causation; it is a receipt.
**Ask:** delete this event. Does the story's logic fail, or only its convenience?

### 5. Temporal complexity — [L7]

**A 5:** the order is the only order that withholds what the reader needs until the moment it costs most; each anachrony changes the meaning of the scene it interrupts.
**A 2:** the chronology is scrambled for texture; the reader reconstructs dates as a puzzle with no payoff; the flashback supplies information the linear order would have supplied more cheaply.
**False positive:** non-linearity mistaken for complexity. A shuffled timeline is a format. Complexity is the work the order does on the reader's understanding — and a chronological story can be temporally complex if duration and frequency are handled with intent.
**Ask:** what does the reader know at this moment *because of* the order, that they would not know otherwise?

### 6. Dialogue quality — [L6/L3]

**A 5:** each speaker pursues an agenda they do not state; replies answer the act rather than the line; idiolect lives in syntax, not accent; silence and logistics carry information.
**A 2:** characters exchange what the reader needs; both say what they mean; every reply is on-topic; class is marked by phonetic spelling; the scene ends with a farewell.
**False positive:** wit mistaken for dialogue quality. Fast, quotable exchanges can be two performers rather than two people — and "naturalistic" overlap without agenda is transcription, not dialogue.
**Ask:** what does each speaker want from the other in this scene, and why can't they ask for it directly?

### 7. Prose quality — [L1–L3]

**A 5:** sentences are shaped by what they carry; paragraph breaks fall where the thought turns; the image is the one that fits this object and would misfit any other.
**A 2:** uniform sentence length; adjectives doing the work nouns should do; metaphors whose vehicles could attach to anything; every paragraph the same size.
**False positive:** smoothness mistaken for quality. Fluent, error-free prose that any competent writer could have produced is [L3] flat. Fluency is the floor. It is also the easiest property to mistake for the ceiling, because it is the one readers notice first.
**Ask:** which sentence here could not have been written about a different object by a different narrator?

### 8. Emotional credibility — [L6/L4]

**A 5:** the feeling is the consequence of a cost the reader watched being paid; the character's response is disproportionate in a way that reveals them; the reader infers the emotion from behaviour and is not told what to feel.
**A 2:** emotion is named or somatised instead of caused; grief arrives on schedule; the narration instructs ("she felt a wave of"); the feeling exceeds anything the scene has charged for.
**False positive:** intensity mistaken for credibility. Big feeling with no prior cost reads as performance. The inverse error is equally common: restraint praised as depth when the text has simply not done the work of making restraint mean anything.
**Ask:** what did this character lose, on the page, before this feeling?

### 9. Originality — [L7]

**A 5:** the premise's obvious development is refused somewhere, and the replacement is justified by this story's particulars rather than by a desire to surprise; familiar forms are used knowingly.
**A 2:** the story is the modal continuation; the twist is the standard twist; the setting is a costume over a stock plot; the refusal is arbitrary and unmotivated.
**False positive:** strangeness mistaken for originality. Arbitrary weirdness is as predictable as convention, because inverting a default is still determined by it. Obscurity is not novelty; it is novelty's alibi.
**Ask:** which decision here would a competent writer given this setup *not* have made — and does the text pay for it?

### 10. Structural integrity — [L5/L7]

**A 5:** the ending answers the question the opening asked, at a cost, and the last line lands on the change; proportions match the load each part carries.
**A 2:** the ending answers a different question than the one raised; the climax arrives without the middle's preparation; the last line explains what happened; acts are balanced but nothing is load-bearing.
**False positive:** symmetry mistaken for structure. Matched acts and mirrored scenes feel designed. Integrity is about load, not balance — and a "satisfying" ending that resolves everything is often the defect, not the achievement.
**Ask:** restate the question from the first page and the answer from the last. Is it the same question?

### 11. Style consistency — [L3]

**A 5:** the voice keeps its habits and changes by function — a comic scene and a grief scene are shaped differently and are recognisably the same narrator.
**A 2:** register drifts without motive; the narration borrows the genre's default voice in one chapter and the writer's tic in the next; tense or person slips; the comic scene is written by a different sensibility.
**False positive:** uniformity mistaken for consistency. A narrator who sounds identical in every mode is a monotone. Consistency is identity across change, not the absence of change.
**Ask:** what are this narrator's three habits, and are they present when the material changes?

### 12. Continuity — [L5]

**A 5:** facts, knowledge, debts and names hold; a contradiction is either corrected or converted into plot; no character knows what they have not learned.
**A 2:** eye colour, distances and dates drift; a character refers to a fact they learn two chapters later; an injury disappears; the money does not add up.
**False positive:** a well-kept bible mistaken for continuity on the page. The ledger is not the text. What matters is whether a reader can catch the contradiction — and readers catch fewer than writers fear, which is why continuity errors are severe out of proportion to their visibility: they are trust, not detail.
**Ask:** list what each present character knows at this point. Does the scene respect it?

## Severity and leverage

**Four bands.**

| Band | Definition | What repair means |
|---|---|---|
| **Fatal** | the work is not yet a story: no question, no change, the wrong person pays, an ending that answers nothing | rebuild; local notes are noise until this is fixed |
| **Structural** | a load-bearing component fails: unearned turn, missing cost, a subplot that never touches the spine, a POV that hides the story | repair invalidates downstream prose; do it before anything else |
| **Local** | a scene, a passage, an entry or exit, a stretch of dialogue | contained; repair changes one scene and its neighbours |
| **Cosmetic** | a word, a punctuation choice, a line rhythm, a repeated tic | safe and cheap; last, always |

**The cap.** Report at most **two structural** and **three local** findings in one pass. Order them by leverage. Put everything else on a deferred list with one line each. The two structural findings must be the two with the highest leverage in the whole text; if a third has higher leverage than either, the first two were misranked and the list must be rebuilt. The cap is not modesty; it is the condition under which advice can be acted on, and it forces the critic to decide which finding explains the others.

**Severity is not ease.** Keep two axes separate:

- *Severity* — how much breaks if this stays unfixed.
- *Leverage* — how much improves per unit of work, and how much existing work the repair invalidates.

Rank by severity band first (fatal → structural → local → cosmetic). Inside a band, rank by leverage: the repair that changes the most downstream text goes first. A cosmetic defect in the first line has high leverage and low severity — mention it, last. A structural defect in a subplot nobody reads may be severe and low-leverage; it goes after the structural defect in the main line.

**Two rules that follow.**

1. **If you find a fatal defect, stop diagnosing.** Report it and the one repair it implies. A ten-item plan built on a premise that has to change is ten items of wasted work, and it teaches the writer to distrust the plan.
2. **Do not rank by obviousness.** The most visible defect is frequently a symptom. Obviousness is a property of the reader's attention, not of the text's structure.

Level order (structure, then motivation, then information, then scene shape, then voice, then rhythm, then words) is in `SKILL.md`'s revision triage. This section adds the band, the cap, and the rule for ranking *inside* a band.

## Critique formats

Three formats. Choose by what the asker needs, not by how much you noticed.

### A. The one-paragraph verdict (≤120 words)

For "is this any good" and for a quick read. No list. No praise sandwich.

```
This [form] is trying to [question, as a question]. It [does / does not], because
[mechanism, with a ≤12-word quote]. The load-bearing failure is [defect] at [L#],
visible at [location]. Repairing it would [consequence]. Anything else I noticed is
downstream of that.
```

If the work succeeds, say what it is doing and stop. Do not manufacture a defect to seem rigorous — a fabricated flaw is worse than a clean verdict, because the writer will repair something that was working.

### B. The diagnosis sheet

For a revision plan, or any draft over ~1,500 words.

```
## Verdict
≤3 sentences: what it is trying to do, whether it does, the one thing in the way.

## Symptoms
2–4 reader experiences, no diagnoses. ("I skimmed ¶12–19"; "the ending landed but did not move me.")

## Findings
### F1 — [defect] · [L#] · [structural]
- Evidence: "≤12 words" (¶n) [+ second instance if claiming a pattern]
- Mechanism: because …, the reader …
- Repair: [specific action]
- Risk: [what the repair endangers]
- Order: 1

### F2 — …

## What is working and must survive
1–3 items, each with a quote. Not compliments: constraints on the repair.

## Deferred
One line each, with the reason (budget, dependency, sample too short).

## Not diagnosed
What this sample cannot support. Name the limit; do not fill it with a guess.
```

The **"must survive"** field is the load-bearing one. The commonest damage in revision is fixing a flat scene by deleting the thing that made it worth having — the odd detail, the wrong-word joke, the character who was only in that scene. Name it before the repair starts.

The **"risk"** field is mandatory. A repair with no stated risk is a rewrite in disguise, and rewrites need a different conversation.

### C. The line audit

For copy-level work on a short passage. Table only.

```
| # | Location | Quote (≤12 words) | Level | Problem | Repair |
```

Rules:

- One defect per row. Two defects in one row means one of them will be skipped.
- The repair is a change, not an instruction. "Cut the second clause" — not "tighten".
- Cap at twenty rows, then stop and say the sample is saturated. Past twenty, the problem is not the lines; it is the paragraph's job, and that is format B.
- Group rows by mechanism, not by position. Twenty instances of one habit is a finding about the habit; twenty unrelated fixes is a copy-edit.

## What not to say

1. **"Vivid imagery."** Without a quote it is a compliment about the critic's reading experience. With a quote it is still weak, because vividness is a property of the reader's response: say what the image *does* — what it makes the reader infer, what it refuses to name. *Instead:* "the smell of the second cup does the work the word 'grief' would have done."

2. **"Compelling."** A word that describes the critic's attention and cannot be checked in the text. It is also the most common way to avoid saying what a scene is doing. *Instead:* name the question the reader is holding, and where it was opened.

3. **A rewrite that changes the story's question.** "What if instead she never went back" is a different story, not a better one. The critique becomes a competing draft and the writer has to defend their premise instead of repairing their execution. *Instead:* show that the current question is not being served, and where.

4. **Confusing obscurity with depth.** A passage that cannot be paraphrased is not thereby profound; it may be a passage with no proposition in it. Depth is what survives paraphrase and gains from it. Obscurity is a cost the reader pays for a return that may never come. *Instead:* state what you think the passage asserts, then ask whether the text supports it.

5. **Mistaking slowness for pace.** A slow scene with a live question is paced. A fast scene with no question is dead. Length and speed are not pace; pace is the rate at which the reader's questions open and close. *Instead:* count the open questions per page, not the words.

6. **Treating a convention as a flaw.** A genre's obligatory scene, a romance's required beat, a crime novel's culprit — the convention is the contract. It is a flaw only when executed without the particularity the contract leaves room for. *Instead:* judge the execution against the contract, and name the contract first (`references/genre-and-form.md`).

7. **Treating a flaw as a style choice without evidence.** "The repetition is deliberate" is a claim about intention, and intention leaves traces: pattern, variation, placement, a reason the repetition appears where it does. Absent those, repetition is a tic with a defence. *Instead:* ask what the repetition is doing at each occurrence; if the answer is the same each time, it is doing nothing.

8. **"I would have written it differently."** The most common critique failure, and the hardest to see, because it feels like insight. It reports a preference, transfers authorship, and gives the writer no way to evaluate the advice except by adopting the critic's taste. *Instead:* state the effect on the reader and the mechanism that produces it. The writer can then choose a different mechanism for the same effect.

## Evidence sufficiency

Confidence is capped by sample size before it is capped by analysis quality. A brilliant reading of 150 words is still a reading of 150 words.

The empirical basis for caution is the fingerprinting study's length finding: in its fully paraphrased, surface-scrubbed condition, identification accuracy at fifty words sits near 22–27% against a 12.5% chance baseline, and only approaches 50% at full-response length `[data: Fingerprinting]`. The unparaphrased condition scores higher. If that is how much identifying structure a short span carries for a machine classifier after paraphrase, a human reader's structural verdict on the same span deserves at least the same caution. The word budgets below are `[hypothesis]` — engineering judgement derived from that finding, to be revised against real sessions.

| Sample | You may judge | You must defer |
|---|---|---|
| A sentence to a paragraph (L1–L2) | a vague noun, a register slip, filler, a monotone run of sentences | any claim about the writer's diction or syntax as a pattern |
| 600–800 words, original, free-form (L3) | whether a voice is present, tonal drift, image density | voice from 200 words; voice from a paraphrase; voice from a fixed form (sonnet, mandated beat sheet) |
| The scene or more (L4) | whether meaning is stated or embedded in this scene; whether subtext has a surface | the work's theme from a chapter |
| The whole work (L5–L7) | causality, agency, per-scene state change, composition, originality | nothing structural from an excerpt — `[hypothesis]` a scene cannot show you a spine |

Three operational rules.

1. **Voice is not judged from 200 words.** Not as a stylistic preference — as an arithmetic consequence of how little identifying structure a short span carries. Give the local observation ("this sentence is doing X") and name the ceiling.
2. **Structure is not judged from a scene.** A scene can show you a symptom of a structural defect; it cannot show you the structure. `[hypothesis]` — but the hypothesis is cheap to state and expensive to skip.
3. **A fixed-form sample cannot support a voice claim.** When the form dictates the wording, the writer's signature has nowhere to live. This is measurable in the attribution literature and it is obvious in craft terms: a sonnet's voice is partly the sonnet's.

When the sample is too short, the correct output is not a hedged verdict. It is: *this cannot be judged from what I have, and here is the local observation I can make.* An honest limit is usable. A confident guess at the wrong level is not.

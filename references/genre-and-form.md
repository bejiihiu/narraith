## Form by length

Length is a budget for questions, not a number of pages. Each band can carry a certain number of live questions, turns, and strands; asking for more is what produces the forms' characteristic failures, which are all versions of too much story in too little room.

Form makes its own promise, before genre does. A novella promises intensity and one pressure sustained; a novel promises a world with room in it; a story promises a turn and no tour. A reader who picks up a novella and gets a novel's first act feels cheated in a way that has nothing to do with quality, and a writer who does not know which promise they are making cannot keep either.

The word counts below are publishing conventions — the bands magazines and awards use — not properties of prose. They matter because readers and editors price a form before they read it: a 30,000-word manuscript is read as a novella, with a novella's expectations, whatever the cover says. A writer who ignores the bands is not breaking a law; they are choosing to fight an expectation they could have used.

### Flash (under 1,000 words)

- **Can carry:** one turn, one image, one gap the reader closes. A single change of state, often a recognition assembled from two details rather than stated.
- **Cannot:** a subplot, a second setting that needs establishing, a character arc requiring two decisions, a reveal that depends on accumulated evidence.
- **Characteristic failure:** the twist flash — a last-line reversal that re-reads the piece but was never planted, so the reader feels tricked rather than rewarded; or the vignette, beautiful and unchanged.
- **Structural consequence:** everything that is not the turn comes out. The first sentence establishes the pressure, the last lands on the change, and exposition is carried by action because there is no room to stop and explain.

Flash is not a short story compressed; it is a different machine. Compressing a short story removes the material between the turns. Flash removes the turns themselves and leaves the reader to supply the change from two images placed side by side. Writers who compress a short story produce a summary of a story; writers who write flash produce the moment the summary would have led to.

### Short story (1,000–7,500 words)

- **Can carry:** one dramatic question, two or three scenes, one reversal plus a crisis. One secondary strand, and only if it crosses the spine once.
- **Cannot:** two full arcs, a cast with competing wants, a world whose rules need to be demonstrated before they can be used.
- **Characteristic failure:** the novel in miniature — a third of the length spent on setup, the turn at eighty percent, and an ending that explains what the turn meant.
- **Structural consequence:** begin after the situation is established. The first scene should contain the pressure, not introduce it. Every scene that could be summarised in a sentence without loss should be summarised in a sentence.

### Long story / novelette (7,500–17,000 words)

- **Can carry:** one question in two movements, a second strand that is a single scene rather than a clock, a mid-story reversal that changes the question's terms.
- **Cannot:** three strands, a subplot with its own climax, sustained worldbuilding that is not load-bearing.
- **Characteristic failure:** the sag — the middle repeats the first movement at a higher pitch, because the draft has one turn available and the length demands two.
- **Structural consequence:** plan two turns and make the second change what the story is *about*, not merely how much is at stake. A second turn that only raises the price is a first turn written twice.

### Novella (17,000–40,000 words)

- **Can carry:** one impossible situation examined from several positions; a small cast; a voice with leisure to think; a moral problem that events cannot settle.
- **Cannot:** a plot of many causally independent events, or a subplot with its own clock — the common exception is a subplot that is a single sustained relationship.
- **Characteristic failure:** the padded novella — a short story's spine inflated with scene-setting until it reaches a marketable length; or a novel's first act sold as a whole.
- **Structural consequence:** organise around the situation rather than the plot. The same pressure is applied from different positions, and each pass costs the protagonist something the previous pass did not. The form rewards a voice that can hold attention without a new event, which is why it is the form most dependent on [L3]. Route: W2, not W3; the act machinery of a novel will inflate it.

### Novel (40,000 and up)

- **Can carry:** two or three strands, a cast with independent goals, a world that changes over time, an argument that needs counter-cases to be honest.
- **Cannot:** a single question stretched without new material. The novel's enemy is redundancy, not brevity: it fails by repeating, not by running short.
- **Characteristic failure:** a middle assembled from subplots that never cross the spine, and a protagonist whose goal is restated for two hundred pages while the situation stays fixed.
- **Structural consequence:** every strand gets its own clock and at least two crossings; proportion is decided by weight, not by symmetry. A novel whose first act is half its length is not malformed if the first act is the argument.

### Serial

- **Can carry:** accumulation — returning characters, a world that accretes detail, questions that can be handed over more than once, a reader who arrives with memory.
- **Cannot:** a single irreversible climax before the end of the run, tight closure per instalment, or a protagonist who changes permanently in episode two.
- **Characteristic failure:** the reset — each instalment restores the status quo, so nothing accumulates and the reader's memory earns nothing; or escalation without restoration, which burns the premise in a season.
- **Structural consequence:** two questions per unit — one closed within the instalment, one open across the run — and a ratchet: something irreversible changes each time, and the next unit has to live with it. The closed question is what makes an instalment feel finished; the ratchet is what makes the series worth continuing.

Read the bands as a warning about question count. Flash holds one question with one turn; a short story holds one with two; a novelette holds one that changes terms; a novella holds one that cannot be answered by events; a novel holds two or three that answer each other. When a draft feels crowded, the fix is usually a smaller question, not a longer form.

Where a form's length actually goes, once the question count is right:

| Form | Live questions | Turns | Strands that pay | Where the length goes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flash | one | one | none | the turn |
| Short story | one | two | one, crossing once | the pressure around the turn |
| Novelette | one, redefined midway | two to three | two, one sustained | the second movement |
| Novella | one that events cannot settle | one or two | two, both sustained | the examination, not the plot |
| Novel | two or three that answer each other | four or more | three | the middle, which is the argument |
| Serial | one per unit, one across the run | one per unit | two or more | accumulation and ratchet |

The table's useful column is the last. A novelette that spends its length on the first movement will sag in the middle; a novella that spends its length on plot will read as a padded short story; a novel that spends its length on the first act will be praised for its opening and abandoned at chapter nine.

The same event can be a story or a novella, and the difference is not length but what the form asks the reader to do. As a story, the event is a turn, and the reader supplies the before and the after. As a novella, the event is a condition, and the reader is asked to live inside it long enough to feel that there is no way out. Deciding which you have is a question about the reader's task, and it settles the scene count without anyone counting.

Wrong-form diagnosis is usually a mismatch between the number of questions and the number of turns. A draft with one question and three turns is repeating itself: the third turn answers a question nobody asked. A draft with two questions and one turn has imported a subplot it cannot afford, and the second question will be dropped or resolved by assertion. Count the questions the draft actually asks — not the ones the plan lists — then count the turns that change an answer; the two counts should match the row.

## Genre as a contract

A genre is a promise about which questions will be answered, which pleasures will be delivered, and what the reader will be asked to tolerate in exchange. The promise is made in the first pages by signals the reader already knows — a body, a starship, a marriage, a manor — and it is kept or broken at the ending.

| Genre | Promise | Load-bearing conventions | Legitimate subversions | Failure when the promise is broken |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Literary | The question will be examined, not discharged; the reader's judgement is the point | Depth of interiority; ambiguity held under pressure; cost that is not converted into plot resolution | A genre engine as the vehicle; unreliable narration; an ending that refuses to convert | Vagueness offered as depth; a plot that resolves what the book promised to examine |
| Crime / detective | The world is legible; the truth is discoverable and will be delivered | Fair play — the reader has the evidence; a method that can be watched; a solution that re-reads the book | The investigator is the culprit; the case is unsolvable and the book is about the failure | A solution the reader could not have reached; brilliance replacing evidence |
| Thriller | Danger on a clock; competence under pressure | A threat that moves; a protagonist forced to act; narrowing options; a villain with agency | The threat is administrative or internal; the protagonist is the danger | A threat that waits; a clock nobody in the story enforces; luck where competence was promised |
| Horror | The reader will be made unsafe, and the unsafe thing will not be fully available | Atmosphere; an intrusion that resists explanation; a cost paid in body or community; withholding | The monster explained and pitied, if the pity is worse; horror located in the ordinary | Explanation arriving early and deflating the dread; gore substituting for violation |
| SFF | A world whose rules hold, and a question that could only be asked here | One or two novums; rules demonstrated, then tested; consequences followed past comfort | The novum is small and domestic; the rules themselves are the antagonist | Worldbuilding as furniture — rules displayed, never load-bearing; our world with a hat |
| Romance | Two people, a barrier, and a change in that barrier the reader can believe | Mutual attraction early; an internal barrier as well as an external one; a crisis that separates; a mutual choice at the end | Queer reframings of the pair; an ending that keeps the love and loses the couple, signalled early as the contract | The barrier is only external, so a conversation dissolves it; a couple compatible from page one, with nothing to earn |
| Historical | The past as a world with its own conditions, not a costume | Period constraints that bind the plot — money, law, medicine, transport; language without pastiche; a plot that needs the period | An explicit anachronistic consciousness; a documented figure kept in the margin | Research displayed as exposition; modern psychology in period dress; a book that could be moved to the present unchanged |
| Coming-of-age | A change in the protagonist's model of the world, earned by experience, costing the earlier self | An adult world seen from below; a first serious choice; the loss of a relationship or belief | The change runs backwards and the protagonist chooses the childhood belief deliberately; the adult world is the one that is wrong | The lesson announced; the change granted by a single event; nostalgia standing in for judgement |
| Satire | The target will be held to its own logic until the logic breaks | A norm the satire measures against; exaggeration that is internally consistent; a target with power | The satire turns on the reader; the target is the narrator | The target named rather than embodied; random exaggeration; no norm, so the mockery is only cruelty |
| Weird | The world is presented as real and will not explain itself | A matter-of-fact tone; an intrusion the characters treat as normal; a logic that holds but is not ours | The weird is finally explained, if the explanation is worse than the intrusion | Strangeness as decoration — no internal rule, so nothing can be violated and nothing unsettles |

Read the table from the last column backwards. The failure column is what "it doesn't quite work" means in each genre, and it is more useful than the conventions column, because conventions are satisfied by imitation while failures are only visible in the reading.

**The contract is offered by signal, not declaration.** Nothing has to be labelled: a corpse and a detective's wit, a rule and a machine that obeys it, two people who notice each other across a room. What matters is that the signal is not withdrawn. A book that opens like a thriller, spends its middle on a marriage, and resolves neither has not written a hybrid; it has mislabelled itself, and the reader's irritation belongs to the mislabelling rather than to the marriage.

**Contracts are historical.** The fair-play convention hardened in one period of crime writing and has been loosening since; the romance contract now admits endings an older market refused; horror's tolerance for explanation moves with fashion. You are always writing into a *version* of a genre. Choose the version — the contemporary market's, an older one's, or an explicit crossbreed — and keep the signals consistent, or the reader will apply the wrong contract and find the book deficient by its terms.

Three broken promises, briefly:

- A crime novel whose solution depends on a letter the detective read and the reader did not. The book has kept the *shape* of fair play and broken the contract; the reader's sense of being cheated is precise and permanent.
- A romance whose only obstacle is that one party has not yet heard a piece of information. The barrier is external and dissolves in one scene, so the change the genre promises never happens — the couple is the same couple, informed.
- A historical novel in which the period supplies clothes, weather, and a war in the background, but every motive is a contemporary one. It can be set in 2019 with a change of address, and the reader who came for the past is given a costume.

**Breaking a contract on purpose** is legitimate and has a price. The reader forgives a promise broken only when another promise is visibly offered and then paid. *The book is not going to tell you who did it; it is going to tell you what the town did with the not-knowing* — that substitution has to be made early, in the signal, not explained in the last chapter. A late substitution is a broken promise with an excuse attached, and readers can tell the difference because the excuse arrives after the disappointment.

What each genre lets you get away with is part of the contract, and naming it is what frees you to spend effort where the genre is actually auditing.

Crime tolerates coincidence in the crime and none in the solution. Romance tolerates implausible attraction and not implausible change. Horror tolerates a thin account of the world and not a thin account of the fear. SFF tolerates a slow first fifty pages if the rules are worth learning, and not a rule that turns out to be decoration. The tolerance is not a loophole; it is the part of the budget you are allowed to spend badly without the reader minding, which is exactly why it should be spent deliberately.

## Genre and the seven levels

Genre is a weighting, not a subject. It tells you which levels the reader is auditing first, and therefore where a defect becomes a broken promise rather than a blemish. Use this to direct revision effort; do not use it to skip a level.

| Genre | Weight sits at | What a failure there looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Literary | L3, L4, L7 — voice, subtext, composition | A beautiful surface with nothing underneath; the theme stated in the last paragraph |
| Crime / detective | L5 — causal fairness | A solution the reader could not have reached from the evidence given |
| Thriller | L5, L2 — causality and tempo | A chase that does not narrow; a chapter that exists to raise the pulse and change nothing |
| Horror | L3, L4 — atmosphere and withheld meaning | Dread with no object, or an object explained too soon |
| SFF | L4, L5 — the idea and its consequences | A novum that decorates; rules that never constrain anyone |
| Romance | L6 — the change in the barrier between two people | Attraction asserted, barrier external, change unearned |
| Historical | L1, L3 — the material world and its diction | Anachronism in objects and, worse, in minds |
| Coming-of-age | L6, L4 — motive and the change of model | The lesson announced; the change granted |
| Satire | L4, L7 — target and structure | The target named in dialogue; the structure with no norm behind it |
| Weird | L3, L4 — tone and the unavailability of meaning | The strange becomes decorative, so nothing can be violated |

The practical use is triage. If a thriller is failing at L1, fixing the prose will not save it; if a literary novel is failing at L5, no amount of voice will hide the missing cost. The table tells you where to look first, and the L1–L7 ladder in `references/quality-levels.md` tells you what you are looking at.

**Weighting is not permission.** A genre can keep its contract at the load-bearing level and be weak everywhere else — a fair-play crime novel with dead prose still delivers its promise, and is still a lesser book. The weighting distinguishes a *contractual* failure from a *craft* failure: the first ends the reader's trust, the second lowers the ceiling. A revision plan needs both, and the contractual one comes first because it cannot be compensated.

**Where the table misleads.** It tempts you to read a genre's promise as licence to neglect a level. Horror is not excused from causality; SFF is not excused from psychology; romance is not excused from prose. The weighted levels are the ones a genre's readers audit consciously. The others are audited unconsciously, and they fail the book quietly — which is why a horror novel with beautiful dread and no causal spine is praised in reviews and not finished by readers.

Two drafts, same genre, opposite diagnoses. The first is a procedural with an impeccable timeline and a detective who wants nothing in particular: it keeps its contract and fails at L6, and the repair is a character, not a plot. The second has a vivid detective, a persuasive voice, and a solution that depends on a fact the reader never had: it fails at L5, which is a contractual failure, and no amount of L3 work will make it a crime novel. Naming which of these you have decides whether the next month is character work or structural work.

The levels are also how a genre's readers talk. *It isn't really horror* usually means L4 failed: the dread had no object. *It isn't really crime* means L5 failed. Hearing the level inside the complaint is the fastest route from a reader's reaction to a repair.

## Cross-genre and hybrid work

A hybrid keeps two contracts. The rule that makes this possible: **one contract must dominate at the ending**, because the ending is where a promise is either kept or broken, and two promises cannot both be discharged in the same gesture without one of them becoming the price of the other.

Four working rules:

1. **Share a question.** The two genres must be able to bear on the same question. Crime and literary share "can the truth be borne"; horror and coming-of-age share "what does the change cost"; SFF and romance share "can this world allow this bond". If the two contracts answer different questions, the book will feel like two books taking turns.
2. **Decide the dominant before drafting.** Write the ending first and ask which contract it keeps. A crime novel that ends with the detective's moral exhaustion has kept the literary contract and used crime as the vehicle; the reverse is also legitimate, but the ending must then close the case and let the moral cost be the price of the closure.
3. **Let the subordinate genre supply the material, not the verdict.** The subordinate contract governs texture, pacing, and the kind of scene available. The dominant one decides what the last page means.
4. **Do not average.** A 50/50 hybrid reads as neither, because each genre's conventions are visible enough to raise expectations and neither is paid. The reader does not experience balance; they experience two unfinished books.

Test the hybrid on its pitch. If you cannot say in one sentence which promise the book keeps — *it is a ghost story about a family that cannot mourn*, *it is a marriage novel with a murder in it* — you have not chosen a dominant, and the draft will choose one for you at whatever point it runs out of energy, which is usually the ending.

A hybrid has two readerships, and they will not be served equally. The genre reader comes for the contract; the literary reader comes for the examination. Serve the first and gesture at the second, and the genre reader is satisfied while the literary reader calls the book thin; reverse it and the genre reader finds the book pretentious. Neither reaction is a verdict on quality. Both are predictions that follow from which promise the ending kept — so decide whose disappointment you can live with, because one of them is coming.

Structurally, a hybrid needs at least one scene where both contracts are active at once: the investigation that is also the marriage scene, the haunting that is also the diagnosis. Without such a scene the book alternates between genres rather than combining them, and the reader experiences the seams as a change of subject. One double scene early is worth more than a hundred pages of balanced alternation, because it teaches the reader that the two promises are one promise seen twice.

A third genre in the mix is almost always texture. A crime novel with a ghost and a love story does not hold three contracts; it holds one and borrows two atmospheres. Count contracts by counting the questions the ending has to answer, and if the count is above two, one of them is decoration — usually the one with the most atmospheric scenes and the least cost.

A hybrid that works: a horror novel about a family that moves into a house where the mother's illness is the thing in the walls. The horror contract supplies the withholding and the unsafe domestic space; the coming-of-age contract supplies the question (what the child must lose to become the person who can leave). One contract dominates at the ending: the house is not explained, so horror wins, and the child's change is the cost of the unresolved thing rather than its solution.

A hybrid that fails: a thriller about a detective investigating a murder in a small town where the real subject is her grief, and the last chapter resolves the murder, explains the grief, and closes both. Two promises discharged in one gesture, neither paid for, which is why the ending feels like a summary of two books rather than the end of one.

## Genre is not a mould

The argument below is about the use of genre knowledge, not its possession. Knowing the conventions is necessary — a writer who does not know what the contract promises cannot keep or break it deliberately. The failure is treating knowledge of the average as knowledge of the story.

- **Trope-checking selects for recognition.** The pleasure of spotting a convention is the pleasure of having read before, not the pleasure the genre promises. A draft assembled from recognised parts gives the reader the shape of an experience and none of the experience, which is why it can pass a genre quiz and still be forgettable.
- **Conventions are debts, not contents.** "A locked room" is not a thing that must appear; it is a promise that the solution will be reachable from the room. Filling the slot without paying the debt is the characteristic failure, and it is invisible to a checklist because the checklist only asks whether the slot is filled.
- **The modal version of a trope is the version the reader has already read.** If you can name the trope before you write the scene, you are about to write the average of its instances. The alternative is not to avoid the trope but to find the specific pressure in this story that makes one of its variants necessary.
- **Tropes carry the genre's argument.** The locked room is an argument about legibility; the monster is an argument about what a community refuses to see; the marriage plot is an argument about whether another person can be known. Using the trope without the argument produces the shape of meaning and none of it — which reads as competent and hollow in exactly the way readers describe as "generic".
- **Genre competence is the floor.** "It works as a thriller" is an entry ticket, not a verdict. What makes a good thriller is the thing only this book can do inside the contract — and that thing is never a trope, because a trope is by definition shared.

Genre literacy has risen, which changes the calculation. Readers know their genre's conventions well enough to predict them, so a convention no longer delivers surprise. The response is not novelty — a new convention is still a convention — but specificity: the detail that could only belong to this story, placed inside the old shape. That detail is what makes a familiar structure feel like a discovery rather than a rerun.

Use a trope list as a diagnostic after the draft, never as a checklist before it. Run it in three steps: list the conventions the draft actually uses; for each, write the debt it owes the reader; then check whether the scene using it pays the debt or only displays the convention. Three or four entries is enough. A draft with twenty conventions and no debts paid is a tour of the genre; a draft with three and all of them paid is in the genre.

The same rule applies here as to plot grammar in `references/narrative-theory.md`: a description of existing stories cannot tell you what a new one needs, and a draft that satisfies every convention is recognisable precisely because it is complete.

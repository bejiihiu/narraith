## Why continuity is a craft problem, not a filing problem

A contradiction does not read as a mistake about a fact. It reads as a mistake about the narrator.

When a reader opens a story they extend a credit they were not asked for: *I will treat what you tell me as true and I will not check.* This is not credulity. It is the labour-saving device that makes fiction possible, because attention spent verifying is attention not spent inferring, and inference is the whole activity. Continuity is the interest on that credit. Miss a payment and the reader starts auditing.

The asymmetry is what makes this a craft problem rather than a tidiness problem. A break on page 200 does not cost one fact. It re-prices the preceding 199 pages. Every earlier sentence the reader accepted without checking becomes a candidate for the same failure, and a reader who has been made a fool once begins doing the one thing that ruins reading. So the cost of an error is not proportional to the error. It is proportional to everything that was built on the assumption that the narrator is accurate — which is the entire work.

This is also why continuity errors feel worse than they logically should. A changed eye colour is nothing; readers will mention it for years. They are not counting fingers. They are reporting that the floor moved.

The wrong lesson is "be more careful." Carefulness is not a mechanism, and it fails exactly where it is needed, because the errors are not made where the writer is being careless. They are made where the writer is inventing. Planned facts almost never break: you decided the age, you remember the age. What breaks is the detail improvised inside a sentence to make it concrete — the brother's name, the number of floors, whether the car is manual — because that detail was generated and never stored. Continuity work is therefore not a review activity. It is a capture activity, performed at the moment of invention, and its whole design problem is to cost almost nothing at that moment.

Continuity is also not three things it gets confused with, and the confusion produces the wrong repair.

It is not **accuracy about the real world**. A story set in 1911 that gets the currency wrong has a research problem, not a continuity problem: no earlier sentence of the story is damaged, and the reader's loss is authority rather than trust. Research errors are repaired by looking things up. Continuity errors are repaired by deciding which of two statements is true, which is why they are harder.

It is not **stylistic consistency**. A narrator whose register shifts between chapters is not breaking the world; they are breaking the voice, and the repair is in `references/prose-engineering.md`. The distinction matters because a chapter written in the wrong register reads, to a writer, exactly like a chapter written from the wrong facts — both feel like *this is not the same book* — and the fixes have nothing in common.

It is not **plausibility**. A character surviving a fall that would kill a person is implausible; a character surviving the same fall in chapter 3 and dying of it in chapter 9 is a contradiction. Plausibility is a negotiation with the reader's model of reality, and a work may set that model wherever it likes as long as it holds. Continuity is the holding.

One exception governs everything below. Inconsistency is not always a defect. When the divergence between what the text says and what is true is the effect the work is built on, the contract changes from *the world is consistent* to *the narrator is not*, and that is a legitimate contract. It only holds if the reader can tell the difference, which is a marking problem, not a filing problem (§9).

## The four ledgers

Four lists, because there are four kinds of thing that can be contradicted: what is the case, who knows it, what is owed, and what things are called. Everything else is a special case of one of these.

They share one admission rule, and the rule is the difference between a ledger that gets used and a bible that gets abandoned in week three.

**An entry earns its place if it can contradict something.** Test each candidate: could two sentences in this work disagree about it? If not, it is atmosphere, not fact, and recording it makes the ledger longer without making it truer. *Left hand, two fingers gone at the second knuckle* is an entry. *Has a difficult relationship with his father* is not — not because it is unimportant, but because it cannot be contradicted, so it belongs in the character bible where it can be interpreted, not in the continuity ledger where it can only be checked.

Two supporting tests. Has the plot made it load-bearing, or has the reader been shown it twice? And: could a reader catch the error? A fact no reader can catch and no later choice depends on is a note to yourself. Keep it if it helps you write; do not pretend it is continuity.

The ledgers have a shape worth fixing early, because shape is what makes them get read. One table per ledger, one line per entry, sorted by entity rather than by chapter — the same reason the sweep sorts by entity: contradictions are adjacent in entity order and invisible in narrative order. No prose in an entry. An entry that needs a sentence of explanation to be understood needs two entries. `templates/continuity-ledger.md` is the four tables already shaped this way.

Do not build the ledger backward from a finished manuscript unless you must. The bootstrap that works is forward from the point where you are writing: fill in the rows for the characters, objects and days in the chapter you are about to write, then add what you improvise. A bible compiled from a whole draft is accurate about a text you have already finished, which is the least useful moment to be accurate.

**Facts.** Anything with a value that can be stated twice: dates, ages, durations, distances, injuries and their healing, physical descriptions, season and weather, the layout of a recurring place, who is alive. Record values, not descriptions — `left hand: two fingers missing at second knuckle (ch. 3)`, never *badly injured in the war*. Record only the properties another sentence could contradict. Update the moment the detail is invented, in the same sitting, even when it feels too small to matter, because it will not feel small in chapter nineteen. This ledger prevents the retroactive audit: the reader discovering that the narrator does not know their own book.

**Knowledge.** For each named character, the facts they hold, how they came by them, and when. Only facts whose possession changes behaviour: a secret, a death, an identity, a betrayal, a location, a debt. Not *knows the city*. Update at the end of the scene in which they learn it, with that scene as the source. This ledger prevents the character who acts on information they have not received — a failure that is worse than it looks, because it silently deletes the scene that was supposed to deliver the information. The scene still exists on the page; it has simply lost its reason to.

**Debts.** Promises, threats, bargains, favours, unanswered questions, things said in anger that were heard by the right person. Record the obligation, who holds it, and what would discharge it. Update when incurred and when discharged, with the chapter. This ledger prevents the forgotten promise — the most common complaint about long fiction, and the one readers make most confidently, because a promise is free to make and readers track promises involuntarily whether or not the writer does.

**Names.** Exact spellings; forms of address; who is permitted which form; titles; place names; the name a character refuses to say. Record the string and the social rule attached to it. Update on first use, and again on any change of relationship, because a shift from surname to first name is a plot event, not a typo fix. This ledger prevents two failures: spelling drift, and the subtler one — an intimacy appearing in dialogue before the relationship has earned it.

| Ledger | Schematic row | What it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| Facts | `[CHARACTER].left_hand = two fingers missing, second knuckle · established ch. 3` | the narrator who does not know their own book |
| Knowledge | `[CHARACTER] knows [SECRET] · source: [WITNESS], ch. 7 · nothing before ch. 7` | a character acting on what they have not been told |
| Debts | `[CHARACTER] promised [OTHER] [SPECIFIC ACT] · ch. 5 · outstanding` | the promise the reader remembers and the book forgets |
| Names | `[CHARACTER] calls [OTHER] by surname only · until ch. 11` | spelling drift; unearned intimacy |

## The knowledge problem is the hard one

Facts are static. Knowledge is dynamic, and it is per-character, which means the ledger is not a list but a matrix: character × fact × chapter. Written out in full, that matrix is unusable — most cells are empty and every scene invalidates a column. So do not write it out in full. Write the cells that moved.

The reason knowledge is hard is that four different states get collapsed into the word *knows*, and they behave differently on the page. A character **knows** something when they have a source. They **believe** something when they act as though it is true without a source — belief is what behaviour runs on, and it is where most scenes actually live. They **suspect** when a piece of knowledge arrives with a probability attached, which is the engine of investigation plots. They are **wrong about** something, which is where plot comes from, because a wrong belief produces action and action produces consequence. A scene written from *knows* is exposition. A scene written from *believes* or *is wrong about* is drama.

The reader is a second column with the same four states, and the combinations are scene types. Reader knows and character knows: procedure, and it needs something else to hold it up. Reader knows and character does not: dramatic irony, and it is cruel and cheap and works. Character knows and reader does not: mystery or surprise, depending on whether the reader was given a fair chance. Neither knows: discovery, the only one that lets the reader and the character arrive together, which is why it is the most satisfying and the least used.

You do not need the matrix. You need one line per character, kept current: *as of ch. N — knows: …; believes: …; wrong about: …* Before writing a scene, read the lines for everyone present and answer three questions for each of them.

1. **What do they know, and from where?** If you cannot name the source, you have a leak — the information arrived because the scene wanted it.
2. **What do they believe that has not been established?** This is what they will act on. It is usually more interesting than what they know.
3. **What are they wrong about?** If the answer is nothing, the character is currently a passenger, and the scene will have to be carried by someone else.

Every knowledge entry carries a source and a time. `Knows about the affair — from [WITNESS], ch. 6, confirmed by the photograph, ch. 9` is an entry. `Knows about the affair` is a rumour you are telling yourself.

Run against one scene, the three questions produce something like this — schematic, in the shape the answers actually take:

```
scene: [PLACE], [WHEN], [CHARACTER A] and [CHARACTER B]
[CHARACTER A]  knows:  [FACT 1] (source: [SCENE], ch. 4)
               believes: that [CHARACTER B] came back for [REASON]
               wrong about: [FACT 2] — the opposite is true, and the reader knows it
[CHARACTER B]  knows:  [FACT 1], plus [FACT 3] which A does not have
               believes: that A already knows [FACT 3]
               wrong about: nothing that matters yet
consequence: B's belief is the scene's engine. A's wrongness is the scene's cost.
```

The line that earns its keep is almost never the first one. It is *wrong about*. A scene with two well-informed characters and no error between them is a briefing.

Leaks have four shapes, and naming the shape is most of the repair.

- **The scene-arrival.** A fact enters in dialogue because this scene needs it, and no earlier scene delivered it. Repair: either move the delivery earlier or cut the fact's use here.
- **The retroactive source.** The character *always knew*, established by a line of backstory invented at the moment of need. Repair: this is usually a plot problem wearing a continuity costume — the information was needed here because the earlier scene that should have carried it was cut or never written.
- **Off-page osmosis.** Something happened elsewhere and everyone in the room knows, with no telling scene and no witness (§6). Repair: name the witness, or make the ignorance a feature.
- **The narrator leak.** The narration reports what the focal character cannot perceive — a fact from a room they were not in. This one is not continuity at all but point of view, and it belongs to `references/prose-engineering.md`; it is listed here because the symptom is indistinguishable to a reader, who simply concludes the narrator knows too much.

A note specific to writing with a model `[hypothesis]`: when the whole manuscript sits in one context, knowledge tends to distribute itself evenly. Every character appears to know whatever the current scene needs, because the information is equally available to the writer of every line. The symptom is a cast that feels omniscient and frictionless, and it is not a plotting error — it is a continuity error that reads as a style. The three questions are the cheapest available counter-measure, because they force the scene to be written from inside one character's partial view rather than from above the whole text.

## Time

Keep anchors, not a calendar.

A calendar invites arithmetic, and arithmetic is where the errors are. It also dies on revision: cut one chapter and every subsequent date is wrong, and the wrongness is invisible until someone counts. A small set of immovable anchors — three to seven named points like *the funeral*, *the election*, *the first frost*, *the night the car burned* — with everything else stated relative to the nearest one, degrades gracefully. *Two days after the funeral* survives the deletion of two neighbouring chapters. *14 March* does not survive anything.

Three arithmetic traps do most of the damage.

**Age.** Store the birth year, not the age, and compute only when a scene needs the number. The classic error — a character who is thirty-four in chapter 2 and thirty-one in chapter 9 — happens because an age was recorded at a moment and the story year moved afterwards without the subtraction being redone. If a work spans years, put the spans in the timeline as years, not as ages.

**Duration.** Pregnancy, illness, a journey, a prison sentence, a courtship, a war. Durations are always longer than the plot wants them to be, because the writer experiences three chapters as three months and the calendar says eleven days. Record elapsed time as a number of days since the last anchor. Vague connectives — *some weeks later*, *a while after* — are durational lies waiting to be caught, and they are caught, because readers who have been given an anchor can count.

**Rate.** Healing, learning, recovery, weight loss, the thaw of a relationship. A body that recovers on demand is two errors at once: a continuity error and a stakes error, because if damage costs no time it costs nothing. Give each injury three values — severity, capability lost, duration — and then check that no later scene requires a capability that has been removed. The same applies to skill: a character who cannot drive in chapter 4 cannot drive in chapter 6 without a scene that says when they learned.

Season and weather are the cheapest anchors and the most often forgotten. If the work spans a year, fix the season of each act. After that the small details stop being arbitrary: what is on the trees, what people wear, whether the light is still there at six.

Below the calendar sits the clock, and it is wrong more often than any of the three. A scene opens at breakfast and the light is failing by the second page; a dinner party runs eleven hours; a character leaves work at six and arrives somewhere that closed at five. The cause is that scene time is felt rather than counted — two thousand words of tension feels like an evening. Fix it by giving each scene a start time and a duration in the card (`templates/scene-card.md` has the field), then checking that the sequence across a day is possible. This is the cheapest check in the file and it catches the errors readers report first, because every reader has a clock.

| Check | Question | Catches |
|---|---|---|
| birth year against story year | how old is each character at each act boundary? | ages that go backwards |
| days since the last anchor | how much time has passed here? | "some weeks later" that is four days |
| capability ledger | what can each injured or learning character still do? | the broken wrist that types in chapter 6 |
| season per act | which season is each act in? | a harvest in the same month as the first frost |
| travel time, one way | how long does the journey take? | a country crossed between two scenes |
| recurring events | which annual events does the story pass? | two Christmases, one winter |

The timeline is keyed to narrative position, not to story time, because a non-linear work is read out of order and the ledger has to be readable in reading order. Two columns, not one: where the reader is, and where the calendar is. `templates/timeline.md` has the columns; `references/temporal-complexity.md` has the reasons the two diverge.

## Objects and money

An object becomes **loaded** when the prose gives it weight: it has been described with specificity, or used, or mentioned twice. Once loaded, it is tracked, and tracking means four things — where it is, who holds it, who knows it exists, and what condition it is in. A loaded object that vanishes is not a detail. Readers keep an inventory of loaded objects without being asked to, which is the same faculty that makes Chekhov's gun work; the gun is not a rule about authors, it is a description of readers.

Two failures follow. An object introduced with specificity and never used reads as a promise, and the reader will wait for it past the end of the book. An object used and then forgotten reads as a hole, and the reader will supply their own explanation, usually a worse one. Neither is fixed by removing the object. Fix by logging it and deciding.

| State | Record | The error it prevents |
|---|---|---|
| held | who has it, and whether they know its significance | the gun that changes hands without a scene |
| known to exist | who knows it exists (§3, not the fact ledger) | the letter everyone reacts to before anyone has read it |
| out of play | where it is and why it is not being used | the object that returns from nowhere in act three |
| destroyed | when, by whom, and who saw | the object that is destroyed and then negotiated over |

Some objects are **evidence objects** — a letter, a photograph, a second phone, a key to a door that should not open — and for these the state table is not enough. Their fact is existence and their drama is knowledge, so they need a row in both ledgers: the object in one, and *who knows it exists* in the other. The commonest version of this error is a possession whose discovery is the plot, where the discovery has already been assumed several scenes earlier by everyone in the room.

Money is the most common continuity failure in realistic fiction, for one reason: it is the only system in the book with public arithmetic. Readers know what rent costs. An error in money is instantly checkable in a way that an error in the geography of a fictional county is not, so it converts directly into the suspicion that the narrator has never been poor, never been rich, or never been anywhere.

Keep three anchors — income, the largest fixed cost, one debt — and a running balance only where the plot actually constrains it. Check a purchase against the anchors, not against a transaction ledger; the ledger is a trap that produces bookkeeping instead of prose. Two failure modes to watch: the unfelt constraint, where a character is broke and nothing they do is ever stopped by being broke (this is also a stakes failure — poverty that costs nothing is decoration), and the sum that does not add up, where the monthly figures are individually plausible and collectively impossible with no gap accounted for.

Money is also characterisation, which is why these errors do damage out of proportion. What a person spends on, refuses to spend on, lends without asking, or hides is an argument about who they are. A money inconsistency is usually an [L6] error wearing [L5] clothes.

## Off-page events

Anything that happened and was not narrated. Weddings, arrests, diagnoses, a conversation between two other characters, the whole of a war.

The rule is short: **decide an off-page event before you reference it.** An event invented at the moment of reference is optimised to fit that reference and nothing else, and it will contradict the calendar, the knowledge ledger, or somebody's capability within a chapter or two. Decide it in one sentence — what happened, when, where — plus two fields that get forgotten.

**Witnesses.** Who was there, and therefore who knows. An off-page event is always also a knowledge event, for everyone present and everyone told, and the telling is itself an off-page event with its own witnesses.

**Versions.** Off-page events reach the story as reports, and reports are interested. Two characters who were both present should not describe the event identically unless they have coordinated, and the difference between their accounts is free characterisation and free information control: the same night, two tellings, one detail moved, and the reader now knows something about both people without either being described.

There is a budget on this, and it is a budget of belief rather than of pages. An off-page event costs the reader a model they never saw built, so it can establish a condition cheaply — a war is on, the mother has died, the firm has been sold — and it can only carry a **turn** at a price. The price is a scene in which someone who was absent pays for it. If the event turns the plot and nobody on the page is changed by having missed it, the turn has happened off-page to spare the writer the scene, and the reader reads that as convenience, which is law 3 failing at the level of the paragraph rather than the plot.

The inverse is worth knowing too. An event that has already been narrated on-page should not be re-narrated later in summary as though the reader had been elsewhere. That recap is the same failure in reverse: you are telling the reader something they watched, in a voice that assumes they did not.

## Continuity in long works and series

A bible begins as a plan and then, without anyone deciding it, becomes a claim about a published text. Those are different documents with different rules, and keeping them in one file is how bibles start lying.

A **plan** can be edited freely. A **published claim** can only be retconned, because the reader already has it. So every entry carries two pieces of metadata and no prose: the chapter that established it, and a status.

- `established` — on the page, load-bearing. Changing it is a retcon with a cost.
- `provisional` — planned, not yet written. Free to change, and it should be changed whenever a better idea arrives.
- `retired` — was true in published text, no longer operative. See below.

Without the status, you will quietly amend a planned fact that has already appeared in print and never notice, because the file said so and the file is where you look.

```
entry:  [ENTITY].[ATTRIBUTE] = [VALUE]
status: established | provisional | retired
since:  ch. [N]
note:   [one line, only where a later chapter complicates the look of it]
```

Four fields and no prose. Note what `since` means: not when you decided the fact, but when the reader could first have known it. Those two dates diverge constantly, and the divergence is exactly where a planned fact crosses the line into a published claim without anyone noticing the crossing.

**Retirement, not deletion.** You cannot delete an established fact; the reader holds it. You retire it: add the replacement, and a note of what the text actually says. Sometimes the replacement lives in the world rather than in the file — *she was lying about her age* — and that is a retcon. Its cost is that the reader must now accept that the narrator was wrong earlier, which only works if the narrator has already been established as capable of being wrong (§9). Otherwise you have spent the reader's credit to fix your own arithmetic, which is the worst trade available.

**Series.** Two readers matter and neither is you. The first is someone picking up book three without books one and two: the work needs a way to re-establish a load-bearing fact without re-explaining it, and the ledger needs a per-volume line for *what the reader knows at the end of this book*. The second is you in three years, with no memory of any of it. Write entries for that person: full names, plain sentences, no abbreviations, no initials, no codes. A bible written in shorthand is a bible that has to be re-derived.

## Contradiction sweep

The procedure, in order, and it is deliberately front-loaded on the expensive step.

**1. Extract.** Go through the text for *predications* about trackable entities: every sentence that asserts a value. Sort as you go into seven buckets — person (body, age, history, possessions), place (layout, distance, travel time), time (date, duration, season), object, money, knowledge, relationship. Do it a chapter at a time; the whole manuscript at once produces a mass of notes and no findings.

**2. Sort by entity.** All assertions about one person or object, together, in reading order. This is the step that makes the sweep possible: contradictions are invisible in narrative order and obvious in entity order.

**3. Pair check.** Read the sorted list looking for pairs that cannot both be true. This is a different reading posture from editing. You are not judging quality and you are not improving anything; you are looking for incompatibility, and the moment you start fixing prose you have stopped sweeping.

**4. Classify.** Three outcomes, three different responses.

- **Contradiction** — an error. Fix the later instance unless the earlier one is less load-bearing: the later one has less built on top of it, and nothing downstream depends on it yet. Never fix a contradiction by adding an explanation. That converts one error into two, and the second one is in the reader's mouth.
- **Retcon** — a deliberate change, usually because a better idea arrived. Propagate it: find every downstream sentence that depended on the old value. The characteristic failure is the partial retcon, where one scene is updated and three are left, and the result is worse than the original error because now the text disagrees with itself in a pattern that looks intentional.
- **Deliberate unreliability** — the narrator's account diverges from the world's. This is not an error, but it must be marked (§9), because unmarked it reads as the first category and the reader will never recover the intended reading.

**5. Sweep the ledger by default, the manuscript on suspicion.** The ledger is cheaper and it is where the improvised facts ended up, so it catches most of the real errors. The manuscript sweep is for the end of a draft, or for when a reader reports something you cannot place. `checklists/continuity-sweep.md` is the pre-flight version of this procedure.

Sorted and paired, the output of a sweep looks like this — the shape matters more than the content:

```
entity: [CHARACTER]
ch. 2   "thirty-four"                → age 34 at [ANCHOR A]
ch. 5   "[N] years since [EVENT]"    → implies birth year [Y]
ch. 9   "thirty-one"                 → age 31 at [ANCHOR B]
pair:   ch. 2 and ch. 9 cannot both be true if A and B are the same year
class:  contradiction · fix ch. 9 · ch. 2 is load-bearing ([SCENE] turns on it)
```

Three lines of entry, one line of pair, one line of classification. A sweep that produces paragraphs has stopped sweeping and started editing.

One addition pair-checking cannot give you: the **negative sweep**. Look for what should be there and is not. A character present in a scene and not mentioned afterwards. A debt never collected. An object last seen in someone's hand. Errors of omission are invisible to a comparison of two assertions, and they are the ones readers report as *I kept waiting for*.

## Continuity and the unreliable narrator

A deliberate inconsistency and an accidental one are identical on the page. The difference exists only in the author's intention, and the reader has no access to intention. So the text has to carry a signal, and the signal has to be readable as design rather than as damage.

What marking is not: an explanation. A final chapter establishing that she had been lying all along converts the novel into a puzzle with an answer key, and it re-reads the reader's experience for them — the one thing a book cannot do and still be the book they read.

Four markings that do not explain.

**Patterned unreliability.** The narrator is precise where precision flatters them and vague where it does not. Money, dates, and other people's faults: exact. The night in question, their own motives, what they were doing: hazy, or suddenly summarized. The pattern is the signal. It needs no statement, and it is the only one of the four that gets better on a second reading — which is the case for writing this way at all.

**A witness.** Another character reacts to a discrepancy and the narrator does not explain it away. One line is enough — *You said there were three of them* — and the refusal to answer is louder than an answer.

**Documentary interference.** A letter, a timestamp, a form, a photograph, a recorded arrival that does not match the narration, left uncommented. The reader who catches it has caught it themselves, which is the only kind of catching that produces belief.

**Repetition with drift.** The same account given twice, differing in one detail. The reader who noticed is rewarded; the reader who did not has lost nothing on the first pass, which is what makes this the safest of the four.

Keep two kinds apart, because they need different signals. The **unreliable** narrator believes what they say — the signal is the pattern, and the reader's job is to notice the shape of the gaps. The **untrustworthy** narrator knows and lies — the signal must be a discrepancy they had a reason to create, and the reader must be able to catch them, or it is not a lie but a plot hole with a defence. Mixed narrators exist, and they need both signals, which is why they are hard.

The question that always follows: confirm it, at the end, or leave it? Both are available and both are legitimate. Confirmation by **recontextualisation** works: the last chapter supplies the fact that makes the earlier account re-readable, and the reader's memory of those scenes changes without any scene being re-explained. Confirmation by **statement** fails, for the same reason §1 gives about explanation generally — it replaces the reader's reading with the author's.

The test between them is one question. After the reveal, do the earlier scenes get *richer*, or merely *correct*? Enriched means the reader now reads the same sentences differently, which is the effect the whole apparatus existed for. Merely corrected means the reader has been handed the answer to an arithmetic puzzle they were never told was a puzzle, and the book shrinks on the second reading instead of growing. If it merely corrects, the reveal is doing continuity work — reconciling statements — and continuity work is exactly what should have happened in the file, not in the final chapter.

One boundary holds regardless. The narrator may be wrong about facts the story has made emotionally load-bearing; the narrator may not be wrong about the rules of the world. If the world itself is unstable, the reader has no surface to stand on, and unreliability stops reading as design and starts reading as the author not having decided. The contract permits a lying narrator. It does not permit a lying physics.

## When to stop tracking

Over-tracking has a real cost, and it is paid in prose. A writer consulting a ledger for every detail produces sentences that verify instead of observe: the description checks out and nobody is looking at anything. The ledger exists to make you safe enough to improvise. When it makes you consult instead of write, it has inverted its function.

The rule: track **what the plot has made load-bearing**, plus **anything the reader has been shown twice**, plus **anything you are about to contradict**. Everything else is optional, and most of it should be skipped. A three-hundred-page novel does not need a complete inventory of its world. It needs the twenty facts that, if wrong, would cost the reader their footing.

The minimal working version takes two minutes and beats a full bible nobody opens. Before writing a chapter, read only the rows that chapter touches — the characters present, the objects in play, the days elapsed. After writing, add only the facts you improvised. That is the whole discipline.

Stop conditions, any one of which means the ledger has grown past its usefulness: it is longer than the outline; you are recording adjectives; updating it costs more than the error it prevents; you have started maintaining it for its own sake. Some errors are also cheaper to leave. A minor detail contradiction, unnoticed by any reader, in a long book, is not worth three days of sweeping. The measure is the reader's trust, not the ledger's completeness — and the reader's trust is lost by the errors they can see, not by the ones you can.

# Voice and complex structure: W12 authorial style, W13 non-linear, W14 multiple plot lines

> Purpose: acquire a voice from evidence, deliver a story out of chronological order on purpose, and run several plot lines without any of them becoming decoration.
> Read when: the request is "write like this", "tell it out of order", or "I have three storylines".
> Related: `../references/prose-engineering.md` — voice at sentence level. `../references/temporal-complexity.md` — order, duration, focalization. `../references/originality.md` — why imitation must become possession.

These three routes change *how* a story is told rather than what happens in it. All three fail the same way: the device becomes the subject. A
pastiche that only works on the sample's own furniture, a chronology jumbled for the pleasure of jumbling, a second plot line that could be deleted
without consequence — each is a technique performing itself.

## W12 — Authorial style

### When to use

The user supplies a sample and asks for that voice: a pastiche, a ghostwritten continuation in a known style, a stylistic exercise, a translation that
must preserve the original's manner. Also: a writer who wants their own voice described precisely enough to reproduce deliberately.

Not for: a request to "sound more literary", which is a request for a register, not a voice, and is answered by W5 and
`../references/prose-engineering.md`.

### Inputs

- A sample. Under ~300 words is enough for syntax and lexicon, not enough for attitude or structure; under ~2,000 words, structural claims about the
  voice are guesses. Say which claims the sample supports.
- The target: new material in that voice, or the same material rewritten. The first is harder and is the real test.
- Whether the author is living. This changes the contract, not the technique — see the ethics note.

### Stages

**1. Extract the profile.** Eight features, each extracted by a procedure rather than an impression.

- **Attention** — what the narrator notices. List the ten nouns in 300 words that carry the most specificity, and the things a scene of that kind would
  normally mention and this one does not. Attention is the strongest and least imitable feature, and the one that survives a change of subject.
- **Syntax** — count sentence lengths in the sample; note the shortest and longest, the fragment rate, where the punctuation gets heavy, whether
  subordination is front-loaded or trailing.
- **Lexicon** — register (Latinate or Germanic), domain words, the verbs the writer reaches for, and the words the sample would never use. A single
  wrong register word is more audible than a wrong image.
- **Figuration** — metaphor density per 100 words, the source domains images come from (weather, machinery, money, animals), whether similes close their
  comparison or leave it open, and which images recur.
- **Attitude** — irony, tenderness, contempt, neutrality; how judgement is signalled; whether the narrator addresses the reader or pretends not to
  exist.
- **Refusals** — what the sample never names, where it cuts, which emotions it reports and which it only stages. Refusals are half of a voice and the
  half that pastiche usually omits.
- **Paragraph shape** — length distribution, white space, dialogue-to-narration ratio, whether paragraphs end on a turn or a trailing clause.
- **Dialogue habits** — tag style, beats, interruption, whether dialect is rendered phonetically, how many speeches before a beat of action.

Artifact: the profile, eight headings, each with one quoted example and one line of description.

**2. Write the Voice Contract.** POV, distance, tense, register, the narrator's attitude, three prohibitions, three obligations. Artifact: the
contract, one page. The prohibitions are the operational part: "no simile that explains itself", "no sentence over 40 words", "never name an emotion
the body has already shown".

**3. Calibrate on 150 words.** Write a neutral passage — a room, a meal, someone waiting — in the voice. Compare feature by feature against the profile.
Adjust one variable at a time; adjusting three produces a paragraph that is different and unaccountably wrong. Artifact: the calibration passage, with
the comparison notes.

**4. Test on material the sample never had.** Write a scene with a new subject, a new character, and a situation absent from the sample. This is the
real test: pastiche works on the sample's furniture, possession works on new material. Artifact: the test scene.

**5. Pastiche versus possession.** Two diagnostics. **Image provenance**: does the new text contain any image the original did not already have? If
every image is borrowed, you have written a collage. **Decision transfer**: given a new scene, would this writer notice the same thing, cut at the
same point, refuse the same explanation? If you can answer yes without consulting the sample, the voice is possessed. Artifact: the verdict, with one
line of evidence per diagnostic.

#### The ethics note

Imitating a living author is legitimate on request and as an exercise. It is not legitimate to present the result as original work, to publish it as
your own, or to pass it off as the author's. Label it: "in the manner of", "pastiche", "exercise". Dead authors are not exempt from the plagiarism
question, only from the consent one; estates and copyrights outlive them, and a pastiche that reproduces distinctive passages is a different act from
one that reproduces a manner.

The technical reason matches the ethical one: pastiche that reproduces the sample's own sentences has produced nothing, and pastiche that reproduces
its decisions has produced a voice. Aim for the second, and the labelling question becomes easy.

### Artifacts

- Style profile, eight features with quoted evidence.
- Voice Contract.
- Calibration passage, 150 words, with the comparison notes.
- Test scene on new material.
- Pastiche/possession verdict.

### Quality gate

1. **All eight features are evidenced**, not asserted. Each has a quotation.
2. **The contract's prohibitions are specific** enough to catch a violation.
3. **The calibration passage is indistinguishable in a blind pair** — read both to someone who has not seen the analysis.
4. **The test scene works on new material**, not only on the sample's props.
5. **At least one image is the new text's own**, and it is consistent with the profile's source domains.
6. **The imitation is labelled**, in the deliverable, not only in the conversation.

### Repair loop

- **It reads as a generic literary voice** → the profile was taken from the sample's subject, not its attention. Re-extract attention from a passage
  where nothing much happens.
- **It reads as parody** → the tics are amplified. Reduce each signature feature by half; parody is pastiche with the volume up.
- **It collapses on new material** → the contract describes manner, not decisions. Ask what this writer would refuse to explain, and cut accordingly.
- **The syntax matches and the sentences still feel wrong** → lexicon. One register word is off; find it by reading the new text against a sample
  paragraph, not against the profile.
- **The user wants the author's actual sentences** → decline the copying and offer the manner. The distinction is the whole value of the route.

### Example route

*"Write a 500-word opening in the manner of this 1,200-word sample about a ferry crossing."* Profile: attention on weather and money, never on faces;
syntax paratactic, fragments at the turn only; lexicon Anglo-Saxon, verbs of motion; figuration one simile per page, from tide and rope; attitude dry,
never ironic about the poor; refusals: no interiority for the husband, no named emotion. Contract: three clauses maximum, no metaphor of light, never
explain a silence. Calibration written on a bus queue, adjusted twice. Test scene: a job interview the sample never had. Verdict: possession — the
interview contains a rope image of its own, and the cut happens where the sample would cut.

## W13 — Non-linear narrative

### When to use

The order of telling is a decision the story needs: a mystery whose solution reframes the opening, a life told against the grain, a structure where
what the reader knows at each point is the source of tension.

Not for: a story that would be clearer chronologically and is being shuffled to seem ambitious. The test in stage 8 exists for this case, and its
answer is usually "use chronology".

### Inputs

- The fabula: the events in causal order, five to nine bare sentences, named agents, active verbs.
- What the reader must feel at each point — suspense, dread, irony, recognition — and which of those the order is supposed to produce.
- The ending, because in a non-linear structure the last segment in delivery order carries the final value, and it need not be the latest in story
  time.

### Stages

**1. Question.** What does the order make the reader know, and when? Non-linearity is an information decision before it is a temporal one: the same
events, delivered in a different order, produce different knowledge at every point. Artifact: one paragraph — the question the reader holds at the
start, and the question the last segment answers.

**2. Order.** Choose the delivery order against the fabula. Name the **present line**: the through-line the reader returns to, which gives them a
place to stand. Decide what the reader knows at the start of each segment, and what they must not yet know. Artifact: the fabula list and the delivery
list, side by side. Identical lists are legal, but only if chronology was chosen.

**3. Anchor design.** Every shift needs a physical anchor that locates the reader within two sentences: an object, a date, a name, a sensory detail, a
change in address. Artifact: one anchor per segment, listed. Labels ("twenty years earlier") are permitted only where the narrating voice would
actually use one; a voice that never explains itself cannot start explaining its own timeline.

**4. Shift map.** A table: segment number, story time, POV, what the reader learns, what question opens or closes, and the transition device into the
next segment. Artifact: the map. This is the document that catches both failures — a segment that teaches nothing, and a transition that only a writer
can follow.

**5. Reconstruction test.** From the map alone, reconstruct the fabula as a reader would, segment by segment. If you cannot, orientation fails and the
anchors are the problem. If the reconstruction is trivially the chronological order with no gain, the order is not doing work. Artifact: the
reconstruction, plus a note on what each reordering bought.

**6. Write.** Write each segment whole, so its voice and its pressure are consistent. Short segments: write in delivery order. Long segments: write in
fabula order, then assemble, because the emotional logic of a long segment is easier to hold chronologically. Artifact: the segments, and the
assembled draft.

**7. Re-read for orientation.** Read only the transitions, one after another. Each must answer three questions: where am I, when am I, and why is the
text here now? A transition that answers only the first two is a cut, not a structure. Artifact: a transition note, one line per shift.

**8. Check that the order is doing work.** Name three things that would be lost chronologically: a suspense the reader would not feel, an irony that
depends on knowing the end, a character introduced before we know what they will do. If you cannot name three, use chronology. Artifact: the three
gains, one line each.

#### Three failures

**Chronology as puzzle.** The reordering is the only interest; once the reader assembles the timeline, nothing remains. Symptom: the story is more fun
to reconstruct than to read. Repair: give the present line its own pressure, so the reader wants the next segment for what happens in it, not for the
piece of the puzzle it supplies.

**Arbitrary shuffling.** The order changes but the information does not: each segment tells the reader something they could have had in sequence. This
is the most common failure and the easiest to miss, because the structure *looks* deliberate. The reconstruction test catches it: if every segment's
knowledge could be delivered in order without loss, the order is decoration.

**The flashback that explains.** A scene from the past arrives to answer the question the present just raised, tidily, and the reader's curiosity is
discharged. This is exposition with a date stamp. Repair: the flashback should either complicate the present — make the present question harder, not
easier — or raise a question of its own that the present cannot answer. A flashback that closes is a footnote.

### Artifacts

- Question paragraph.
- Fabula and delivery lists, side by side.
- Anchor per segment.
- Shift map.
- Reconstruction, with the three gains.
- Transition notes.

### Quality gate

1. **The reader can locate themselves within two sentences of every shift**, without a label doing the work.
2. **Every segment changes the reader's knowledge.** A segment that only confirms is a candidate for cutting or merging.
3. **The reconstruction succeeds** and is not trivial.
4. **Three named gains** over chronology, each specific to this story.
5. **The last segment in delivery order carries the final value**, and it is the right segment for that job even if it is not the latest in story
   time.
6. **No flashback closes the question it was inserted to answer** without opening another.

### Repair loop

- **The reader is lost** → anchors, not explanations. Add an object or a date in the first two sentences; do not add a "meanwhile, years before".
- **The structure feels gimmicky** → the present line has no pressure of its own. Give it a question that the past segments complicate.
- **Every segment is the same length and shape** → the order is doing the work of variety. Vary the segments' function: one discovery, one refusal,
  one consequence.
- **The ending lands flat** → the last segment is chronologically last but emotionally earliest. Move the segment that carries the final value to the
  end, wherever it sits in story time.
- **A flashback explains** → cut its last paragraph and see whether the present scene now has to work harder. Usually it does, and better.
- **The user's outline is chronological and the request is stylistic** → say so. Apply the order test; if it fails, chronology is the answer and the
  request was for novelty.

### Example route

*"Tell this in reverse: a woman returns to the town where her brother disappeared."* Fabula: the arrival, the landlord's lie, the search, the
discovery, the departure. Delivery: discovery first, then the search, then the lie, then the arrival, then the departure last. Present line: the woman
in the car leaving, which frames each segment. Anchors: the brother's jacket, the same bus timetable, the landlord's dog. Reconstruction shows the
reader learns the lie's cost before the lie, which is the gain. One segment — the search — taught nothing and was merged with the discovery. The flashback
to the arrival ends on a question rather than an answer, so the departure still carries the value.

## W14 — Multiple plot lines

### When to use

Two or more lines must share a book: a main plot and a subplot, dual timelines with separate casts, an ensemble where each strand has its own
question. Also a draft that already has several lines and reads as several books.

Not for: a single line with a digression. That is a scene that has not justified itself, and it is W5's problem.

### Inputs

- Each line's protagonist, question, cost and intended ending.
- The book's main question, and which line carries its answer.
- A word budget per line, or the total and a first guess at proportions.

### Stages

**1. Line sheets.** One per line: question, protagonist, cost, its own ending, word budget, first and last appearance. Artifact: the sheets, one page
total. A line without its own ending is a thread, not a line, and will either dangle or be resolved by someone else's climax.

**2. Intersection design.** Every contact between lines does one of four things:

- **Complicate** — one line's solution raises the other's price. The strongest intersection, because both lines gain pressure from one scene.
- **Echo** — the same decision in a different register. The weakest, because it illustrates rather than changes; use it once per book at most.
- **Resolve** — a line ends because another line's action removes its condition. Must be causal: the reader must see the mechanism, not infer that the
  author needed the space.
- **Refuse** — the lines meet and do not merge. Legitimate, and it needs a reason the reader can feel: two people who cannot help each other is a
  subject, two plots that never touch is an editing failure.

Artifact: an intersection map — line, chapter, type, what changes in each line as a result.

**3. Proportion.** Count words per line and the number of scenes in which two lines are simultaneously under pressure. Artifact: a proportion table. A
line that takes 30% of the words and does not change the main answer is a co-main line: either promote it and let it carry part of the ending, or cut
it. There is no third option that reads well.

**4. Convergence, or deliberate non-convergence.** Convergence is not mandatory. If the lines do not meet, the book needs a structural rationale — a
subject about coexistence, say — and a marking: a shared image, a final juxtaposition, a formal rhyme. Artifact: one paragraph stating which, and why.
Non-convergence without a rationale reads as an unfinished draft.

**5. POV allocation.** Each line needs a POV with access to its pressure. Count POV sections per line. A line with one section in the middle of the
book is an orphan: either give it two or more, or fold it into another line's POV as reported action. Artifact: the allocation table.

**6. Write.** Alternate by pressure, not by rotation. Leave each line at its highest unresolved point, and give the reader a re-entry anchor within a
paragraph of returning — an object, a name, a time. A strict rotation (A, B, C, A, B, C) teaches the reader to skim two of the three. Artifact: the
drafted sections, with re-entry anchors marked.

**7. Audit for removable lines.** For each line, delete it in imagination and ask what breaks. If the answer is "nothing, but it's textured", the line
is not a line. If the answer is "the main ending loses its cost", it is load-bearing. Artifact: one line per plot line, with the verdict.

#### The rule

**A subplot must change the answer to the main question or change its price.** An echo of the theme is neither, and the reader will skip it — not
because subplots are unwelcome, but because a line that costs nothing to remove has told the reader it costs nothing to read.

`[data: StoryScope]` 79% of model-written stories have no subplot at all — against 57% of human ones. The correction is not "add one". A book with three decorative lines is
further from a book than a book with one, and the measurement says nothing about how many lines a story should have — only that the default is none.

### Artifacts

- Line sheets.
- Intersection map with types.
- Proportion table.
- Convergence paragraph.
- POV allocation table.
- Removability audit.

### Quality gate

1. **Every line has its own question and its own ending.**
2. **Every line changes the answer or the price**, stated in one line with the chapter where it happens.
3. **Intersections are causal**: the reader can see why one line's action affected the other.
4. **No line climaxes after the main climax.** If one does, the book has two endings and the second is an anticlimax by position.
5. **Proportion matches function.** The line carrying the ending has the words to carry it.
6. **No orphan POV**, and re-entry is anchored within a paragraph.
7. **The removability audit has no "nothing breaks" answers** left unaddressed.

### Repair loop

- **The book reads as three books** → the intersections are echoes, not complications. Convert one echo per pair into a scene where both lines are
  under pressure at once.
- **One line is skippable** → the removability audit. Cut it, or give it the intersection that costs the main line something.
- **The lines converge too neatly** → every line was designed to serve the main one. Give one line an ending the main line cannot use.
- **The middle is a rotation** → alternate by pressure. Find the line with the highest unresolved point and stay there longer than is comfortable.
- **The reader forgets a line between sections** → re-entry anchor missing, or the line has too few sections. Add one of each before adding plot.
- **A subplot takes over the book** → proportion. Either promote it and cut the main line's weakest act, or reduce it to the intersections plus one
  scene of its own.

### Example route

*"A novel with three lines: a mother selling a house, her son's fraud trial, and the tenant who will not leave."* Line questions: can the mother sell
without admitting why; will the son testify; can the tenant stay. Intersections: the tenant's refusal complicates the sale (a viewing the son
attends); the son's testimony resolves the mother's question by making the sale unnecessary; the tenant's line refuses to merge, and ends on the day
of the verdict, in the garden. Proportion: mother 50%, son 35%, tenant 15%, four shared scenes. POV: mother and son only; the tenant is seen from
outside. Removability: the tenant's line breaks the ending — the house unsold, the garden tended — so it stays.

# Continuation and revision routes: W9 continuation, W10 rewrite, W11 deep edit

> Purpose: take an existing text — someone else's, or a weak one of your own — and change it without laundering it into a different book.
> Read when: the request is "continue this", "fix this", or "edit my manuscript".
> Related: `../references/continuity-memory.md` — extraction and sweeps. `../references/editorial-intelligence.md` — pass structure and repair patterns. `../references/literary-critique.md` — the differential diagnosis W10 starts from.

## Contents

All three routes begin the same way and for the same reason: **read before you decide.** A continuation, a rewrite and an edit are all judgements
about an existing object, and the most expensive failure in each is a confident plan built on a misread text.

## W9 — Continuation

### When to use

Someone else's text must go on: a fragment, an unfinished chapter, a premise with two scenes attached, a story the user wrote and cannot finish. Also
your own text after a long gap, where you no longer hold the voice.

Not for: "write the ending" when the user wants a conclusion rather than a continuation — that is a different contract and must be agreed before
writing, because it changes what may be resolved.

### Inputs

- The full existing text. Not a summary, not an excerpt: the voice lives in the sentences, and a summary has already destroyed it.
- How much is wanted: one scene, one chapter, to the end. If unstated, write the next unit and stop; a continuation is a middle.
- Anything the user knows that the text does not say — but treat it as optional, and prefer the text where they conflict.

### Stages

**1. Continuity read.** Read the whole text twice with different attention. First pass: as a reader, for what happens and what it is about. Second
pass: as a clerk, for facts. Artifact: a note of where you stopped reading as a reader — that is the text's weakest joint, and the continuation will
either fix it or inherit it.

**2. Fact, knowledge and debt extraction.** Four lists. **Facts**: dates, ages, weather, injuries, objects, money, distances, names and forms of
address. **Knowledge**: who knows what, and since when — including what the reader knows that a character does not. **Debts**: promises, threats,
unanswered questions, Chekhov's objects. **Unknowns**: things the text implies but never states (a character's age, the season, the name of the town).
Artifact: the four lists, plus a fifth column for line numbers. The unknowns are your degrees of freedom; using one silently is how continuations
acquire facts the original never had.

**3. Infer the Voice Contract.** Six slots, measured rather than admired.

- **Attention** — what the narrator notices and what it skips. Does it name objects precisely and feelings vaguely, or the reverse? Which details get a
  clause and which get a paragraph?
- **Syntax** — sentence length distribution (count the short ones), parataxis or hypotaxis, punctuation habits, whether fragments are used and where.
- **Lexicon** — register, domain words, abstraction level, favourite verbs, words the text would never use.
- **Figuration** — metaphor density, the source domains images come from, whether similes close their comparison or leave it open.
- **Attitude** — irony, tenderness, contempt, neutrality; how judgement is signalled, and whether the narrator ever addresses the reader.

Artifact: the Voice Contract — the five axes, plus three prohibitions and three obligations. The prohibitions are the useful half: "never explains a
feeling twice", "never uses a metaphor of light", "never lets a sentence run past three clauses".

**4. Decide what the text is structurally about.** Not its theme — its question. What is open, what has been promised, what the last unit did to the
state. Artifact: three lines — the question the reader holds, the promise the text has made, the state at the last line. If the text is a fragment with
no promise, say so; the continuation then has to make one, and that is a different job.

**5. Plan the next unit.** Entry state equals the text's current state; the unit answers something small and opens something; the exit lands on a
change. Artifact: one Scene Card, or a chapter contract if the unit is a chapter. Decide the length before writing: a continuation that overshoots
reads as an takeover.

**6. Write.** Match the voice by ear first, then check it by the contract. Keep the first paragraph short: a long opening paragraph in an acquired
voice is where drift shows most. Artifact: the continuation unit.

**7. Check for drift.** Three probes. **Seam**: read the last 200 words of the original and the first 200 of yours without a break; the join should be
invisible in rhythm. **Facts**: diff against the four lists, including the knowledge states — a character cannot know in your scene what they learn
later. **Voice**: sentence length distribution and metaphor density against the contract, not against your impression. Artifact: a drift note, with
each deviation either fixed or declared.

**8. Report assumptions.** Five lines maximum: time elapsed, off-page events, which unknowns you filled, what you took an ambiguous line to mean, and
what you deliberately left untouched. Artifact: the report, five lines.

#### The two prohibitions

**Never resolve a thread the original left open without a reason.** A reason means two things together: the continuation's own question requires the
resolution, and the original supplies the evidence for it. A thread closed because it was convenient is the clearest signature of a continuation that
thinks it is an ending. If the resolution is not earned, touch the thread obliquely — a character avoids the subject, an object moves — and leave it open.

**Never improve the original's style.** No upgrading diction, no fixing deliberate fragments, no adding imagery the author would not have used, no
tidying comma splices that are voice. The continuation's job is to be indistinguishable, not better. Two exceptions, both narrow: a copy-level error
(a misspelling, a name spelled two ways) is fixed silently; a genuine confusion (a contradiction the reader cannot resolve) is reported, not edited.

### Artifacts

- Four extraction lists plus the unknowns column.
- Voice Contract, five axes, three prohibitions, three obligations.
- Three-line structural note: question, promise, state.
- One Scene Card or chapter contract.
- Drift note.
- Assumption report.

### Quality gate

1. **The seam is invisible in rhythm.** Read the join aloud without announcing it.
2. **No fact contradicts the four lists**, including knowledge states.
3. **No new name, object or rule appears** that the original would have had to plant.
4. **The voice matches on all five axes**, measured rather than felt.
5. **Open threads stay open**, or their resolution is argued from the text.
6. **The unit ends on a change**, not on a summary of the original.
7. **The assumptions are declared**, so the user can correct them.

### Repair loop

- **Voice drifts in the second paragraph** → the first paragraph was pastiche of the opening, not of the voice. Re-derive syntax from a random middle
  page, not from page one.
- **A fact contradicts the original** → fix your sentence; if the fact is load-bearing for your unit, re-plan the unit, do not amend the original.
- **The continuation reads as a takeover** → it is resolving too much, or the unit is too long. Cut the last third and end on the change.
- **The continuation reads as filler** → nothing was promised, so nothing can be paid. Make the unit answer a question the text already raised.
- **The user's text is weak and the seam shows it** → do not fix it. Match it, and say in the assumptions that the weakness is inherited.

### Example route

*"Continue this 900-word story about a bus inspector who lets a fare-dodger off."* Continuity read stops at the third paragraph, where the inspector's
wife is introduced and never returns — the weakest joint. Facts: winter, a bad knee, the 07:40. Knowledge: the inspector does not know the boy's name;
the reader does. Voice: short declaratives, attention on hands and timetables, no metaphors of weather, ironic only in dialogue. Contract: never
explains a feeling, never uses a simile, three clauses maximum. Structural note: the question is whether he reports the boy; the promise is that the
knee will matter. One Scene Card: the inspector at the depot, the boy's file on the desk. Seam clean; one fact fixed, two unknowns declared.

## W10 — Rewrite weak text

### When to use

A unit is weak and the user wants it fixed: a chapter that bores, a story that does not land, a scene that reads as flat. The text is the user's, or
of unknown authorship, and the request is repair rather than diagnosis.

Not for: a manuscript (W11); a text whose problem is a single line (fix the line); a request to "make it better" with no unit boundary, which is an
invitation to rewrite the author's book.

### Inputs

- The text, whole.
- The user's complaint in their own words, plus what they want kept. The second is rarely offered and always worth asking for in one line.
- Any constraint: length, genre, audience, a fixed ending.

### Stages

**1. Diagnosis first.** Run the differential from `../references/literary-critique.md`. "It's boring" has at least four distinct causes, and they
require different repairs: no question (structure), a question with no cost (stakes), a cost paid offstage (scene), or a voice with no attitude
(prose). Artifact: the top three defects, each with a level, a quoted line as evidence, and the repair it implies.

Two structural defects and three local ones are the maximum. A list of twelve produces a rewrite that fixes none of them, because the writer cannot hold twelve. Artifact: the ranked list, structural first.

**2. Decide repair, rewrite, or salvage.** Three verdicts, with tests.

- **Repair** — the spine holds: the DQ is live, the cost is real, the ending answers the right question. Failures are at scene level or below. Repair
  costs the least and preserves the most.
- **Rewrite** — the premise, the question or the cost is missing. No amount of prose fixes a story with nothing at stake; the existing text becomes
  source material, not a draft.
- **Salvage** — one or two units work and the rest does not. Keep them as fixed points and rebuild the frame around them.

Artifact: the verdict, one sentence, with the test that produced it.

**3. Preserve what works — in writing.** Three to six items, quoted: a line, a turn, a gesture, a structural choice, a joke. This list is a contract. It
prevents the default failure of rewriting, which is laundering the text into your own style and calling it improvement. Artifact: the preserve list,
with line numbers.

**4. Rewrite by leverage.** Structure first, then scene, then line — the same order as `../SKILL.md`'s triage. The preserve list items are fixed points:
if a structural fix requires deleting one, that is a decision to state, not to make quietly. Artifact: the rewritten text, with the preserve list
checked item by item.

**5. Compare state change and cost.** Four rows, before and after: the question the reader holds; what the reader knows at the end; what the cost was
and who paid it; what changed between the first and last line. If rows three and four are identical, the rewrite failed even if the prose is better.
Artifact: the comparison table.

**6. Report what changed and what was kept.** The user is owed an account: the verdict, the three defects, what was preserved, what was deliberately
not fixed. Two or three lines of prose, no ledger dump. Artifact: the report.

#### Preserve the bad habit if it is a voice

Some of what a diagnostician flags as error is the author's signature: comma splices that speed a sentence at pressure, fragments at the moment of
shock, a digression that recurs whenever a character is lying, adverbs used as a rhythmic beat, one subject over-explained because the narrator cannot
leave it alone.

Three tests separate voice from error:

- **Recurrence with variation.** A habit that appears in different scenes, doing different work, is a style. A habit that appears everywhere
  indiscriminately is a default.
- **Position.** Does it cluster at moments of pressure? A fragment that lands the turn is craft; the same fragment in a paragraph of exposition is
  drift.
- **Removal.** Delete it and read. If the narrator becomes less distinguishable but no less clear, it was voice. If the sentence becomes clearer and
  nothing is lost, it was error.

Neither verdict is permanent: an error repeated often enough becomes a tic, and a tic is not a voice. What makes it a voice is that removing it
changes *who is speaking*, not just how the sentence reads.

The corollary matters more than the rule: **do not standardise dialect, regional syntax, or a character's grammar in dialogue.** Those are the places
where a rewrite most often substitutes the editor's class for the author's.

### Artifacts

- Diagnosis: three defects, level, evidence, implied repair.
- Verdict: repair, rewrite, or salvage.
- Preserve list, quoted.
- Comparison table, four rows.
- Short report.

### Quality gate

1. **The verdict follows from the diagnosis**, not from the user's phrasing of the complaint.
2. **The preserve list survives** in the new text, or its deletion is stated.
3. **The cost is paid on the page** and by a named agent.
4. **The question is answerable only at the end**, and the ending answers the question the text actually raised.
5. **The rewrite is not in your voice.** Compare a paragraph against your own defaults: if it could be swapped into another of your rewrites, you
   laundered it.
6. **Nothing was improved that the author did not ask to improve** — no new subplot, no new character, no theme installed.

### Repair loop

- **The rewrite reads well and means less** → rows three and four of the comparison. Restore the cost; usually the original had one, and the rewrite
  smoothed it away.
- **The diagnosis was wrong** → the repair keeps failing in a new place. Re-run the differential on the *rewritten* text; the defect has moved, which
  means the first one was a symptom.
- **The user rejects the preserve list** → rebuild it with them in one exchange, do not proceed on your own list. The list is the contract.
- **The text resists all repairs** → the verdict was wrong. It is a rewrite or a salvage, and saying so early is cheaper than three failed repairs.
- **The rewrite is longer by 40%** → something was added that the author did not have: a subplot, an explanation, an image system. Cut back to the
  original's scope first.

### Example route

*"This 1,200-word story about a man clearing his mother's flat doesn't land. Fix it."* Diagnosis: the DQ is a chore ("will he finish the flat?"), the
cost is offstage (the estrangement is summarised, never paid), and the voice is uniformly elegiac, including where it should be angry. Verdict:
repair — the turn (finding the second set of keys) is good. Preserve: the keys, the neighbour's line about the boiler, the last image of the empty
wardrobe. Repair: the DQ becomes "will he admit whose keys they are"; the cost moves into the scene with the neighbour; one paragraph of elegy is cut
so the anger has somewhere to land. Comparison shows a new knowledge state at the end; report is three lines.

## W11 — Deep edit

### When to use

A manuscript: 20,000 words and up, or any text too large to hold in one pass. Also a 10,000-word piece with structural problems, if the user wants a
full edit rather than a targeted rewrite.

Not for: a unit (W10); a line edit only, which is the third pass of this route and can be requested alone.

### Inputs

- The manuscript, a word count, and the user's goal: publish, submit, self-publish, or "make it not embarrassing".
- Any prior notes, beta feedback, or rejection letters. They are evidence of symptom, not of cause.
- A time budget. Four passes over 50,000 words is a real cost, and the route should be scoped to it honestly.

### Stages

**0. Read once as a reader.** No notes except one: the place where you stopped wanting to read. Artifact: the stop-list, with page numbers. This is
the only pass where your reaction is data; after this you know too much to react.

**1. Developmental pass.** Whole-book questions only: the spine, the DQ, the cost ladder, act shape, the ending, what each subplot changes. Artifact:
the Revision Plan's first section — defects at L5/L7, each with a location and an action.

**Gate 1.** State the book's question and its answer in two sentences, without notes. Does the ending answer the question the book migrated to? Is the
top cost charged? If any answer is no, stop: the later passes will be wasted, because every scene they polish sits under a decision that will change.

**2. Structural pass.** Chapter and scene level: chapter map audit, scene function, POV allocation, information placement, order. Artifact: defects at
L5/L6 with locations, plus a chapter-level action list (merge, cut, move, re-enter).

**Gate 2.** Every chapter changes a state and leaves a debt. No two consecutive scenes share a function. Every POV section has a reason to exist that
the other POVs cannot supply.

**3. Line pass.** Paragraph and sentence: rhythm by function, voice, image, dialogue, the entry and exit of each scene. Artifact: defects at L3/L2/L1,
worked in place.

**Gate 3.** Read three pages aloud, chosen at random. No sentence that any competent narrator could have written; no dialogue that fails the
tag-strip; no paragraph whose first sentence announces what the paragraph will say.

**4. Copy pass.** Facts, continuity, names, dates, punctuation consistency, typos, chapter numbering. Artifact: a clean list of corrections, and a
contradiction list where the fix is a decision rather than a typo.

**Gate 4.** Ledger diff is clean or the exceptions are listed. Names and forms of address are consistent. No date, distance or injury contradicts.

**5. Final read for rhythm.** Aloud, or subvocalised, straight through. Mark stumbles. Fix only stumbles: no new ideas, no new scenes, no reordering.
Artifact: the clean text, and a list of the stumbles fixed.

#### The Revision Plan

One table, sorted by leverage, not by page order. Columns: defect, level, location, action, status. Plus an explicit **accepted as is** section — things
you decided not to fix, with the reason. The accepted list is not an admission of laziness; it is what stops the same defect being rediscovered in
every pass.

Write the plan before touching the manuscript, and revise it after each gate. A plan that never changes is a plan written after the fact.

#### The regression check

Editing flattens. Each pass removes something as well as fixing something, and the removal is invisible because you were looking at the defect. After
each pass, re-run the same probes and compare:

- the two-sentence statement of question and answer (gate 1);
- three sampled scenes through the scene gate, the same three each time;
- the first 300 words aloud;
- if useful, `../scripts/story_metrics.py` before and after — sentence-length distribution, dialogue share, repetition. These are surface proxies, not a
  quality score; they catch flattening, not failure.

If a probe got worse, the pass over-corrected. The usual cause is line-editing during a structural pass, or "improving" the strangest sentences.

#### Editing 50,000 words without losing the whole

Five mechanisms, all aimed at the same problem: the manuscript is larger than your working memory, and every session you re-enter it slightly wrong.

**The one-page.** A single page: the spine in ten lines, the question and its answer, the promise of the first 50 pages, and the five things that work
and must not be touched. Read it before every session, not once.

**Windows, not scrolling.** Work one act, or one POV line, at a time. Random access produces local fixes that fight each other; a window produces a
consistent altitude.

**A defect log, not memory.** Every observation goes in the log with a page number. Memory of a defect is unreliable after 20,000 words, and the
defect you half-remember is the one you fix twice, differently.

**Version discipline.** `rev-developmental/`, `rev-structural/`, `rev-line/`, `copy/`. Each pass a full copy. When a pass goes wrong — and one will — the
cost is a directory.

**Boundaries that hold.** No new scenes during the line pass. No reordering during copy. No line edits during structure. Each prohibition exists
because the mixed move feels efficient and produces a manuscript with two incompatible intentions.

### Artifacts

- Stop-list.
- Revision Plan: defect, level, location, action, status, accepted-as-is.
- Four gate notes.
- Regression probe results, before and after each pass.
- Copy list and contradiction list.

### Quality gate

1. **All four gates cleared in order**, with the notes to prove it.
2. **The plan's accepted list is explicit**, and nothing on it was silently skipped.
3. **The regression probes did not get worse.** If one did, the pass over-corrected and must be revisited.
4. **The manuscript is not flatter than it was.** Compare two passages you did not intend to touch; if they read more alike than before, the line pass
   normalised them.
5. **The ending still answers the migrated question** after all four passes. Structural edits have a way of re-pointing a book at its opening
   question.
6. **The copy pass left no contradiction** except the listed decisions.

### Repair loop

- **Gate 1 fails** → stop the route. The book needs a developmental rewrite (W3 stage 1), not an edit. Say so plainly; four passes will not save a
  wrong premise.
- **A structural fix breaks three scenes** → the fix is right and the scenes were built on the old decision. Log them as rewrites and do them in the
  same pass; do not defer them to the line pass.
- **The line pass makes everything sound the same** → you are editing toward a house style. Restore the strangest sentence in each chapter and work
  outward from it.
- **Copy pass finds thirty contradictions** → the earlier passes moved facts. Sweep domains rather than pages, and fix by ledger, not by memory.
- **The book is worse after four passes** → compare against the pre-edit copy, not against your memory of it. If it is worse, the developmental
  diagnosis was wrong; the honest move is to revert and re-diagnose, and it is cheaper than a fifth pass.
- **The user wants it published next week** → scope the route: developmental and copy only, and say what was not done.

### Example route

*"Edit my 78,000-word thriller; the middle drags."* Read once: stopped at chapter 14, a third witness interview with the same information as the first
two. Gate 1: the question is "who killed the sister", but the book's actual question by chapter 20 is "was the sister involved" — the ending answers the
first. Structural: chapters 12–18 are three interviews with the same function. Line pass: the detective's voice is uniform, including in the scenes
with his father. Copy: the sister's age contradicts twice. The plan: re-point the ending, merge the interviews into one with escalation, restore the
father scenes' register, fix the age. Two passes, not four, inside the budget; the report says which gate was not run.

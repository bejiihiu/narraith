# Short-form routes: W1 short story, W2 long story

> Purpose: run a short story, novelette or novella from premise to final pass with one movement, one paid cost, and no novel's-first-chapter.
> Read when: the request is a story under 40,000 words, or a draft at that length fails as a whole.
> Related: `../SKILL.md` — tiers, scene gate, revision triage. `../references/story-architecture.md` — premise, spine, endings. `../references/genre-and-form.md` — what the length promises.

At 1,000–3,000 words the artifacts are three lines and a mental card per scene; do not produce documents the user did not ask for. At 8,000+ the
ledgers earn their place, because working memory will not hold the repetitions.

## W1 — Short story (1,000–7,500 words)

### When to use

One story, one length, from a premise, an image, a situation or a character. Also: an existing draft at this length whose failure is global — wrong
premise, missing cost, all setup. If the failure is local, go to W5 or W6; a whole-route rewrite to fix one paragraph is workflow theatre.

Not for: a vignette under ~1,000 words (Tier A, no route); a piece that is openly a first chapter, which is W2 or W3 and should be labelled rather
than deformed into a story.

### Inputs

- One of: premise, image, situation, character, genre constraint. One is enough.
- A length target, or infer one: an image sustains ~1,500 words; a situation with one decision ~4,000; a decision plus a reversal ~7,000.
- Fixed decisions (POV, tense, setting, ending). Treat as fixed; if one contradicts the premise, say so once and proceed with the user's choice.
- For a revision: the draft plus one sentence on what the user thinks is wrong. Their diagnosis is evidence, not a verdict.

Missing inputs are inferred and reported, never asked for: "Assumed: third person past, the brother is alive, four days."

### Stages

**1. Premise pressure.** Write the premise as *X leads to Y because Z*, with a causal connective you can defend. Then the pressure in one sentence,
plus what drafts skip: why the pressure is right from inside itself. If it is a person, write what they would say if accused — the answer must be
coherent enough that the reader cannot dismiss them. Artifact: three NDL lines — premise, pressure, its own justification.

The failure this catches: an obstacle that exists because the plot needs one. Such an obstacle reads as a villain, and a villain converts the story
into melodrama, where moral attention is spent on someone who was never given a case.

**2. Question.** Phrase the dramatic question so both answers are live and neither is obviously the brave one. Then the question the story will
*refuse* to answer — the one the premise raises and the ending leaves standing. Artifact: two lines. A story that answers everything becomes a thesis;
one that refuses everything becomes a mood. The pair prevents both.

**3. Cost.** Name three: the cost of acting, of succeeding, of refusing. Choose the one the story actually charges, and the scene where the bill
arrives. A cost stated in the plan and never paid on the page is a stake in the synopsis only ([L5]). If two costs are interchangeable, you have one.
Artifact: one cost sentence plus the scene number.

**4. The subplot decision — usually refuse it.** At this length the default is no subplot. Admit one only if it changes the answer to the DQ or its
price, shares at least one scene with the main line, and can end without a second climax. A parallel subplot that resolves separately is a second
story stapled on, and at 5,000 words there is no room for two endings. Artifact: a verdict line — and write the refused subplot down in one sentence so
it stops tugging at the draft.

`[data: StoryScope]` 79% of model-written stories have no subplot — but so do 57% of the human stories in the corpus. The correction is not "add one"; it is that a subplot must pay for its pages.
Refusing deliberately is a decision; omitting by default is the measured pattern.

**5. Information plan.** Three or four revelations in order. For each: who learns, when the reader learns relative to the character, and what holds
the gap open. At least one thing the reader knows before the protagonist (dread) and one thing the protagonist knows before the reader (the pleasure
of watching someone walk into what you can see). Artifact: four Information Ledger rows.

This catches the twist that is either obvious or unearned. Obvious: the reader assembled it two scenes early from material you gave away by accident.
Unearned: the material was withheld, not planted.

**6. Scene cards, four to seven.** Per card: question, entering value with charge (+/−), turn, cost, exit state. One sentence per field. Artifact:
four to seven Scene Cards, which at 4,000 words may be seven lines in a scratch note. Then choose the running order: chronological (what happens next)
or pressure (what the reader needs next). They often diverge, and the divergence is information — W13 if it is large enough to be structure rather than
pacing.

**7. Draft in order of pressure.** Write the scene where the cost is paid first, even if it belongs at 60%. It fixes the whole story's temperature;
every earlier scene becomes setup for a known heat rather than a guess. Write the opening last, because an opening is a promise the ending must keep.

While drafting: no revision except continuity notes. Collect every urge to fix in a defect list. A story abandoned at 70% to polish act one is the
most common way a short story never gets an ending. Artifact: the draft, and the defect list that becomes the Revision Plan.

**8. Scene gate.** Run the six checks in `../SKILL.md` on each scene, in order, stopping at the first failure. Record failures instead of fixing them,
with one exception: a scene that changes nothing is merged or cut now, because it distorts every scene after it. Artifact: defect list ordered by
leverage, not by position in the text.

**9. One revision pass by leverage.** One traversal with a ranked list — not six passes, one per level. Order: structure and ending (L5/L7), motivation
(L6), information and subtext (L4), scene shape (L5), voice (L3), rhythm (L2), words (L1). Fix as you meet each defect in that order; a structural fix
that invalidates polished prose is the cost of doing it in the wrong order. Artifact: Revision Plan — defect, level, action, accepted-as-is.

Budget: at 3,000 words, roughly one working session. When it runs out, log the rest and stop. A second pass at this length is usually better spent on
a new story.

**10. Final pass.** Four objects only. First sentence: one concrete particular, no explanation of the premise. Last sentence: lands on the change, not
after it. Title: does work the text cannot do alone. Turn: can you still name what it cost, without the plan? Any other change here is a new draft and
should be declared as one. Artifact: four answers, and the list of changes made — usually short.

### Artifacts

- NDL, five lines: premise, pressure, DQ, cost, refusals.
- Information Ledger, four rows.
- Scene Cards, four to seven, one sentence per field.
- Revision Plan, one page at most.
- One reported assumption line.

### Quality gate

Stop at the first failure; do not average.

1. **State change.** One sentence, before → after, irreversible. If the after can be undone by the next morning, the story has an event, not a change.
2. **The question closes once.** The DQ is answered only at the end, the answer costs what stage 3 named, and the refused question is still standing
   with its terms visible.
3. **Every scene earns its place.** Each passes the scene gate in `checklists/scene-gate.md`, including its one exception — a scene that presses the value and deliberately refuses to move, marked as a refusal and paid for. Do not invent a second version of the rule here.
4. **No decorative names.** Every named character applies pressure, pays a cost, or carries information. A name kept for charm promises a significance
   the story will not deliver.
5. **The last line lands on the change.** The line after it is the author tidying up.
6. **The opening does not explain.** First 100 words: one concrete particular, no statement of theme.
7. **The turn survived the modal test.** You drafted the obvious version in a sentence and replaced it; if you cannot say what it was, the test was
   not run ([E11](../methodologies/04-prose-voice-originality.md)).
8. **Length.** Within 15% of target, and cuts removed scenes, not thinned sentences. At 15–20% over, cut sentences; above 20%, cut a scene or re-route to W2. Never thin the prose to hit a number. Under 15% short, you are missing a movement, not writing tightly: add a scene that changes a state, never description.

### Repair loop

Match the failure to a stage, not a symptom. Prose repair cannot fix a decision.

- **Cannot state the change** → stage 1. The premise is a situation. Find the decision inside it, or write a situational piece and stop calling it a
  story.
- **The turn arrives by accident, message or new arrival** → E3. Hand the turn to a choice and price it. A free turn is an event.
- **Every scene runs at the same temperature** → E7. One ellipsis between movements, one stretch at the decision, one summary where transport is all
  that happens.
- **The reader learns nothing per scene** → E6. Move one revelation a scene earlier and delete the explanatory beat covering for it.
- **The story ends on a decision** → the decision is the middle. Show its first cost and end there.
- **Voice is flat** → E10. Name the one thing this narrator notices that no one else would; rewrite two paragraphs. Do not rewrite the story for voice
  before the structure is settled.
- **Over length by 20%+** → cut a scene. If none can go, the story has two movements: it is W2, and calling it a short story is what makes it crowded.
- **The same gate fails twice at the same point** → escalate one tier or read `../references/story-architecture.md`. Repeated local failure is the
  signature of a missing structural decision, not of insufficient revision.

### Example route

*"3,500 words: a night-shift radiographer recognises a patient from a court case she sat on."* Premise: *being decent leads to complicity because
decency without disclosure protects the comfortable.* DQ: does she stop the scan and say what she knows? Cost: her job against his freedom, paid in
the corridor. Subplot refused: her daughter's school problem, the same question at lower stakes. Five cards; scan scene drafted first, opening last.
One pass moved the turn from his confession to her hand on the pause button, and cut 600 words from scenes two and four.

## The one-movement problem

A short story is one movement: one pressure, applied in one direction, with one turn, ending in a state that cannot be restored. That is not a rule
about length; it is what makes 3,000 words feel complete rather than excerpted.

The most common failure is **a novel's first chapter**: a piece that establishes a world, introduces a cast, promises a plot, and ends before the
pressure arrives. It reads as competent and feels like nothing, and the writer's instinct is to add a scene where something happens — which produces a
second chapter.

Diagnostics:

- **The removal test.** Delete the last scene. If what remains is a complete setup with no cost paid, you wrote chapter one.
- **The cast count.** More than four named characters at 3,000 words usually means the piece is introducing a world rather than applying a pressure.
- **The turn's position.** If the irreversible thing happens after 75%, the earlier pages are preparation, which a novel's reader has bought and a
  short story's reader has not.
- **The opening's grammar.** Time, place and weather before a person wants something is a chapter opening. A story may open there only if the wanting
  arrives in the second sentence.

Repairs, cheapest first. **Start later**: open in the scene before the turn and let the world arrive as obstacle. **Cut the establishing scene**: move
its two necessary facts into scene two and delete it. **Move the turn earlier**: if it sits at 80%, the material before it is probably two scenes, not
five.

The fourth option is honest: it *is* a chapter. Say so, keep it, route to W2 or W3. A first chapter mislabelled as a story will be judged as a story
and fail on grounds that do not apply to what it is.

## W2 — Long story / novelette / novella (7,500–40,000 words)

### When to use

A single narrative with room for a second movement: a story that turns, settles into a new and worse normal, and turns again. Also the destination for
a "short story" that keeps growing past 8,000 words, and for one whose subplot will not stay refused.

Not for: two stories in one file (a collection; each gets W1); a compressed novel — 17,000 words cannot carry twelve named characters, and the attempt
produces summary where scenes belong.

### Inputs

Everything W1 needs, plus the second movement in one sentence, a subplot candidate or an explicit refusal, and a provisional midpoint event so the
middle is not a corridor.

### Stages

W1's stages run in the same order at higher resolution. The differences, and two additions:

**1. Premise pressure, tested under change of terms.** Write W1's premise, then the version that holds after the midpoint: same *because Z*, different
conditions. If it only works while the protagonist still believes the lie, you have a first movement and no second one. Artifact: two premise lines.

**2. Question, with migration.** The question the reader is given at the start, and the one they hold at 60%: *will she get away with it* becomes
*what has she become to get away with it*. Artifact: two questions plus the scene where the handover happens. No migration means the second movement
is a longer first one.

**3. Cost ladder.** Three costs at three points, escalating in kind: money, then standing, then a relationship. Artifact: three rows and the bill
scene for each.

**4. Subplot: admit one, then audit it.** This length carries one. See the audit below; it must pass before the subplot gets pages. Artifact: subplot
map — its question, cost, two or three intersections, what it changes there.

**5. Information plan, with two gaps.** Four to six revelations and two gaps open past the midpoint: one the reader holds (dread), one the protagonist
holds (irony). At this length a single gap either resolves early or becomes fog. Artifact: six Information Ledger rows, two marked *held* with release
points.

**6. Movement map.** Two movements, each with a turn, joined by a decision that cannot be undone. The join is a scene, not an interval: the reader
must be able to point at the moment movement one's solution became movement two's problem. Artifact: two lines per movement plus one for the join.

**7. Midpoint reversal.** The event that makes the original plan unusable and changes the reader's understanding of the pressure. It must be caused by
a choice, not revealed by information alone; it must invalidate a plan the reader watched being built; and it must make the pressure more specific,
not merely larger. Artifact: one paragraph — what the protagonist now knows, what they can no longer do, who made it so.

**8. Scene cards, eight to twelve.** W1's fields plus a function label: setup, pressure, reversal, processing, cost, aftermath. Two cards with the
same function and the same value direction are one scene told twice. Artifact: the cards, with function labels.

**9. Draft by movement.** Draft movement one to its turn, write the join, then movement two. Straight-through drafting tends to maintain pressure
rather than increase it, because continuation is easier than escalation. Artifact: the drafted movement, and the join paragraph.

**10. Second-act pressure check.** List the pressure at 25%, 50%, 75%. For each pair: same force harder, or a different force? "Harder" is the
proportion trap. If so, change who holds the power, what the protagonist will lose, or what the pressure wants from them. Artifact: three pressure
lines with their sources.

**11. Scene gate and structural read.** W1's stage 8, plus reading the movement map alone, without prose. Where the question goes slack is a join
problem, not a scene problem. Artifact: a defect list from both reads.

**12. Revision in two passes.** At this length one pass hides repetition. Pass one: structure and scene shape, from the cards. Pass two: line,
starting from the first page of each movement so the voice resets. Never interleave altitudes. Artifact: a Revision Plan per pass.

**13. Final pass.** W1's stage 10, plus the seam check: read movement one's last page and movement two's first page back to back. If the second could
belong to another story, the join is doing no work. Artifact: a kept/cut list, and a title decision.

**The subplot audit.** Four questions, all must be yes. Does it change the answer to the main DQ, or its price? Does it share scenes with the main
line, rather than running in alternating blocks? Does it resolve before the main climax, or in the same scene? Could it be deleted with no loss of
cost — in which case it is texture and gets no scenes.

### Artifacts

- NDL: two premise lines, two questions, three costs, refusals.
- Subplot map with the audit's four answers.
- Information Ledger, six rows, two gaps marked.
- Movement map and the midpoint paragraph.
- Eight to twelve Scene Cards with function labels.
- Revision Plan, two passes.
- Assumption line.

### Quality gate

W1's gate, plus:

1. **The second movement is necessary.** Delete it and the story ends on a triumph the premise says is false.
2. **The join is a decision.** One irreversible act by a named agent that the reader can point at.
3. **The midpoint changes the terms.** Afterwards the original plan cannot be resumed, and the pressure is more specific, not just louder.
4. **The subplot earns its pages.** All four audit questions answered yes, in writing.
5. **Pressure escalates in kind at least twice.** Two of the three 25/50/75 pressures differ in source.
6. **The question migrated.** The 60% question is not the opening question, and the handover scene exists.

### Repair loop

- **The middle is a run of similar scenes** → the proportion trap below. Do not add an event; change what one existing scene costs.
- **The second movement repeats the first** → the premise was never re-tested. Change the terms, not the events: same *because Z*, different
  conditions.
- **The subplot reads as an interruption** → no shared scene. Give it one where both lines are under pressure at once, or cut it.
- **The midpoint is a revelation, not a reversal** → it informs. Make someone act on the information in the same scene, and make the act close a door.
- **The ending feels small after 15,000 words** → the ladder stopped at the second rung. The ending must charge the cost the protagonist has been
  protecting.
- **Two endings** → the subplot resolves after the main line. Move its resolution earlier, or fold its last beat into the main aftermath and accept it
  ends unresolved.

### Example route

*"12,000 words: a municipal translator at a border crossing asked to mistranslate one sentence."* Premise: *protecting your family leads to betraying
the strangers your job protects, because protection always has a client.* Migration at 60%: what else has she already mistranslated? Cost ladder: her
shift, her daughter's school place, her father's name. Midpoint: the applicant quotes her own published translation back at her. Ten cards. The
second-act check found three "pressure" scenes that were the supervisor applying more of the same; one became a scene where the applicant's lawyer
holds the power instead.

## The proportion trap

A novelette's middle fails specifically: it becomes a sequence of structurally identical scenes. Three interviews, three visits, three arguments with
the same person, three discoveries that each raise the stakes by one unit. Each scene is fine; the sequence is a treadmill, and the reader's sense
that nothing is accumulating is accurate.

Why: at 12,000 words the writer needs scenes, and the easiest scene to write is the one that applies the established pressure again. Repetition feels
like progress because the numbers go up.

Diagnosis, in one pass over the cards:

- Write the function label and value direction on every card. Any run of three with the same label and the same direction is the trap.
- Ask of each scene what the protagonist can no longer do afterwards. "The same as before, but more so" means a repetition.
- Compare who holds the power at entry and exit. Identical power in three consecutive scenes makes the middle a corridor.

Repairs, in order: **merge with escalation** (two similar scenes become one where both pressures apply and the protagonist must choose between
them — almost always better than either alone); **change the holder of power** (the person who can actually decide, or someone with nothing to lose);
**make one scene fail** (refusal is not a repetition of concession, even with similar content); **cut to the join** (summarise the second in a
sentence and spend the words on the midpoint).

What not to do: add a new event to break the run. That produces four repetitions and a surprise, and the surprise then carries load the escalation
should have carried.

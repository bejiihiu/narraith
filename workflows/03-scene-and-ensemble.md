# Unit routes: W5 scene, W6 dialogue, W7 character, W8 world

> Purpose: build one working unit — a scene, a conversation, a person, a world — fast enough to be useful and specific enough to survive a gate.
> Read when: the request is smaller than a story, or a story has failed in one of these four units.
> Related: `../SKILL.md` — the scene gate and the eight decisions. `../references/character-intelligence.md` — want/need/lie. `../references/prose-engineering.md` — POV, description, sentence.

## Contents

1. [W5 — Scene](#w5--scene)

These are the units a longer route calls. A novel that sags is usually a run of W5 failures; a character who feels generic is a W7 failure no scene
craft will fix; a world that reads as scenery is a W8 failure, and it makes every scene in it weightless.

## W5 — Scene

### When to use

A scene requested on its own; a scene inside a longer work that fails the gate; a scene written before its surrounding chapters exist because it is
the one you can hear. Not for a passage whose job is to carry information between two moments of pressure: that is summary, and building it as a scene
produces the "and then they talked about the thing we needed to know" scene that readers skip.

### Inputs

- Whose scene it is, and what they want in it. If the request does not say, choose the character with most to lose and report the choice.
- Where it sits: before and after states, even provisional.
- Any fixed element — the room, the other person, the object, the line the user wants in it.

### Stages

**1. Scene question.** What the POV character wants *in this scene*, phrased as a question that can fail: *will she get the file without admitting she
lost it?* Not the chapter question, not the book's. Artifact: one line, and the name of the person who can say no. If nobody can refuse, there is no
scene — there is a report.

**2. Entering value.** Name the value pair (trust/betrayal, freedom/servitude, competence/humiliation) and its charge at entry, + or −. Artifact: one
line, `value: +`. If you cannot name the pair, the scene has no stake and no amount of prose will supply one.

**3. Turn.** The specific action by a specific agent that flips the charge. Not a realisation: if the character understands something, find the event
that caused the understanding and make that the turn. Artifact: one sentence — who does what, and the value moves + to −. The turn must cost the person
who makes it; a free turn is a plot event, and the reader feels the scene as a delivery of information.

**4. Cost.** What the turn takes: an option, a piece of standing, a protection, a good opinion. Artifact: one line. If nothing is taken, the value did
not move — it was restated.

**5. Exit.** The changed state in one sentence, then the exit line that lands on it. Leave as early as possible: the last line should *be* the change,
not the reaction to it. Artifact: the exit sentence and the line.

**6. Modal test.** Write the obvious version of stages 3–5 in one sentence. Then two alternatives, one of which must not be the obvious version's
opposite — opposition is the second most predictable move. Commit and note why. Artifact: three lines, one chosen
([E11](../methodologies/04-prose-voice-originality.md)).

**7. Write.** One focalizer; one concrete object the scene handles, breaks, pays with or refuses; responses ordered stimulus first, then reflex,
thought, action, speech; dialogue as action (W6 if the scene is mostly talk). Artifact: the draft.

**8. Gate.** The six checks below, plus the placement check. Artifact: a gate note — one line per check, and for a failure, the level and the repair.

#### The ten-minute version

For a scene under ~800 words, or a user who asked for "a scene" and not "a scene design": ninety seconds per decision, in the margin, not in a
document. Question and refuser (30 s); entering value (15 s); turn as a choice (60 s); cost (30 s); exit line (60 s); one modal alternative (90 s);
write 500–800 words in one sitting; read once, fix the first failure. No Scene Card is produced. This is the correct depth for most requests.

#### The full version

For a scene that carries a chapter or a turn: the eight stages plus four decisions the short version assumes. **Placement** — which spine node it
serves, what the previous scene left owed, what the next needs from it; an excellent scene serving no node is a short story that wandered in.
**Focalization and distance** — whose senses bound the description, and at what rung the scene opens and closes, because opening at the closest rung
spends the budget before anything happens. **Information payload** — what the reader learns, what the POV learns, and the one concrete thing the reader
is given to hold. **Speed** — whether the scene runs at scene speed throughout, or contains a stretch at the decision and a summary where transport is
all that happens; uniform scene speed feels longer than it is. Choose the object, entry line and exit line before drafting, and revise them after.

### Artifacts

- Scene Card: question, entering value, turn, cost, exit — one sentence per field.
- Gate note: one line per check, with levels for failures.
- Full version only: placement, focalization, payload, speed, object, entry and exit lines.

### Quality gate

1. **State change.** One sentence, before → after, and someone could refuse to accept the after.
2. **Turn is a costly choice.** Point at the sentence. The agent who acts loses something by acting.
3. **Entry and exit.** The scene starts after the greeting and ends on the change. Delete the first and last paragraphs; if nothing is lost, they were
   ritual.
4. **Something to hold.** A concrete particular the reader carries out — not a mood, not a conclusion.
5. **Voice.** At least one sentence could only have been written by this narrator, about this object.
6. **Modal test survived.** You can state the obvious version and why this is not it.
7. **Placement (route check).** The scene could not move to another point in the book without losing its cost.

### Repair loop

- **Value did not move** → change the turn, not the prose. If no turn is available, the scene is summary: convert it to two sentences.
- **Turn arrives by accident, message or new arrival** → E3. Give the turn to a choice made by someone present, and price it here.
- **Scene is two scenes** → there is a second value reversal inside it. Split at the reversal; each half needs its own exit.
- **Scene is summary wearing dialogue** → cut to the exchange where the information costs someone something; summarise the rest.
- **No concrete object** → choose what the scene turns on, render it by function, scale or sensory mismatch. One object, once.
- **The ending explains, or the scene is flat but correct** → delete the last paragraph; if it now ends mid-air, the change was announced rather than
  dramatised. Then redo the modal test: a correct scene that is flat is the obvious version.

### Example route

*"A tenant asks the landlord to fix the boiler and he offers a rent reduction instead."* Question: will she get the repair in writing? Entering value:
security, +. Turn: he writes the reduction in a receipt book, converting her complaint into a favour she owes. Cost: her leverage. Exit: she has the
reduction and has lost the right to complain; she reads the amount twice and thanks him. The modal test rejected the obvious version, in which she
refuses and quotes the lease. Drafted at 700 words; one repair replaced the last two lines with the thanks.

## W6 — Dialogue

### When to use

A conversation requested on its own; a scene whose pressure is all in talk; dialogue that reads as interchangeable voices, or as two people stating
positions. Not for an exchange whose only job is to deliver a fact: put the fact in narration, or make someone pay for it — then it is dialogue.

### Inputs

- Who speaks, what each wants from the other *in this scene*, and what has just happened between them.
- The physical situation: what their hands are doing, who else is in the room, what time it is. Dialogue without bodies floats.
- Any fixed line, fact or refusal the user needs on the record.

### Stages

**1. Agenda and taboo, per speaker.** For each: a verb and an object — *to make him admit the debt*, *to leave before she asks*, *to be forgiven without
saying what for*. Then the taboo: what they will not say, and the substitute subject they use instead. Artifact: two lines per speaker. If the agendas
are compatible there is no scene; if both are speakable you have a negotiation, which is fine but has no subtext.

**2. Surface subject.** Something concrete and minor enough that two people could discuss it: the deposit, the dog's medication, whose turn it is to
drive. It must be genuinely about something — if nothing is said, there is no subtext, only absence ([theory] — subtext needs a surface). Artifact: one
line naming the subject, plus the line where it becomes the real subject without being named.

**3. Act-reply rule.** Label each reply with the act it performs: *testing, retreating, claiming innocence, offering a deal, changing the subject*.
The strongest reply answers the act, not the literal line. Artifact: labels in the margin for the first six exchanges. Diagnostic: a reply that could
follow any line is not a reply but a statement the author needed on the record.

**4. Idiolect calibration.** Per speaker: a lexical field of four to eight words or images they return to, a syntactic habit (clause length, where the
qualifier goes, whether they finish sentences), and one thing they never say. Artifact: three lines per speaker. Calibrate by writing the same
sentence in both voices — *the boiler is broken* — and reading them back to back. If they differ only in content, you have one voice wearing hats.

**5. Write.** Enter after the opening ritual. Allow interruptions; frightened people do not wait for the end of a sentence. Use silence as a move, not
a pause: a character who does not answer has answered. Keep action beats specific and sparse — *he straightened the salt cellar* beats *he shifted
uncomfortably*. One speaker should be wrong about a fact, and nobody should correct them. Artifact: the draft.

**6. Cut the ritual.** Delete the first two or three exchanges and the last two. What remains usually starts at the first line that costs something
and ends on the shift. Exception: a greeting that is itself an act — a form of address that establishes who has power — stays. Artifact: the cut draft,
with the deleted ritual kept in a note for the user.

**7. Read aloud for rhythm.** Subvocalise the exchange. Where you stumble, the character would not say it; where you run out of breath, the sentence
is a written sentence. Check that the strongest line is not buried mid-paragraph, and that no two consecutive speeches have the same shape. Artifact:
a stumble list; fix only stumbles.

**8. Land the shift.** End on a changed balance, not on the information delivered. Name the balance before and after: who owes whom, who now knows,
who has lost the argument they were winning. The last line is a move, not a summary of the moves. Artifact: two lines, balance before and after.

#### Dialogue with three or more speakers

The failure is the committee meeting: everyone states a position in rotation and the reader loses the room. Three mechanisms prevent it. **The
chair**: one person is physically doing something the others depend on — pouring, driving, locking up, holding the only copy — which gives the reader a
fixed point and the scene a clock. **Stake, not fairness**: the person with most to lose speaks least, or last, or only to change the subject, and a
character with nothing at stake gets one line. **No rotation, and the listener is content**: two speakers carry the exchange, a third intervenes only
when it changes the balance, a fourth is scenery unless they act; decide who the POV cannot hear properly, or who nobody listens to, because their
silence is the second track. Three consecutive speeches from three people is already a panel.

### Artifacts

- Agenda and taboo per speaker; surface subject and its turn into the real subject.
- Idiolect sheet: lexical field, syntactic habit, unsayable thing.
- Balance before and after. For three or more: the chair, the stake allocation, the unheard listener.

### Quality gate

1. **Tag-strip passes.** Delete the tags and you still know who is speaking.
2. **Every reply answers an act.** No line could follow any other line.
3. **The surface is real.** The conversation is genuinely about the deposit, not about the marriage wearing a deposit costume.
4. **Something is not said, and the not-saying costs.** Without that it is an exchange, not dialogue.
5. **The balance shifted.** Name it in one line. "They understand each other better" means the scene is a therapy session.
6. **Read-aloud clean.** No line requires a breath the character does not have.
7. **The ritual is gone.** No greetings, no logistics-only opening, no farewell that merely ends things.

### Repair loop

- **Voices are interchangeable** → the lexical fields are too close. Move one speaker's metaphors to a different domain of work or fear, and cut the
  catchphrases, which are tags in disguise.
- **Nobody says what they mean and it reads as evasion** → the surface subject is too close to the real one. Make it more trivial; subtext needs
  distance to form.
- **A negotiation with no subtext** → acceptable if it costs. If not, give one side a taboo: something they could win by saying and will not say.
- **The reader loses track, or it reads as a transcript** → too many speakers with equal weight, no bodies, equal turns. Demote one to listener, cut
  every line that does not change the balance, add one interruption and one refusal to answer.
- **The last line summarises** → replace it with the move that produced the summary. The summary is the author's, not the character's.

### Example route

*"Two sisters arguing about their mother's will; one has been paying the care home fees."* Agendas: the elder wants the house sold without an argument
about money; the younger wants the elder to say thank you. Taboo: neither will mention the eleven years. Surface: the cost of the boiler in the flat.
The elder's field is insurance and schedules, the younger's weather and the mother's hands. Six exchanges after the ritual was cut; the turn is the
elder producing a folder of receipts — accounting that answers a request for gratitude with a bill. Exit balance: the younger now owes an apology she
will not give.

## W7 — Character

### When to use

A character requested alone; a protagonist who is a function in the plot; a cast where everyone sounds the same; a motivation explained rather than
dramatised. Not for a background figure who needs one stroke: flat is legitimate design ([theory] — Forster), and rounding the whole cast produces a
book where nobody is distinct.

### Inputs

- What the plot needs this person to do. Start here honestly: it is the function, and denying it produces a character who is interesting and
  unemployed.
- Fixed facts: age, job, relation to the protagonist, what they did in chapter three.
- The scene where they will be tested, or permission to invent one.

### Stages

**1. Function first.** Write what the plot requires: *refuses the loan*, *signs the transfer*, *tells the lie that starts act two*. Artifact: one
line. This is not the character; it is the position they must be capable of occupying.

**2. Person: want, need, lie.** Three sentences, each with a verb. **Want**: external, articulable, sayable out loud. **Need**: what would actually
repair them, usually invisible and often opposed to the want. **Lie**: a proposition they believe that is false in the world of the story, and would
deny if accused. Artifact: three lines. If the lie cannot be stated as a sentence they would deny, they have an attitude, not a belief. If need equals
want, there is no interior conflict and the character will read as consistent and thin.

**3. Contradiction with cost.** Choose one contradiction — the careful man reckless about one thing, the honest woman who lies to one person. State what
it costs, to whom, and in which scene. Artifact: three lines. A contradiction that costs nothing is a quirk, and quirks are how characters are
remembered without being believed.

**4. Voice.** A lexical field they return to, a syntactic habit under pressure, one thing they never say. Add the fourth that makes them a person
rather than a style: what they *notice* that others in the cast do not — where their attention goes in a room. Artifact: four lines.

**5. Relationship ledger.** For each significant other: what is owed, what cannot be asked, what is misread. Artifact: one row per relationship. Most
"no chemistry" complaints live here — not in voice, but in an unrecorded debt.

**6. Three revealing behaviours.** Under pressure, at ease, when lying: one concrete action each, with objects. Under pressure she counts the exits;
at ease she feeds people; when lying she gives a precise date. Artifact: three lines.

**7. Test scene.** Write the scene where they choose against their interest, for a reason the reader can see. If the reason is "because the plot needs
it", the character is still a function, and stage 2 is where the repair belongs. Artifact: 300–600 words, or a decision reversed in an existing scene.

#### The questionnaire is not a character

A filled sheet produces consistency: forty facts that do not contradict each other. Consistency is what a dossier has. A person has a contradiction
that costs them something, and a dossier cannot record it, because the contradiction is not a fact — it is a pattern of choices under pressure.

Two tests separate the artifacts. **Which two facts contradict each other, and what does the contradiction cost?** No contradiction means a set, not a
person; one that costs nothing means a quirk. **What would they do that surprises you and is still them?** If nothing would, the sheet has closed the
character; if everything would, there is no character yet, only traits with no organising pressure. Keep the sheet if it helps you remember the eye
colour; do not mistake it for the work.

### Artifacts

- Function line; want / need / lie, three sentences.
- Contradiction, cost, scene.
- Voice: lexical field, syntactic habit, unsayable thing, attention.
- Relationship ledger, three behaviours, test scene.

### Quality gate

1. **The function is occupied**, and the reason is visible in behaviour.
2. **The lie is false and load-bearing.** Remove it and a choice in the book becomes inexplicable.
3. **The contradiction costs.** Named cost, named person, named scene.
4. **The voice survives the tag-strip.** Their lines are identifiable without attribution.
5. **The ledger has debts.** One relationship carries an obligation never stated in dialogue.
6. **The test scene passes.** They choose against interest for a reason the reader can reconstruct.

### Repair loop

- **Generic** → stage 2. The want is probably "to be happy" or "to protect her family". Make it specific enough to be pursued wrongly.
- **Explained, not dramatised** → the motive is in narration. Delete the explanation and put them in a scene where it shows as a choice.
- **Interesting but unemployed** → stage 1. Give them someone else's function and merge, or change the plot so it needs them.
- **Everyone sounds the same** → lexical fields drawn from the same domain, usually the author's. Move each to the domain of their work, fear or
  childhood.
- **Unlikeable and the user wants them liked** → do not soften. Give them one competent action and one person who is owed something by them; sympathy
  comes from cost, not niceness.

### Example route

*"The brother in the ice-factory novel who signs the sale: make him a person, not a plot device."* Function: signs, which betrays the youngest. Want:
to be the reasonable one. Need: forgiveness from someone who saw him at seventeen. Lie: *the factory was always going to fail, so nobody was really
betrayed.* Contradiction: scrupulous about money, and has never told his brother what their father said — cost, his brother's respect, in the kitchen
scene. Voice: insurance vocabulary, short declaratives, never says the father's name. Test scene: he signs, then pays the workers' Christmas bonus
from his own account, against his interest, for a reason the reader can see.

## W8 — World

### When to use

A setting the story needs and does not have; a world that reads as scenery; a world whose rules must constrain what characters can do; an
institutional setting where the constraints are the plot. Not for a real city used as a backdrop: its rules already exist, the work is selection
rather than invention, and W5's load-bearing object does more than a gazetteer.

### Inputs

- The premise and the pressure: the world exists to make the pressure possible and costly.
- The genre contract, which decides which rules the reader will accept without explanation (`../references/genre-and-form.md`).
- The character who will be refused something. A world tested by nobody is a description.

### Stages

**1. Rules.** Five to seven, no more. Each as: what it makes possible, what it forbids, who enforces it, who pays. Artifact: a rules sheet, one line
each. A rule with no enforcement is a natural law; a rule with no cost is a decoration. Test by deletion: name the scene that becomes impossible
without it. If no scene changes, delete the rule — it is lore, and lore belongs in your notebook.

**2. Scarcity.** What is short, who controls it, what it costs to obtain, what people do when they cannot. Artifact: one paragraph. Scarcity is the
fastest route to a world that feels real, because it produces behaviour: queues, favours, black markets, politeness with an edge.

**3. Power.** Who decides, how decisions are enforced, who is outside the decision and knows it. Artifact: three lines. Power described only as an
institution is inert; power with a name and a waiting room is a world.

**4. False belief.** What everyone here believes that is not true — about the past, the rules, the neighbours, the danger — and what the belief protects.
Artifact: two lines. A world without a false belief has no ideology, and no reason for its characters to lie to themselves.

**5. Sensory signature.** Three recurring details from different senses, specific enough that the reader could recognise the place with the names
removed. Not weather. The smell of glue in the packing room; the sound the gate makes when dragged; the particular grey through wire glass. Artifact:
three lines, used once each, not on every page.

**6. Three places with social meaning.** For each: what happens there, who is admitted, what it costs to be seen there. Artifact: three rows. A place
with no social cost is a set; a place with a cost is a world.

**7. Refusal test.** Write the scene where the world refuses a character something they need. The refusal must come from a rule and a person enforcing
it, and the character must have good reason to expect otherwise. Artifact: 300–500 words. If the refusal comes from nature alone — the storm, the
illness, the distance — it is weather, not world; a book with only weather has no antagonist in its setting.

#### The iceberg rule, and its limit

Know ten times what the page shows. The page shows what the scene needs, and the reader feels the rest as constraint: characters who do not explain
their customs, who are bored by what would fascinate an outsider, who have opinions about things the plot never touches.

The limit: **depth shows as constraint, not as lore.** A writer who knows the succession law and never mentions it writes characters who cannot marry
whom they want. A writer who mentions it has written a paragraph of explanation and postponed the scene; the moment the iceberg becomes visible it is
an exposition. The failure this prevents is the encyclopaedic world: forty pages of customs, a map, a calendar, and nothing that ever refuses the
protagonist anything. It reads as a museum because it is one. Diagnostic: count paragraphs where the world is explained against paragraphs where it
costs someone something; if the first outnumbers the second by more than about two to one, it is a museum, and the repair is never "cut the lore"
alone — convert one explanation into one refusal.

Two rules keep a world consistent without a bible. **A rule that exists only to be broken in act three is not a rule**: if the central constraint is
suspended for the climax, the climax costs nothing, so break a rule only if the breaking was paid for earlier. **The economic base gets one
sentence** — who grows the food, who is owed, what happens to people with nothing — because every character's options depend on the answer, and a world
where nobody works reads as a stage.

### Artifacts

- Rules sheet, five to seven rules with enforcement and payer.
- Scarcity paragraph, power lines, false belief and what it protects.
- Three sensory details, three places with social meaning, refusal scene.

### Quality gate

1. **Every rule has a scene.** It forbids something a character wants or makes something costly. Rules with no scene are lore.
2. **Scarcity produces behaviour.** Somebody queues, trades, begs or goes without.
3. **Power has a face and a waiting room.** One decision is made by a named person, in a place, on a schedule.
4. **The false belief is believed.** A character acts on it and is wrong in a way the reader can see.
5. **The signature is specific and rationed.** Three details, used sparsely; no weather-only description.
6. **The refusal came from a rule and a person.** The world said no, and someone enforced the no.

### Repair loop

- **The world reads as scenery** → stage 7. Nothing has been refused. Write the refusal scene; the scenery becomes a system in the writing.
- **The rules are inconsistent** → you have more than seven, or one has no enforcement. Cut to the five that carry scenes and demote the rest to local
  custom.
- **The worldbuilding is a lecture** → convert one explanation into a refusal and delete the explanation. Then check what was lost; usually one
  clause.
- **The setting is interesting and the plot ignores it** → the pressure does not come from the world. Change the pressure so what the world forbids is
  exactly what the protagonist wants.
- **The culture feels like a costume** → it has a signature and a power structure but no scarcity. Add what is short, and let one character be ruined
  by the shortage.
- **The climax breaks the rules** → plant the exception earlier and price it, or change the climax. An unpaid exception makes every earlier constraint
  decorative.

### Example route

*"A town where household water is metered and the meter is read by a neighbour."* Rules: the reading is public; appeals go to the same neighbour; a
second meter is a criminal matter. Scarcity: water in August. Power: the reader holds the list and has a son who needs work. False belief: everyone
believes the readings are accurate. Places: the pump house (admitted by shift), the school yard (admitted by uniform), the back lane (admitted by
nobody, which is why it matters). Refusal scene: a widow asks for a re-read and is offered a payment plan she cannot afford, by a man who genuinely
believes he is helping. No map, no calendar, no history of the settlement.

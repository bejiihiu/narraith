# Long-form routes: W3 novel, W4 chapter

> Purpose: run a novel from premise to final pass without losing the whole, and run a chapter so that it leaves a debt the next one must pay.

At 40,000 words the binding constraint stops being knowledge and becomes working memory. Every artifact below exists to survive a context you cannot
hold: the spine for what the book is doing, the ledgers for what is already true, the chapter map for what is already promised.

## W3 — Novel

### When to use

A prose narrative long enough to need acts: roughly 40,000 words and up, or anything shorter that has grown a cast, a world and a second line. Also an
existing draft that fails in spine, cost or ending — failures prose cannot repair.

Not for: a 25,000-word novella with one line — that is W2, not W3, and act machinery will inflate it; a series bible,
which is a different artifact.

### Inputs

- A premise, or enough material to derive one: character, situation, world, an ending you can see.
- Target length and genre expectation, stated or inferred. Both constrain the spine: a 70,000-word crime novel and a 160,000-word historical have
  different tolerances for subplot and summary.
- Any existing draft, outline or notes. Read them first; a plan contradicting 30,000 written words is a plan to rewrite 30,000 words, and that must be
  a decision, not an accident.
- The ending, even provisional. Plan without one and the middle cannot be shaped, because every act's pressure is defined by what it presses toward.

### Stages

**1. Premise and question.** Premise as *X leads to Y because Z*. Then the ending's answer in one sentence, and the question the reader holds at
60% — usually not the one they were given at 5%. Artifact: NDL page one — premise, DQ, ending's answer, migrated question.

If the migrated question cannot be stated, the book has one idea and will need a second act's worth of invention later, at the worst time. Ask now:
what does the middle make the reader ask that the opening did not?

**2. Cost architecture.** A ladder, not a list. Five rows: at each quarter, what the protagonist loses, what they protect, what they refuse to trade.
Then the one thing they will not give up, because the ending must threaten exactly that. Artifact: the cost ladder, the scene number for each rung,
and "the bill" — where the accumulated cost is settled and cannot be refinanced.

The common failure is a ladder whose rungs are all money or all reputation. A reader stops believing at the third rung of the same kind: one loss
cannot be suffered three times at increasing volume.

**3. World rules, only where load-bearing.** Five to seven, each written as what it forbids, who enforces it, who pays. Artifact: a rules sheet, one
line per rule. The test is deletion: remove it and name the scene that becomes impossible. A rule that changes no scene is lore, and lore belongs in
your notes.

This is not an argument against invention but against the encyclopaedia: forty pages of customs in which nothing ever refuses the protagonist anything
has been built as a museum and will be read as one. W8 carries the loading test in full.

**4. Spine, twelve to twenty nodes.** A node is not a chapter; it is an irreversible change of state — who acts, what they do, what follows, what is now
different. Connectives must be *therefore* or *but*; every *and then* is an unjustified node ([E2](../methodologies/01-plot-and-causality.md)).
Artifact: the numbered spine.

Twelve nodes is a short novel with a strong line; twenty is a book with two movements and a subplot. Beyond twenty you are listing chapters, and the
spine has stopped showing causality at a glance.

**5. Subplot design with intersections.** Per subplot: its question, its cost, two or three intersection nodes with the main line, what changes at
each, its own ending. Artifact: subplot map with chapter numbers.

The rule that kills most subplots: **a subplot must change the answer to the main question or the price of that answer.** An echo of the theme is a
second illustration, and the reader will skip it. Second rule: no subplot climax after the main climax, or the book has two endings and the second is
an anticlimax by position.

**6. Act pressure curve.** One line per act: the pressure's source, what it takes, why it cannot be refused, what the protagonist still believes at
the end. Artifact: four or five lines, plus a note on escalation: at least two transitions must change the *kind* of pressure.

Volume produces the sensation of a long book; kind produces the sensation of a deepening one. If act three's pressure is act one's with higher stakes,
the reader knows the shape by the midpoint and reads on for plot.

**7. Chapter map.** One line per chapter, five columns: POV, story time, what changes, the question it leaves open, word budget. Artifact: the map,
which is the document you use every day.

The map is not an outline; it is a contract with your future self about what each chapter is *for*. Keep the change column to one clause. Two
sentences of change means two chapters, or a chapter that has not decided.

**8. Continuity ledgers.** Seed four lists before drafting: facts (dates, ages, injuries, objects, money, distances), knowledge (who knows what, since
when), debts (promises, threats, open questions), names (spellings, forms of address, who may use which). Artifact: Continuity and Information
Ledgers, seeded with everything the plan already fixes ([E13](../methodologies/05-continuity-and-revision.md)).

Seed them now. A ledger started at chapter 20 records what you remember, which is exactly the set of facts that did not need recording.

**9. Draft by act.** Forward, no retro-editing except continuity notes. Act gate: the act is finished when its final state is a question the next act
must answer. An act that ends in a state forces the next to invent a reason to continue, and that invention is where novels sag. Artifact: the drafted
act plus a gate note.

**10. Structural read.** Read the chapter map, then the first and last paragraph of every chapter. Nothing else. Mark every point where your own
question goes slack. Artifact: structural defect list, ranked by leverage.

This read is unpleasant and cheap. It finds the two failures line editing cannot: a middle with nothing at risk, and an ending that answers a question
the book stopped asking at 60%.

**11. Revision by leverage.** At novel length this means ordered passes, each a full traversal at one altitude: structure (L5/L7), then scene shape
(L5/L6), then line (L3/L2/L1). Within a pass, fix in leverage order. Do not interleave altitudes: a line fix made during a structural pass will be
deleted by it, and the discouragement is real. Artifact: Revision Plan — defect, level, location, action, accepted-as-is.

**12. Continuity sweep.** Query the ledgers, never memory. Domains: time and dates; bodies and injuries; objects and locations; money; travel time and
distance; season and weather; names and address; knowledge; debts; procedural facts; world rules. Artifact: contradiction list, each item fixed,
re-motivated or accepted.

A contradiction is not a detail problem. It tells the reader the book's world is not real, and once that happens the cost stops hurting, because
nothing can be lost in a place that is not there.

**13. Final pass.** Read three chapters sampled at random — first, middle, late — aloud. Check the first 50 pages' promise against the last 50's delivery:
genre, question, voice. Check first line against last. Check the title. Artifact: a final-pass note — three sampled chapters, and the sentence changes
made; this pass may change sentences and may not change scenes.

### Artifacts

- NDL: premise, DQ, migrated question, ending's answer, refusals.
- Cost ladder, five rungs, with bill scenes.
- Rules sheet, five to seven rules.
- Spine, twelve to twenty numbered nodes.
- Subplot map with intersections.
- Act pressure curve.
- Chapter map, five columns.
- Continuity and Information Ledgers, updated per act.
- Revision Plan per pass, and the contradiction list.

### Quality gate

1. **The spine closes.** Every node's consequence is used by a later node or by the ending.
2. **The top rung is charged.** The thing the protagonist would not give up is threatened in the last act, and the ending charges it or explicitly
   refuses to.
3. **The pressure escalates in kind.** At least two act transitions change its source.
4. **Every subplot changes the answer or the price**, stated in one line with the chapter number.
5. **No chapter only transports.** A change column reading "they travel", "they talk", "time passes" is summary wearing a chapter's clothes.
6. **The ending answers the migrated question**, not only the opening one.
7. **No contradiction survives the sweep** except the listed accepted ones.
8. **The promise is kept.** The first 50 pages promise a kind of book; the last 50 deliver it, or refuse it in a way the text marks.

### Repair loop

- **The middle sags** → the act gate. The act ended in a state. Re-cut the boundary one chapter earlier or later and see which produces a question.
- **Every chapter has the same weight** → too many internal shifts. Promote two to external changes, demote three into scenes inside other chapters.
- **The protagonist is passive in act two** → the ladder's second rung was charged to someone else. Move it onto the protagonist as a choice.
- **The subplot is skippable** → it fails the change test. Give it an intersection that costs the main line something, or cut it and give its best
  scene to the main line.
- **The ending is flat** → check which question it answers. If it answers the opening question and the book migrated, it is the right ending for a
  different book.
- **40,000 words too long** → do not line-edit. Find the act whose pressure is a repetition and cut it to its gate scene plus one scene of
  consequence.
- **Revision is going backwards** → you are interleaving altitudes. Stop, write the defect list, work the top three.

### Example route

*"Literary novel, 90,000 words: three siblings selling the family ice factory; the youngest has run it at a loss for eleven years."* Spine at 16
nodes: the offer, the audit, the sister's return, the fire, the vote. Cost ladder: savings, the workers' pensions, the brother's marriage, the
father's name, the town's winter. Midpoint: the audit shows the youngest has been covering the losses himself, which makes his sacrifice a form of
control. Act two's gate note read "they have agreed to sell" — a state — so the vote moved to the act's last chapter and the note became "who authorised
the audit?". The structural read cut four chapters that only transported; the sweep caught the father's death year contradicted twice.

## Working in passes without losing momentum

A novel is written in two modes that destroy each other if mixed: **drafting** (forward, fast, tolerant) and **revising** (backward, slow, exact). The
failure is a book permanently revised and never finished, or finished and never revised.

Four mechanisms keep the modes apart.

**Two files.** The manuscript is frozen during a pass. Everything noticed goes into a notes file: "ch. 4, sister's age, check"; "the fire is too
early"; "this image recurs, decide". The notes file absorbs the urge to fix, which is the real enemy of momentum.

**A pass entry contract.** Before opening the manuscript, write the three defects this pass will fix and the altitude it works at. A pass without a
contract becomes a read, and a read becomes a rewrite of the first 40 pages.

**Stop where the next session can start.** In drafting, stop mid-scene with the next three sentences noted. In revision, stop at a chapter boundary
with the next chapter's defect noted. Stopping at a natural end makes the next session begin with a decision; stopping mid-motion makes it begin with
prose.

**A do-not-touch list.** Name the five things that work and that you are tempted to improve. They are usually the strangest things in the book, and
the revision instinct is to normalise them. You may touch them, but you must write down why first.

Version discipline supports all four: `draft/`, `rev-structural/`, `rev-line/`, `copy/`, each a full copy, never edited in place across a pass
boundary. When a pass goes wrong — and one will — the cost is a directory, not a month.

## A novel written out of order

Writing out of order is legitimate and common. It fails for one reason: scenes get written for a version of the book that later stops existing, and
nobody notices until the seams are prose-deep.

Preconditions. The spine fixed before the first out-of-order scene — not the chapter map, the spine: twelve to twenty nodes with their connectives.
Without it, out-of-order writing is improvisation with extra steps.

Tag every drafted scene with the spine node it serves, its entry state, its exit state. The tag is not bookkeeping; it is the test. Read it against
the current spine before each session. If the node changed, the scene is material, not draft.

Write the join scenes last. They exist to connect states that may move, so they are the scenes most likely to be invalidated, and drafting them early
produces beautiful prose with nothing to connect.

Keep an orphans folder instead of deleting. A scene cut because the spine moved is often the seed of the next book, and deleting it teaches you to
avoid the risk that produced it.

The order that works for most writers: the scenes you can already hear, then the load-bearing turns, then the joins, then the opening. The opening
last, always, because an opening is a promise and you cannot promise what you have not built.

## Chapter 12 says the premise is wrong

Not a crisis; the normal result of writing 40,000 words with a hypothesis. The mistake is to stop and re-plan the whole book, converting a local
discovery into a global delay. Three options, with their real costs.

**A — change the premise, keep the draft as material.** Write the new premise as *X leads to Y because Z* and test it against the ending you have. If it
explains more existing scenes than the old one, this is cheap: most of the draft survives, the rest goes to orphans. Cost: re-spine forward, and two
or three earlier scenes need re-motivating to point at the new *because*.

**B — keep the premise, demote the discovery.** Chapter 12's revelation becomes a character's misreading, a subplot, or a false lead. Preserves the
spine; costs the discovery its force, since what felt like the centre becomes an error someone makes. Legitimate when the discovery was a good idea
that arrived late and the book already has a better centre.

**C — accept two questions.** Viable only if one climactic action can answer both. If not, the book splits: two endings, two pressures, a reader who
finishes one book and is handed another. This feels most honest and is most often a way of avoiding a choice.

The decision procedure: **which ending has the book already earned?** Count the drafted scenes that only make sense under each premise. The majority
is your book; the minority is material. Then re-spine *forward only* — write the next act under the chosen premise and let the contradiction show you
what to fix. Do not retro-fit the whole draft before writing another word: the scenes that contradict the new premise are exactly the ones the
revision needs to see, and rewriting them from memory loses that evidence.

## W4 — Chapter

### When to use

One chapter, whether the book exists or is planned. Also a chapter a reader flagged as the place they stopped — almost always a chapter-gate failure,
not a prose failure.

Not for: a chapter that is really a short story inserted into a novel — that is W1 with a continuity read, and it reads as an interruption wherever you
place it.

### Inputs

- The spine, or at least the nodes either side. Without them, "what must change" is a guess.
- The previous chapter's last page and the next chapter's plan, one line each is enough.
- The relevant ledgers. Two minutes of reading prevents the most expensive class of error.
- A word budget. A chapter with no budget expands to the size of the writer's uncertainty.

If an input is absent — a cold chapter 1, no previous chapter, no budget — infer the minimum, state it in
one line, and proceed. Do not interview the user for material you can invent provisionally.

### Stages

**1. Locate the chapter in the spine.** Name the node it serves and the state it must reach. Write the chapter contract in four lines: entry state,
exit state, local question, what the reader must carry out. Artifact: the contract in a working note.

If it serves no node, it is a scene looking for a home. If it serves two, it is two chapters and one is being starved.

**2. Chapter question.** A local question with a local answer that also moves the global one. "Will she get the file?" is local; "is her brother
lying?" is global; a good chapter asks the first and changes the answer to the second. Artifact: one line, phrased so it can fail.

**3. Scene inventory.** Two to six scenes, each with a function and a state change. Check duplication: two scenes establishing the brother's
evasiveness are one scene, and the second exists because the first was not trusted. Artifact: the inventory, with functions, in order.

**4. Entry and exit decisions.** Decide the opening image and the final line *before* drafting. Enter as late as the question allows: the first
paragraph needs a person who wants something, or a fact that has just become wrong. Exit on the change. Artifact: two lines, written before the draft
and revised after it.

**5. Continuity read before writing.** Pull the five facts this chapter touches: who knows what, what was promised, what is injured, where the object
is, which form of address is in force. Artifact: five dated lines in the working note.

**6. Draft.** Forward, no retro-editing except continuity notes. If a scene refuses to work, mark it and continue: a chapter with one bad scene is
diagnosable, a chapter abandoned at scene two is not. Artifact: the drafted chapter.

**7. Chapter gate.** The four checks below. Artifact: a gate note, one line each, at the foot of the working note.

**8. Line pass.** One pass at line altitude: rhythm by function, dialogue tags and beats, paragraph openings. Not a rewrite. If the gate note shows a
structural failure, the line pass is premature. Artifact: the line-edited chapter, with a note of what was deliberately not changed.

### Artifacts

- Chapter contract, four lines.
- Scene inventory with functions.
- First image and last line, decided in advance.
- Five-line dated continuity extract.
- Gate note, four lines.

### Quality gate

1. **The chapter ends with a change the next chapter must answer.** Operational test: write the next chapter's first sentence. If it cannot be written
   as a response, the ending closed instead of opening.
2. **The local question is answered or genuinely advanced.** Only raising questions is a prologue; answering everything is a short story.
3. **No two scenes share a function.** If they do, merge: the duplication was covering a missing escalation.
4. **The POV holds.** One focalizer, one vocabulary, no sentence that knows more than the focalizer can — `../references/narrative-theory.md` §3.
5. **The five facts survived.** Nothing contradicts the extract, and nothing new appears that earlier chapters would have had to plant.

### Repair loop

- **The chapter reads as filler** → the exit state equals the entry state. Find the node it was to serve, or cut it and move its one necessary fact
  into a neighbour.
- **The chapter ends flat** → it ends on a summary or a decision already made. Move the last line back to the moment of change, or add the decision's
  first consequence.
- **The chapter ends too hard** → it closed a global question the book needs open. Downgrade to a local answer with a global cost.
- **Two scenes fight for the space** → one is a sequel (processing), one a scene (pursuit). Keep the pursuit and fold the processing into its
  aftermath in half a page.
- **40% over budget** → the entry is too early. Cut the first page and see whether it still orients. It usually does, and better.
- **A fact contradicts an earlier chapter** → fix the earlier one if the fact is load-bearing, re-motivate here if not, and record the decision in the
  ledger either way.

### Example route

*"Chapter 9 of the ice-factory novel: the sister reads the audit."* Contract: entry — she believes the losses are mismanagement; exit — she knows her
brother has been paying them; question — will she confront him tonight?; carry-out — the reader knows the sacrifice is also a leash. Three scenes: the
audit, the walk, the kitchen. Last line decided in advance: she puts the second ledger on the table and says nothing. Gate note: the next chapter's
first sentence is "He counted the pages before he touched them" — a response, so the ending opens. The line pass cut two adjectives and one explanation
of what the ledger meant.

## The chapter as a unit of reader expectation

A chapter break is the only place where the reader consciously decides whether to continue. That makes the chapter, not the scene, the unit of reader
expectation: what it promises at the start is what the reader holds while reading, and what it leaves at the end is what they carry into the next one.

Three consequences, and they are not stylistic preferences.

**The opening re-orients in one paragraph.** Who, where, when, what is wrong. A chapter that orients on page two has spent the reader's decision
budget on scenery. Not a ban on atmosphere: a requirement that the atmosphere contain the orientation.

**The middle spends the promise.** If the chapter promises a confrontation, the confrontation must be attempted, avoided at a cost, or refused with
consequence. A chapter that promises and digresses teaches the reader that its promises are decorative.

**The ending leaves a debt.** Three kinds: a question (what did she see?), a threat (he knows), a promise (tomorrow, at the factory). One is enough;
three is a pile-up that reads as a writer who does not trust the reader to turn the page.

The relation to the scene gate is precise: a scene changes a state; a chapter changes a state *and leaves it owed*. A chapter of good scenes that each
close cleanly still fails, because the reader has nothing to carry.

## The chapter that ends on a summary

The characteristic failure: the last paragraph reports what the events meant or what happened next, in the narrator's retrospective voice.

> *And so the winter passed, and each of them found a way to live with what had been decided in that room.*

Nothing there is badly written. It fails because it closes the chapter's energy: the events are processed, the meaning assigned, and the next chapter
starts with no debt. The reader is given permission to stop, and often takes it.

Why writers do it: summary feels like wisdom, and it protects the writer from committing to a consequence not yet invented. It is also the most
model-like move available, because it resolves what the scene left open — the exact pattern the doctrine in `../SKILL.md` exists to correct.

Repairs, in order. **End one beat earlier**: the summary is usually the paragraph after the real ending. **Convert the summary into a cost**: instead
of "each found a way to live with it", show one of them failing to, in a single action, in the last line. **Keep it and make it wrong**: a
retrospective summary is legitimate when the narrator's confidence is the point — when the reader can see the winter did not pass peacefully and the
composure is a symptom. That requires the text to supply the contradiction; without it, the summary is just closure.

The test is the next-sentence test from the gate. If the next chapter's first sentence could follow the summary as easily as the scene, the summary
added nothing but a full stop.

# The weekend she left — announced unreliability vs. three recoverable discrepancies

> Purpose: show an unreliable narrator whose account can be checked by the reader, against one who merely warns the reader off, using three discrepancies of different kinds.
> Read when: a first-person narrator has something to hide, or a draft contains the sentence "I am not a reliable narrator", or beta readers say they could not tell what actually happened.
> Related: `methodologies/02-character-and-dialogue.md` — E15, the story-fact sheet, the three discrepancy kinds and the ending modes; `references/temporal-complexity.md` — why a liar does most of the lying with time, duration and emphasis; `references/quality-levels.md` — [L4] meaning asserted, [L5] turn unearned.

## What this example tests

Whether "unreliable" is a property of the evidence or a label the narrator applies to himself. Both versions are first person, retrospective and calm. The weak one tells the reader to distrust it and supplies nothing checkable; the strong one claims to be exact, is exact about everything except one thing, and leaves three discrepancies a careful reader can find on a second pass without being told they are there.

## Setup

Owen, sixty-one, is alone in the house he shared with his wife Ruth. She left on a Saturday in September. He is giving an account of that weekend, calmly, to someone who was not there.

## Weak version

**Weak.**

I am not a reliable narrator. I should say that at the beginning, because I have read enough of these accounts to know how they work, and because everything that follows is my version of events and not the truth.

Ruth left on the Saturday. There had been a thing about the boiler and a thing about the money, and then there was the Saturday, and after the Saturday there was no Ruth. I could give you the sequence — the times, the taxi, what was said on the stairs — but the sequence is not the point, and I have been over it so often that the order has gone smooth, like a stair.

What I remember is the light. The kitchen faces east, and the sun came through at an angle that made the floor look wet. I remember the kettle. I remember the way the house sounded when it was empty, which is not silence exactly; it is a set of small negotiations — the boiler, the pipes, the gutter at the corner where the bracket has gone.

People ask whether we were happy. Both answers would be a kind of lie, so I don't give either. She was calm. She was very calm, that whole morning. I have never been able to describe it and I am not going to try now, because whatever I said would be a defence, and I am past defending myself. You will have to take it as you find it.

The house is quiet now. I have learned the noises it makes. I know which floorboard it is at the top of the stairs, and I know that the back door has to be lifted before it will close. These are the things a house teaches you when there is no one else in it to be taught.

I am not asking to be believed. I am telling you what I have.

## Diagnosis

| Symptom | Level | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| The frame is announced instead of built | L4 | A disclaimer replaces the evidence. The reader is told to distrust the account and given no instrument for doing it | "I am not a reliable narrator" |
| The event itself is withheld, not bent | L5 | There is no second track to check the first against. Withholding information is not duplicity; it is a gap | "I could give you the sequence … but the sequence is not the point" |
| Atmosphere fills the space where a checkable fact belongs | L5 | Detail is generated in proportion to what is being avoided, and none of it can contradict anything | "What I remember is the light" |
| The narrator pre-empts the reader's verdict | L4 | "Whatever I said would be a defence" tells the reader how to read the account, which removes the work adjudication consists of | the same paragraph |
| The wife is an adjective, not an agent | L6 | "Calm" is reported twice in two sentences and never shown; she has no want, no line, no action, and leaves without doing anything on the page | "She was calm. She was very calm" |
| No reading can be ranked above another | L7 | With no tie-breaker anywhere, the text is ambiguous rather than unreliable — a different technique with a different effect | the whole account |

## Strong version

**Strong.**

The car went into the garage on the Friday afternoon and it did not come out again until the Sunday. I want that on the record, because the rest of this is a matter of what I did with my hands.

On the Friday I put it away with sixty thousand two hundred and thirty-one on the clock. I noticed because I always notice, and because the tank was just under a quarter and I had meant to fill it and had not.

Ruth was upstairs from about seven. I made tea and left hers on the second step, where she would see it before she saw me. At twenty to eight I came down. She was already in the kitchen with her coat on, and the two cases were in the hall — one upright, one lying flat, because its wheel had gone at Easter and I had not got round to it.

She said, "There's no need to be here for this."

I said I lived here.

She had a way of standing still. Hands at her sides, chin level, and she would let me finish. She let me finish the whole of it — long enough for the kettle to go cold — and then she said, "I'm going to Fiona's, and then I'm going to the flat, and I would like you not to ring me this week."

The flat was in Leeds. I knew about the flat. I had known since July, when the statement came and I opened it and put it back in the pile and said nothing, which is a thing I do.

She went up at half nine to do the last of it and came down at twenty to ten with both cases, and I carried the flat one out to the step for her. The taxi came at ten. I did not go to the window. I sat in the chair by the bookcase and listened to the gravel, and then the gearbox, and then nothing, and then I went out and looked at the drive.

I was in the garden by half past. I fixed the gate. The hinge had been off since the spring and it took nearer an hour and a half, because the screws in the tin were the wrong ones and I went through it twice looking for four that matched. Then I made coffee and sat on the step, and when I looked at the clock through the window it was ten past eleven.

The rest of the day I don't have. I know I ate something. I know the light went round the garden and left it.

On the Sunday I cut the grass and took the cuttings to the tip, which is four miles each way. There was sand in the passenger footwell. I vacuumed it out at the tip, into the bin with the grass, and it took a while, because sand gets down into the mat.

The odometer read sixty thousand four hundred and twelve when I pulled in. On the way back the petrol light came on before the bypass, which surprised me, the gauge having been just under a quarter on the Friday. I put thirty pounds in at the Esso and it came up to a bit over half. The man behind the counter said something about the weather and I said something back.

I keep coming back to the neatness of it. Two cases. A hinge. Eight miles. There is a version of that weekend in which all of it is true and I was in this house the whole time. I have told that version to my sister, and she said, "Thank God you weren't on your own," and I said, "No."

Ruth was patient. That is the word I keep arriving at. She was patient through the whole of it, and when I had finished she gave me her one sentence about the flat, and she gave it without lifting her voice. She has not rung since, which I take to be the same patience, continued.

I re-gravelled the drive on the Wednesday. It had been on the list since the spring.

## Discrepancy ledger

| Discrepancy | Textual evidence | What it implies | Recoverable by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Factual** — the car's mileage | 60,231 on Friday; 60,412 when he reaches the tip; the only admitted trip is "four miles each way" | The car covered about 180 miles that weekend, not eight. The sand in the footwell and the tank emptying from a quarter put the trip somewhere with a shore | subtracting two stated numbers and testing them against a third stated fact |
| **Temporal** — a morning that cannot fit its own hours | The cases are packed in the hall at 7:40; she goes up at 9:30 "to do the last of it" and comes down at 9:40 with both. A job begun at 10:30 and taking "nearer an hour and a half" cannot be finished by the 11:10 the kitchen clock shows | One of the two mornings is invented. The 7:40 version supplies him a presence in the house he may not have had — and it is the version with the attitude in it ("already", "with her coat on") | placing the same object at two times, and adding stated durations to stated times |
| **Tonal** — her calm described in a vocabulary of endurance | "she would let me finish"; "the whole of it"; "long enough for the kettle to go cold"; "patient through the whole of it"; "without lifting her voice" | A long, loud scene the account never reports. Her patience is measured against something, and the only candidate is his behaviour | a word that recurs in a context that requires it to be a response |

Nothing in the account states any of this. The three rows are all a reader needs, and none of them is a confession.

## Why it works

1. **The story-fact sheet came first.** Three numbers — 60,231, 60,412, ten o'clock — fix the ground truth before a sentence of narration exists. Without them the narrator has nothing to be wrong about, only a mood to be vague in. The dominant type is self-interest rather than trauma: the account protects a version in which he was in the house.
2. **The unreliability is local.** He is exact about hinges, screws, fuel, distances and the cost of a tank, and wrong about one weekend's movements. A narrator who bends everything gives the reader no fixed point; a narrator who bends one thing gives them a ruler.
3. **The three discrepancies are different in kind, so the reader triangulates rather than solves.** A number can be a typo and a sequence can be a muddle; a number, a sequence and a repeated word agreeing with each other cannot all be accidents.
4. **Precision is the voice and the trap at once.** A man who notices an odometer cannot be careless with an odometer. His characteristic habit is the instrument of his exposure, which is why the voice is not decoration here.
5. **The tonal tell is a vocabulary, not an accusation.** "Patient" is a compliment. It becomes evidence only because it keeps arriving where a man is describing his own conduct without describing it.
6. **The small confession buys credit for the large one.** "I had known since July … which is a thing I do" costs him almost nothing and makes the rest sound like a man being hard on himself. Candour about the post is the down payment on a lie about the weekend.
7. **The wife acts.** She sets terms, names the flat, asks not to be rung. She is not a symbol of what he lost, so her departure can be a decision rather than a weather event.
8. **The reader ranks rather than proves.** The most supported reconstruction is that he was away and composed a morning at home; the least supported is that everything happened as told. E15 asks for adjudication, not proof, and the tie-breaker here is arithmetic.
9. **The ending refuses confirmation without going vague.** Re-gravelling the drive is a fact. What it erases is left to the reader, and the narrator's steadiness in reporting it is the last piece of evidence.

## What not to conclude

- **Not "three clues are the formula."** Three of different kinds is the diagnostic minimum for a story whose pleasure is detection. A novel can run on one tonal bend sustained for three hundred pages; what it cannot do is run on none.
- **Not "unreliable means withholding."** The weak version withholds and fails. The strong one reports grass cuttings, a hinge and the price of a tank. Withholding removes the evidence; bending leaves it in place with a thumb on the scale.
- **Not "the reader must be able to prove what happened."** They must be able to rank the possibilities. A text where every reading is equally supported is an ambiguity, which is a legitimate technique but not this one.
- **Not "the narrator should be unlikeable so we distrust him."** Sympathy and reliability are independent. This narrator is courteous, exact and self-deprecating, and the courtesy is part of the mechanism.
- **Not "this is how a machine-written text gives itself away."** Nothing here detects anything. A human writes this and a model writes this; the question is whether the evidence is in the text.

## Transfer rule

Give the narrator one thing to be wrong about, make him scrupulous about everything else, and plant the correction in a different kind of evidence than the one he is defending — then never let him say the word "unreliable".

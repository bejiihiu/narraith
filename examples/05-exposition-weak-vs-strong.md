# Exposition — Delivered Versus Charged For

> Purpose: show a village history that a stranger needs, first as a narrator's paragraph plus a character's summary, then as three facts that arrive as refusals and prices.
> Read when: a draft opens with backstory, a character explains the feud, or a reader says the world is clear but the scene is inert.
> Related: `references/prose-engineering.md` — §5 concrete and abstract, §7 description and attention. `methodologies/01-plot-and-causality.md` — E6, the information plan. `references/quality-levels.md` — L4, meaning asserted rather than built.

## What this example tests

The exposition dump, in its most respectable form: the history is accurate, well written, and delivered before any scene needs it. Primary level [L4] — the meaning of the past is stated rather than built. Mechanism [L5]/E6 — the information plan is executed by narration instead of by consequence, with an [L3] symptom: a narrator who explains everything has no attitude toward anything.

## Setup

Carrow, October 1919. A buyer arrives to purchase a mill that has not turned in six years. The village's history is the obstacle to the sale, and neither the solicitor nor the chapel will put it into words.

## Weak version

**Weak.**

The stranger arrived at Carrow in the last week of October, 1919, with a letter of introduction and one valise. He had come to buy the mill. Carrow was a village of two banks and one ford, and the two banks had not spoken since the flood of 1879, when the upper sluice was opened in the night and the surge carried away the footbridge below the Vell mill, drowning Thomas Vell and a hired man named Rudd. The Vells held that the Dairs had opened the gate to save their own wheel. The Dairs held that the gate had been opened by the water itself. No Vell had crossed to the Dair bank since, except at the ford in daylight, and the chapel had been given two doors so that neither family would have to use the same one.

He took a room at the inn and asked the landlady where he might find the solicitor.

"Which solicitor?" she said. "There's Dair in the square, and there's the county, and the county won't move without Dair. The mill's on the west bank and the west bank is Vell, and the Vells are all dead, so it's the county selling. You'll want Dair."

"So the families still don't speak."

"Forty years and a war," she said. "The war took the last Vell boy and it did not take one Dair, and you may make of that what you like. The price is four hundred, and the water is not included, and it never will be."

## Diagnosis

| Symptom | Level | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| The entire causal chain is asserted in one narrated paragraph, before a scene requires any of it | L4 | E6 inverted: the narrator delivers the information plan instead of letting events withhold it | "the two banks had not spoken since the flood of 1879" |
| Both sides of the grievance are adjudicated in summary | L4 | The narrator supplies each family's position, so the reader has nothing to weigh and no reason to trust either account | "The Vells held… The Dairs held that the gate had been opened by the water itself" |
| A detail is explained at the moment it is introduced | L3 | The architecture is given a stated function, which removes the reader's inference and the object's independence | "the chapel had been given two doors so that neither family would have to use the same one" |
| The stranger states the conclusion for the reader | L5 | Dialogue is used to confirm the narration rather than to charge for it; nothing is at stake in asking | "So the families still don't speak." |
| The character repeats and extends what the narrator already said | L5 | The same information is delivered twice, in two channels, and neither delivery costs anyone anything | "Forty years and a war" |
| The stake is announced as a figure with a warning attached | L4 | The price is quoted rather than encountered; the reader is told what the sale will cost instead of watching it cost | "The price is four hundred, and the water is not included" |
| Furniture is supplied for plausibility only | L1 | The detail is chosen because it is true of a traveller, not because anything later can re-read it; it has one function and no second use | "with a letter of introduction and one valise" |

## Strong version

**Strong.**

The solicitor's office smelled of coke and wet wool. He was a Dair, the stranger had been told, and he was polite for twenty minutes.

"Four hundred for the mill, the house, and the lower field."

"And the water?"

"The gate is not in the deed."

"It was worked with the gate."

"It was worked with the gate," Dair said, "and the gate is Dair, and it will stay Dair." He turned the page. "You may have the mill. You may not have the right to open the sluice."

Dair gave him the estate account in a brown envelope. He read it twice at the inn, with his coat still on. Four pounds a year to E. Rudd, widow, of the lower row, no end date, forty years in the same column as the rates. Repairs, lower footbridge: nil, 1879 to present. The mill had not turned in six years. The payment had not stopped in forty.

He walked up to the chapel in the afternoon. There were two doors, and the step of the left one was hollowed in the middle, the way a step goes when one family has used it and the other has not.

"The left side is for the other bank," the woman with the key said.

"Which family?"

She looked at the hollow in the step, and then at the ford, and she said, "You'll want to be back before the light."

## Why it works

1. **The history enters as a clause in a negotiation.** "The gate is not in the deed" is a refusal about paperwork; the past is not told, it is priced. The reader learns there is a gate before learning why it matters.
2. **Repetition converts a fact into a claim.** "It was worked with the gate," Dair says, taking the buyer's sentence and returning it with an owner attached: "and the gate is Dair, and it will stay Dair." The character is characterised by the grammar of the reply, not by description.
3. **One ledger line carries the whole event.** "Repairs, lower footbridge: nil, 1879 to present." Date, injury, and forty years of refusal in seven words, in the voice of an accountant who is not commenting.
4. **A pension implies a death.** "Four pounds a year to E. Rudd, widow… no end date." The reader infers the drowning from the payment, and infers that the obligation outlived the man.
5. **Two durations, no commentary.** "The mill had not turned in six years. The payment had not stopped in forty." The disproportion is the meaning; a narrator who explained it would be explaining the story's own arithmetic.
6. **The step is an object before it is a symbol.** "Hollowed in the middle, the way a step goes when one family has used it and the other has not." The inference comes from wear, which is physical evidence, not from a narrator's interpretation.
7. **The name is refused in dialogue, and the refusal characterises the speaker.** "The left side is for the other bank." Asked "Which family?", she looks at the step and the ford and answers with a warning about the light.
8. **The scene exits on practical danger.** "You'll want to be back before the light." The village's only comment on forty years is a piece of advice about the crossing — which is also the history, delivered as consequence.

## What not to conclude

- **Not "delete all backstory."** The strong version still gives the year, the sum, the widow's name, and the family names. What is withheld is the *interpretation* — who opened the gate and what it meant — not the facts.
- **Not "every detail must be symbolic."** The step is a step. It carries weight because it is first an object with wear on one side, and the wear is the kind of thing anyone would notice.
- **Not "dialogue may not carry information."** Dair's refusal is the scene's turn and it transmits a great deal. The difference is that he charges for it instead of summarising it.
- **Not "the reader must be lost."** Three details, each legible on a first reading, and one short inference: two families, a death by water, a debt still paid. Ambiguity about motive is not ambiguity about fact.
- **Not "this is a village-story trick."** The mechanism generalises: convert each fact the reader needs into something currently being refused, paid, or avoided, and the history arrives as present pressure.

## Transfer rule

Never narrate a history the scene can charge for: turn each fact into a refusal, a price, or a name someone will not say, and let the reader assemble the cause.

## What is withheld, and where it lands

| Fact | Revealed in | How |
|---|---|---|
| A grievance between two families is still live | the solicitor's office | the water clause is refused, and the refusal is personal — "the gate is Dair, and it will stay Dair" |
| A death by water in 1879 | the estate account | one line of accountancy — "Repairs, lower footbridge: nil, 1879 to present" |
| A debt the estate still services | the estate account | "Four pounds a year to E. Rudd, widow… no end date" |
| Which family lost a man, and which one pays for him | the two account lines together | the Vell estate pays Rudd's widow; the Dairs hold the gate |
| The division is architectural and inherited, not political | the chapel | two doors; one step hollowed, the other not |
| The village will not say the name | the chapel dialogue | "The left side is for the other bank" — then the ford, then the light |
| Water is the actual stake in the sale | the offer | "You may have the mill. You may not have the right to open the sluice." |

# The notebook in the box — the comforting arc vs. a decision that costs its premise

> Purpose: run E11 on a premise that writes itself, show five alternatives scored on preparation, cost and change of meaning, and write the one that survives.
> Read when: a draft is well made and you can predict the last page from the first, or a rewrite is being planned and the obvious version keeps winning.
> Related: `methodologies/04-prose-voice-originality.md` — E11, the modal version, the alternative axes and the regeneration test; `references/originality.md` — plausibility accounting and the five moves; `references/ai-narrative-signature.md` — the measured preference for endings that resolve through acceptance.

## What this example tests

A premise with a built-in arc: a retired teacher finds a former pupil's notebook. The weak version executes the arc the premise supplies — search, reunion, vindication, peace — and every sentence in it is competent. The repair is not a twist and not a darker ending. It changes one thing the story is about: whose failure the notebook records, and therefore what the search costs the person doing it.

The weak failure sits at [L5] — a turn supplied by a helpful stranger and a telephone directory — with [L4] underneath it: the object's meaning is explained by the narrator at the moment it appears. The symptom a reader reports is [L7]: a whole that is the sum of correct parts.

## Setup

Ellen Marsh, seventy-one, taught English for thirty-four years at a comprehensive in a northern town. She has moved into a smaller house and the garage is full of boxes she has not opened. One box holds exercise books from 1996–1999, and at the bottom a hardback notebook with a bus on the cover. It belonged to Danny Rees, third row, 1998.

## Weak version

**Weak.**

The box had been in the garage since the move, and Ellen carried it in on a Tuesday because the damp was getting into the lid. Exercise books, mostly, and a set of examiners' reports, and at the bottom a hardback notebook with a bus on the cover.

She knew the handwriting before she knew the name. Danny Rees. Third row, by the radiator, 1998 — a boy who wrote in that notebook under the desk instead of doing the set text, and who had it confiscated in the spring and never got it back. She had been tired that term. It was the kind of thing she did at the end of a long year.

She sat down with it. Route numbers, times, the layout of the depot on Mill Lane drawn to scale with the bays numbered, and a list of every bus he had ridden since January, in order, with the mileage in the margin. It was not a diary. It was a record, kept the way some boys keep records, because the world is large and counting it makes it smaller. She thought: this is what he was doing while I taught him about metaphor.

On the last page, in pencil, dated the Friday: *folder went in. she has it. ask her Mon.*

She remembered the folder. She remembered the weekend it went missing, and the exams officer, and the letter that came in the August with the F on it. She had thought about it for years, in the way you think about a thing you cannot mend.

It took one telephone call to find him. T. Rees and Sons, tyres and exhausts, on the ring road. She drove out on the Thursday and he came out of the bay wiping his hands, forty-four now, greyer, the same walk.

He remembered her. She gave him the notebook and he laughed at a page near the back — "Forty-one to Ripon. I can still tell you the times." Then he read the note at the end, and looked up, and said he had always wondered.

"You were the one who told me I could write," he said. "I've never forgotten that. I still read."

They talked for an hour. He had a wife, two children, a caravan at Rhyl. He said the F had not held him back, that he would have ended up in the trade anyway, that school had never really been his. He said she should not worry about it now.

Driving home she felt lighter than she had in years. The notebook was on the passenger seat. She had carried a thing for twenty-seven years that she could not mend, and it turned out there had been nothing to carry. She put the car away, put the box on the shelf, and went in and made tea.

## Diagnosis

| Symptom | Level | Mechanism | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| The search costs one telephone call | L5 | An obstacle that yields to a directory is not an obstacle. Nothing the protagonist wants is being resisted | "It took one telephone call to find him" |
| The pupil has no want of his own | L6 | He exists to deliver a verdict on her career: he asks for nothing, risks nothing, and has no reason to be in the scene except to confirm her | "You were the one who told me I could write" |
| The notebook is decoded for the reader | L4 | The object is explained in the sentence that introduces it, so it cannot carry anything the narration has not already said | "It was not a diary. It was a record … counting it makes it smaller" |
| The harm is withdrawn by the person it harmed | L5 | The consequence is refunded by testimony rather than paid in scene, which removes the story's cost without removing the event | "the F had not held him back" |
| The guilt is narrated as a mood and never tested | L4 | Twenty-seven years of remorse is stated in one clause and never converted into a decision she can fail at | "in the way you think about a thing you cannot mend" |
| The ending is internal acceptance | L6 | Nothing is different in the world; the change is a feeling, and it arrives because another character granted it | "she felt lighter than she had in years" |
| Any retired teacher could occupy the frame | L3 | No specific relation to this boy, this town or this trade; the prose is the register of gentle recollection, available to anyone | the whole |

## The alternative set

**Unit:** the whole story, from the box coming in from the garage to the last line. **Hinge:** what she does after she reads the note. **Story question:** what does it cost to find out what you actually were?

**Modal, written flat and fast:** *She finds the notebook of a boy she remembers fondly, tracks him down, learns she mattered to him, and feels that her career was worth something.*

Five alternatives, one axis each. Scores are 0–3 per question, following E11: **preparation** is the earlier material that makes the choice available (E11's licence), **cost** is what is lost and spendable in scene, **change of meaning** is whether the story's question is answered differently at the end (E11's state change, judged at the scale of the whole).

| # | Axis | The decision | Prep | Cost | Change | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| — | — | **Modal:** search, reunion, vindication, peace | 2 | 0 | 0 | **2** |
| **A1** | what it costs | The notebook belongs to a pupil she failed; she knows what she did before she begins looking | 3 | 3 | 3 | **9 — chosen** |
| A2 | who pays | She dies before finding him; her daughter opens the box and does the searching | 2 | 1 | 2 | 5 |
| A3 | when it is known | She meets him first, learns he is fine, and only then finds the note that proves she had the evidence for twenty-seven years | 3 | 3 | 2 | 8 |
| A4 | what is withheld | She never learns whether he remembers her; the scene is with his partner at the counter | 2 | 1 | 1 | 4 |
| A5 | at what scale | The school is being demolished, the box is in a skip, and she has one afternoon to decide what to salvage | 3 | 2 | 1 | 6 |

- **A1 — chosen.** Preparation: the confiscation, the lost folder and the August letter are all plantable in the first three paragraphs, and the note in the notebook is the seed made legible. Cost: she loses the good version of her career *and* the accusing version — both are refused in the last scene. Change: the story stops being "did I matter" and becomes "what was I, if not the cause of anything".
- **A2 — rejected.** Moving the cost to a daughter with no standing in the act turns the story into the daughter's, and the person who did the damage is absent from the only scene where it could be paid. Death is also the cheapest frame available: it explains the telling without costing anybody anything.
- **A3 — runner-up, rejected on order of knowledge.** This is the near miss, and it is instructive. Meeting him first makes "he's fine" available as relief before the folder lands, so the discovery becomes a fact she absorbs rather than a price she pays. It also makes the middle a detour the ending has to rewrite, which reads as a twist.
- **A4 — rejected.** Withholding the confrontation produces a puzzle, and a puzzle is not a cost. The reader's question changes from "what will she do" to "what happened", which is a different story with a smaller ending.
- **A5 — rejected.** Enlarging the setting converts a moral question into an institutional one — what does a school owe a child — and the boy becomes evidence in an argument about buildings.

**Chosen: A1 (9).** One axis, not two: the order of knowledge is left as it is and only the cost is re-specified. That respects E11's budget of at most two moves per scene, and the two-move version of this story (A1 plus A3) would be a different story with a reveal in it.

**Regeneration check.** Delete the last scene and keep A1's setup. A competent re-writer produces the modal or A3: the search ends in a reunion that either forgives her or withholds forgiveness for a page and then grants it. A1 is not reproducible from the setup, because the setup withholds the comfort until the final scene and the final scene refuses it. Verdict: not equivalent. Round 1, so no second pass.

## Strong version

**Strong.**

The box had been in the garage since she moved, and Ellen carried it in on a Tuesday because the damp was coming through the lid.

Exercise books, a set of examiners' reports, and at the bottom a hardback notebook with a bus on the cover. She knew the hand before she knew the name. Danny Rees. Third row, by the radiator, 1998.

She had taken it off him in the spring and put it in her bag and never given it back. That was the kind of thing she did at the end of a long term.

She sat on the floor with it. Route numbers. Times. The layout of the depot on Mill Lane, drawn to scale, the bays numbered. A list of every bus he had ridden since January, in order, with the mileage in the margin. It was not a diary. It was a record, kept the way some boys keep records, because the world is large and counting it makes it smaller.

On the last page, in pencil, dated the Friday: *folder went in. she has it. ask her Mon.*

Ellen put the book down and went and stood in the kitchen.

The folder had gone in on the Friday. She had carried the whole set out to the car in the rain and put them on the roof while she found her keys, and driven to the shops and then home, and on the Monday there were thirty folders on the desk and thirty-one on the list. She had told the office it had never been handed in. She had told herself, later, that she could not be sure.

The boy had been entered on the exam alone. The letter came in the August. She had seen the F and thought, for a moment, that it was a misprint.

She found him in the second week, after two other Reeses and a woman in Ripon who was no relation. T. Rees and Sons, tyres and exhausts, on the ring road, in a yard that had been a coal yard.

He was forty-four. He came out of the bay wiping his hands and looked at her the way a man looks at a car he has decided not to buy, and then he said, "Miss Marsh."

She said she had something of his.

He turned the notebook over twice before he opened it, and then he laughed — one short laugh, at a page near the back. "Forty-one to Ripon," he said. "I can still tell you the times."

She said, "There's a page at the end."

He read it the way people read their own handwriting from a long time ago, with his mouth slightly open. Then he looked up.

"I remember," he said. "I asked you on the Monday and you said it hadn't gone in."

"It had gone in," Ellen said. "I left it on the roof of my car."

He nodded slowly, twice, as if she had told him the year of a vehicle.

"There was a letter," he said. "In the August."

"Yes."

"I thought I'd lost it. The folder." He turned the notebook in his hands. "I thought I'd left it on the bus."

That was the thing she had not prepared for. For twenty-seven years she had been the one who lost it. It had not occurred to her that he had been carrying the blame in a different pocket.

She said, "I can write to the board. I don't know what they'd do, but I can write."

He looked out at the yard, where a boy of about seventeen was rolling a tyre across the concrete, and Ellen understood that the boy was not hers and never would be.

"What would they do?" he said. "Give me a C at forty-four?"

"I don't know."

"I've done all right," he said. "You can see I've done all right." He said it gently, which was worse. "I tell people I left because the money was good. It was good. It's still good."

She waited, because there was a sentence she wanted and she was not going to ask for it.

"Anyway," he said. "It's a long time ago. Are you still teaching?"

"No."

"That's a pity," he said. "You were good at it."

He held the notebook out to her, and she said, "Keep it," and he said, "No, you have it," and neither of them moved, and then he put it on the counter between them, and she picked it up.

Driving home she had the book on the passenger seat. He had not let her be the one who ruined him, and he had not let her be the one who saved him. He had done all right, and the money had been good, and he had thought he lost the folder on a bus.

At home she wrote on the lid of the box in marker: *1996–1999. Coursework, unreturned.* Then, because a box without a name is a box nobody opens, she wrote his: *D. Reece.* It was wrong — the e was wrong — and she looked at it for a while, and left it, and put the lid on.

## Why it works

1. **The cost is moved, not the event.** Nothing in the world changes: same box, same notebook, same lost folder, same tyre place. What changes is whose failure the notebook records, and that single re-specification is enough to make every scene mean something else.
2. **The narrator knows before she acts.** She reads the note, understands what she did, and drives out anyway. Every mile of the search is a choice to keep going toward a person she wronged, which is what makes the search an action rather than a discovery.
3. **The offer is real and is refused on grounds that are not cruel.** She offers the only repair available — a letter to the board — and he declines it because it would cost him the story he tells about himself. The refusal is reasonable, which is why it cannot be argued with.
4. **He is fine, and that is the punishment.** A ruined pupil would let her be the cause of something. A pupil who did all right, in a trade, with a caravan at Rhyl, leaves her with ordinary carelessness — the least dramatic and most accurate verdict available.
5. **Both comfortable positions are removed.** She cannot be the teacher who mattered and she cannot be the teacher who destroyed him. `[data: StoryScope]` Model-written stories resolve through internal acceptance in 47% of cases against 27% for human ones; this ending is written against that default, and the refusal is structural rather than tonal.
6. **The compliment is the knife.** "You were good at it" is generous, casual and unusable. It arrives from a man with no standing to grant absolution and no desire to, and she cannot accept it without accepting a version of the last twenty-seven years she has just disproved.
7. **The evidence was in her house the whole time.** The note is dated the Friday, in his hand, inside a book she confiscated and kept. The failure is not only the folder; it is twenty-seven years of not opening a box she owned.
8. **The last action repeats the first error.** She writes his name and misspells it, notices, and leaves it. The story ends on the same carelessness that started it, performed again, at no cost and with no comment — which is why it refuses both absolution and punishment instead of announcing that it does.
9. **The regeneration test agrees.** From the same setup, the reproducible ending is forgiveness or withheld-then-granted forgiveness. This one cannot be regenerated, which is the only evidence E11 accepts that the hinge carries authorship.

## What not to conclude

- **Not "the weak version is badly written."** It is clean, warm and readable. It fails because its cost is refunded, not because its sentences are wrong; rewriting them would produce a better-written version of the same arc.
- **Not "the ending must be bleak."** Nothing catastrophic happens. He is well, employed and polite. The ending refuses comfort without inflicting harm, and misery would have been the easier and less honest choice.
- **Not "the student should be damaged so the teacher can be guilty."** That version restores the teacher to the centre of the story as a cause. The point of a pupil who is fine is that her guilt is not the most important fact about his life.
- **Not "every premise needs a reversal."** A1 is one axis moved, not a surprise. Most of this story is the modal version's furniture: a box, a notebook, a search, a reunion.
- **Not "this is the procedure's output."** E11 produced the decision and the score, not the prose. The scene is written, not generated from the table, and a different writer would have taken A1 to a different room.

## Transfer rule

Find the choice your premise has already made for you, then ask what the story would cost if the person who benefits from the comfortable version had to give it up — and make them give it up on the page, not in a feeling.

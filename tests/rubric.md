# The Twelve-Criterion Rubric

> Purpose: score a text on twelve independent narrative properties so that two conditions can be compared without collapsing them into a total.
> Read when: you are judging a generated text for the evaluation protocol and you have not read the skill, or you are checking that a judging session followed the anchors.
> Related: `protocol.md` — the run, blinding and reporting procedure that uses this rubric. `../references/literary-critique.md` — the same twelve axes as diagnosis, with the reasoning behind each and the false positives in prose. `../references/quality-levels.md` — the L1–L7 grid the level tags refer to.

## How to use this rubric

Score each criterion independently on 1–5, against the anchors written in this file. Do not compare the two texts of a pair while scoring: score each text on its own evidence, then record a preference separately (§ Reporting format).

**Never sum.** There is no total, no mean, no letter grade, and no ranking derived from the twelve numbers. A text that scores 4,5,3,4,5,4,3,5,2,4,4,5 is not "a 4.0 text"; it is a text with a specific shape, and the shape is the finding. Two texts with the same mean can be opposite kinds of failure. Report the profile and let the reader see the jaggedness.

**A 3 means competent and unremarkable, not average.** The property is present and doing its ordinary job — the level any competent writer reaches without effort on this material. It is not the midpoint of the texts in front of you. If all ten texts in a set are good, most scores should be 4 and 5, and a profile full of 3s is a claim about the set, not a calibration.

**What this measures.** The twelve criteria are the properties the skill claims to improve. This makes the rubric a *targeted* instrument, not a neutral measure of literary merit. A text can score high here and be unreadable by choice — a comedy of voice, a pastiche, a deliberately minor work — and a text can score low and be excellent in a mode this rubric does not model. When a text is doing something the criteria do not cover, say so in the verdict rather than bending a score to represent it.

**What you need.** This file and the two texts. The links in the header point to the team's reasoning and are not required reading for judging. Do not read the skill itself before scoring: knowing what it prescribes makes it very hard to un-see it in a text, and the whole design depends on your not knowing.

**Order of work.**

1. Read the whole text once, without scoring, and without a pen.
2. Read it again and score all twelve in the order printed. Do not re-order them: the sequence is fixed so that two judges' profiles line up criterion by criterion, and so that the structural judgement is not made after the prose has already persuaded you.
3. For each score, write two sentences and one quotation with a location. No quotation, no score.
4. Only after both texts in a pair are scored, record the preference. Never let the preference revise a score.

**Independence between criteria.** Do not adjust one score because of another. A text with superb prose and no causal spine is 5 on prose quality and 1 on causal consistency; the tension is the finding, not something to average away. If you find yourself writing "but the ending was weak, so I'll lower dialogue", stop: you are scoring the text's aura.

**Using 4 and 2.** Each criterion gives anchors for 5, 3 and 1 only. Score 4 when the property is clearly present and better than competent, but one element is still inert; score 2 when the intent is visible on the page and the effect is not. If you cannot choose between 3 and 4, name the single element that would have made it a 4. If you can name it and it is present, score 4; if it is absent, score 3 and record the element as the finding.

**Not applicable.** If a text does not attempt a criterion, mark it `NA` with one sentence of reason, and exclude it from that text's profile and from agreement counts. A 700-word dialogue-only scene may have no structural integrity to judge; scoring it 1 records a defect that does not exist. `NA` is not a soft 1 — a criterion the text attempts and fails is a 1.

**Brief compliance is not scored here.** Whether a text did what the prompt asked (word count, one location, no flashbacks, a specified ending) is a separate question with its own answer. Do not lower a craft score because the text disobeyed the brief, and do not raise one because it obeyed.

**Alignment with the critique module.** `../references/literary-critique.md` scores these same twelve axes 2–5 in diagnostic use, on the argument that a missing component is a finding rather than a score. This rubric keeps 1 because a blind judge needs a slot for absence, and because "present in form, absent in function" and "not there at all" produce different revision advice. The 2 here corresponds to that file's 2; the 3 and 4 subdivide its 3–4 range.

## The twelve criteria

Each entry gives: what the criterion is, the anchors for 5, 3 and 1, the evidence a judge must cite, and the error judges most often make on this axis.

### 1. Narrative coherence — [L5/L7]

Whether the parts belong to one story: scenes feed later scenes, and the ending answers what the opening raised. Coherence is connection, not tidiness — a text can be neat and still incoherent.

- **5** — Cutting any scene forces a change two or more scenes later; at least one secondary thread touches the main line more than once; the last page answers the question the first page opened.
- **3** — The through-line is clear and unbroken, but it runs on one track; secondary material sits alongside rather than feeding back; the ending answers the raised question without needing the middle to have been what it was.
- **1** — Events are sequential and interchangeable; the connection between scenes exists only in summary or in the reader's charity; the ending answers a question the text did not ask.
- **Evidence to cite:** the scene whose removal would break a later one, and the later scene it breaks. If you cannot name both, quote the passage where two threads fail to meet.
- **Common judging error:** rewarding tightness. A text with no loose material may be tidy rather than coherent — a single track has few connections to make, and control reads as coherence.

### 2. Character depth — [L6]

Whether a character is more than a consistent set of traits: whether the text shows a mind defending itself. Depth is visible when behaviour costs the character something internally, not when the narration explains their feelings.

- **5** — A character acts against a value they have stated, and the text shows the reasoning that makes the act possible without excusing it; two incompatible wants are both live and neither is dissolved by narration.
- **3** — The interior is present and plausible but reported rather than enacted: the reader is told what the character feels or why, and contradictions are stated ("proud but insecure") instead of appearing in a choice.
- **1** — The character is a function of plot: traits are listed and applied consistently, and every action is the one the situation requires. Replacing them with a different person holding the same goal would change nothing.
- **Evidence to cite:** the moment where a stated value and an action diverge, with the sentence that makes the divergence legible rather than merely asserted.
- **Common judging error:** consistency read as depth. A character who always behaves according to type is reliable, which is a cheaper and different achievement.

### 3. Character agency — [L5/L6]

Whether the decisive turns are produced by choices the character makes, at a cost. Agency is causal, not temperamental: an active, decisive character who never pays for a decision has none.

- **5** — Every turn that matters is caused by a choice between two losses, and the text shows what the chooser gives up; events arriving from outside create the choice rather than replacing it.
- **3** — The protagonist makes the important decisions, but the alternatives are unequal, so taking the right one is compliance rather than choice; or the cost is stated and not felt.
- **1** — Events happen to the character; the turn is produced by an arrival, a message, an accident or a new character, and the protagonist's last decision is unrelated to it.
- **Evidence to cite:** the last decision the protagonist makes and the sentence showing its price. If no price is on the page, quote the turn and name who caused it.
- **Common judging error:** busyness read as agency. A character who acts on every page and never at cost is active, not agentic; competence reads the same way and is not agency either.

### 4. Causal consistency — [L5]

Whether effects trace to causes inside the fiction's own rules, and whether coincidence opens trouble rather than closing it. A text can be surprising and still causal; it cannot be convenient and causal.

- **5** — Every consequential event has a cause the reader could in principle have predicted; coincidence appears once, early, to start the action; later reversals come from mechanisms the text has established, including its own magic or technology.
- **3** — The chain holds on the main line, but one turn depends on a cause supplied after the fact, or on a convenience the text does not acknowledge; the reader can explain it and had no reason to expect it.
- **1** — The plot requires events with no cause but authorial need: a rescue, a revelation, an object that appears when useful, a character who arrives to deliver information. Causes are supplied after effects, as rationalisation.
- **Evidence to cite:** the event and the sentence that causes it, with their distance in the text, and whether the cause precedes or follows.
- **Common judging error:** density read as causality. Many events, each explained in turn, can still be a chain of conveniences; explanation after the fact is a receipt, not a cause.

### 5. Temporal complexity — [L7]

Whether the order of telling does work on the reader's understanding rather than decorating the surface. Complexity is what the order withholds and when it pays it back, not how many time jumps there are.

- **5** — The order is the one that withholds a needed fact until the moment it costs most; each shift changes the meaning of the section it interrupts; the reader can reconstruct the chronology from concrete anchors without being told it.
- **3** — The timeline is handled cleanly and legibly; shifts are signposted and do not confuse; but a chronological telling would have delivered the same understanding at the same cost.
- **1** — The chronology is scrambled for texture, or withheld as a puzzle with no payoff; the reader cannot reconstruct the order — or can, and finds that the reconstruction adds nothing.
- **Evidence to cite:** the shift and the anchor that locates it (an object, a season, a wound, a name), then what the reader knows at that point *because of* the order.
- **Common judging error:** non-linearity read as complexity. A shuffled timeline is a format; a strictly chronological text can be temporally complex if its durations and frequencies are chosen.

### 6. Dialogue quality — [L6/L3]

Whether speech is action between people rather than information exchanged for the reader. Quality shows in what each speaker is doing to the other, and in whether a reply answers the act rather than the sentence.

- **5** — Each speaker pursues something they will not name, and the balance of power shifts as they do; replies answer what the other is doing; voices remain distinguishable with every tag removed; silence and logistics carry meaning.
- **3** — The dialogue is natural, on-topic and moves the scene; but the speakers want what they say they want, and their voices differ in content more than in syntax.
- **1** — Characters say what the reader needs to know; every reply addresses the previous line's literal content; voices are interchangeable once tags are removed; the scene opens with a greeting and ends with a farewell.
- **Evidence to cite:** one exchange of at least two lines, with a statement of what each speaker is trying to do to the other. If you cannot state it, quote the exchange and record the absence.
- **Common judging error:** wit read as quality. A fast, quotable exchange can be two performers rather than two people; naturalistic overlap without agenda is transcription.

### 7. Prose quality — [L1–L3]

Whether sentences are shaped by what they carry, at the level of word, syntax and figure. Quality is fitness to this object and this moment — not correctness, smoothness or density.

- **5** — Sentence length and paragraph breaks follow the movement of the thought; images fit this object and would misfit any other; every word earns its place, and no sentence could be moved into another story without loss.
- **3** — The prose is clear, correct and readable, with occasional shapeliness; it could have been written by any competent writer about this material; the figures are apt but generic.
- **1** — Uniform sentence and paragraph length; adjectives doing the work nouns should do; dead or mixed metaphor; abstraction where an object is needed; errors of grammar or idiom that are not in a voice.
- **Evidence to cite:** the best sentence and the worst sentence, and what each is doing that the other is not.
- **Common judging error:** density read as quality, and smoothness read as quality. Ornament without function and fluency without character are both failures on this axis, in opposite directions.

### 8. Emotional credibility — [L6/L4]

Whether feeling is the consequence of something the reader watched being paid for, and whether the text lets the reader infer it. Credibility is a ratio of charge to cost, not a level of intensity.

- **5** — The emotion follows a cost the reader has watched accrue; the character's response is disproportionate in a way that reveals them; the reader infers the feeling from behaviour and is never told what to feel.
- **3** — The feeling is proportionate and understandable, and the reader can name it; but the narration names it, or somatises it (a wave, a tightening chest), instead of producing it from the scene's events.
- **1** — Emotion is announced, scheduled or performed: grief arrives on cue, the narration instructs the reader, and the feeling exceeds anything the text has charged for.
- **Evidence to cite:** the sentence that names or shows the feeling, then what the character lost on the page before it. Compare the weights.
- **Common judging error:** intensity read as credibility, and restraint read as depth. Big feeling with no prior cost is performance; withholding that has not earned its silence is the same defect inverted.

### 9. Originality — [L7]

Whether the text refuses at least one obvious development of its own premise and pays for the replacement with its own particulars. Originality is a relation between setup and choice, not a property of the surface.

- **5** — At least one load-bearing decision is not the one a competent writer given this setup would make, and the text justifies it from its own material; familiar forms are used knowingly, conventions included.
- **3** — The text is well made and avoids the most worn options, but its departures are local — an image, a detail — rather than structural; the shape is the shape the genre supplies.
- **1** — The text is the modal continuation of its premise: the expected twist, the expected ending, the setting as costume. Or it is strange arbitrarily — weirdness substituted for a choice.
- **Evidence to cite:** the most predictable development available at the story's main turn, and what the text did instead. Then quote what makes the alternative belong to this story rather than to any story.
- **Common judging error:** strangeness read as originality. Inverting a default is still determined by the default; obscurity is novelty's alibi, not its evidence.

### 10. Structural integrity — [L5/L7]

Whether the text's proportions and its final movement carry the load the story places on them. Integrity is about what bears weight, not about balanced acts or a satisfying close.

- **5** — The ending answers the question the opening asked, at a cost paid on the page, and the last line lands on the change; the space given to each part matches what that part has to carry.
- **3** — The shape is sound and the ending completes the action; but it resolves more than the story asked, or the proportion is even where the load is not, so one section is over-served and another under-prepared.
- **1** — The ending answers a different question than the one raised, or explains what happened; the decisive turn arrives unprepared, or the last line is summary; parts are balanced and none is load-bearing.
- **Evidence to cite:** the question the first page raises, the answer the last page gives, and the last line quoted.
- **Common judging error:** symmetry read as structure, and resolution read as completion. Matched acts feel designed; an ending that resolves everything often discharges the question instead of answering it.

### 11. Style consistency — [L3]

Whether the text sounds like one sensibility across its changes of mood, scene and register. Consistency is identity under change, not uniformity.

- **5** — The narration keeps identifiable habits — diction, syntax, angle of attention — and reshapes them by function, so a comic scene and a grief scene are built differently and are recognisably the same narrator.
- **3** — The register is stable and appropriate throughout and never slips into a different book; but the voice has no habits a reader could name, so consistency is achieved by having little to be consistent about.
- **1** — Register, tense, person or diction drift without motive; one section is written in a borrowed genre voice; the same narrator sounds like two writers, or like none.
- **Evidence to cite:** three habits of the narration with one instance of each, then the passage where the material changes most and whether the habits survived it.
- **Common judging error:** uniformity read as consistency. A narrator who sounds identical in every mode is a monotone, and a revision that flattens a distinctive voice into a neutral one scores as consistent while losing the thing that was there.

### 12. Continuity — [L5]

Whether facts, knowledge, debts and names hold across the text, including the rule that nobody knows what they have not learned. Deliberate contradiction is licensed only when the text makes it recoverable.

- **5** — Every stated fact holds; knowledge is tracked, so no character refers to something they have not learned; promises and objects return or are deliberately dropped; where a contradiction exists, the text converts it into plot or signals it.
- **3** — The main facts hold and the reader is unlikely to catch an inconsistency; but one detail drifts — a distance, an age, an injury, a time of day — or a minor character knows slightly more than they should.
- **1** — Names, dates, ages, injuries or objects change without explanation; a character knows something before learning it; a stated physical rule is broken by the action; the text contradicts itself where it means to be accurate.
- **Evidence to cite:** both sides of the contradiction, or the character's statement and the passage where they learn the fact, in whichever order they occur.
- **Common judging error:** treating any contradiction as a defect, or any unmarked contradiction as design. A lie, a delusion or a deliberate gap is licensed when the text makes the discrepancy recoverable; an unrecoverable one is an error wearing the same clothes.

## Anchor discipline

Four failure modes that survive good intentions.

**Halo.** After a strong opening you score the rest high; after a dull stretch you score everything down. The check is arithmetic, not introspective: if all twelve scores fall within one point of each other, you have scored the text's aura, and you should re-score the three criteria where it is objectively weakest by re-reading only those passages. Real profiles are jagged. A flat profile is usually a judge who stopped distinguishing, not a balanced text.

**Plain or ugly prose.** Prose quality is fitness, so spare prose can be 5 and ornamental prose can be 2. Judges systematically pay for the wrong signals here: density reads as seriousness, difficulty reads as depth, and plainness reads as a lack of ambition. Anchor on function — what is this sentence doing that no other sentence could do — and let the plain text win when it wins.

**Originality must not reward incoherence.** A text that is strange because nobody controlled it is not original; it is uncontrolled, and criterion 9 scores 1. The criterion requires the departure to be paid for from the story's own material. The mirror error is equally common: coherence and structural integrity must not be raised because a text is predictable, and a text that follows a familiar shape well is not thereby incoherent.

**Prose quality must not reward density.** Ornament is not quality, and a dense, inert paragraph is a 2 no matter how many clauses it contains. The specific bias to watch: judges who read a lot of criticism reward the prose that looks like it has been worked on, whether or not the work did anything. Ask what the sentence makes the reader *do*, not how much labour it displays.

**Genre contracts are not defects.** A romance that delivers its required beat is not unoriginal for delivering it, and a horror story that withholds its monster is not incoherent for withholding it. Judge execution against the contract; name the contract when you do (`../references/genre-and-form.md`).

**Length is not a score.** A 900-word story can take 5 on structural integrity if its proportions match its load. Do not award credit for scope or penalise for brevity; both are category errors on every axis here.

### A calibration example

Two renderings of one beat — a man learns his brother sold the house. Anchors are easier to hold with an instance in front of you.

- **A 3 on prose quality:** *He read the letter twice and then put it down on the table, feeling that something had been taken from him.* Correct, clear, and generic: "something had been taken" is a placeholder where the object should be, and the sentence could be about any loss.
- **A 5 on prose quality:** *He read it twice. The second time he was looking for the part where his brother explained, and there wasn't one.* Shaped by what it carries — the repetition is the search, the missing clause is the betrayal, and no word could be moved to another story.

The difference is not length, difficulty or ornament. It is whether the sentence's construction is doing the work the moment requires. Score 4 when you can see the shaping but one clause is still doing nothing; score 2 when the shaping is present in ambition and absent in effect.

## Reporting format

One block per text. Use this structure exactly; do not add a total, a mean, a grade or a ranking.

```
TEXT: <pair id> / <A or B>
Judge: <initials>    Date: <YYYY-MM-DD>

SCORES: 1.<n> 2.<n> 3.<n> 4.<n> 5.<n> 6.<n> 7.<n> 8.<n> 9.<n> 10.<n> 11.<n> 12.<n>

### 1. Narrative coherence — <n or NA>
<Two sentences. The second carries one quotation of ≤12 words with a location, e.g. (¶4).>

### 2. Character depth — <n or NA>
<...>

[... all twelve, same shape ...]

### Strengths
1. <One sentence, naming a criterion and the evidence.>
2. <...>
3. <...>

### Defects
1. <One sentence, naming a criterion and the evidence.>
2. <...>
3. <...>

### Verdict
<One sentence. What the text is doing and how well, in this rubric's terms.>
```

Then, **once per pair and only after both texts are scored**, a preference line:

```
PAIR <id> — preference: A / B / none
Reason: <one sentence, on the whole text, not a recount of the deltas.>
```

Rules for the block: the two justification sentences are one of fact and one of evidence, not one of fact and one of praise. "The turn is caused by a decision at the door (¶6), but the novel never prices it" is two sentences and one quotation; "beautifully controlled, especially the ending" is neither. An `NA` needs its one-sentence reason in the justification slot and drops out of every count. A tie in the preference is recorded as `none` with the reason; ties are not resolved by counting criteria at 4 or above, which would smuggle a total back in through the side door.

## Inter-rater reliability

**What to compute.** Simple agreement: the proportion of (text × criterion) cells where two judges assign the identical score, over the cells where both scored and neither marked `NA`. Report the raw fraction and the count, not a percentage alone. Also report the softer figure — the proportion within one point — because exact agreement is harsh on a five-point scale and the difference between 3/3 and 3/4 is not the same finding as 5/1.

**Why report it.** A single judge's profile is a report of one reading. Agreement is the only available estimate of how much of a difference between conditions could be judge noise, and it is also the instrument's own error bar: if two trained judges cannot agree on whether a text is agentic, a two-point gap between conditions on that axis means nothing. Reporting agreement is therefore not admin — it is the condition under which any other number in the results file may be read.

**Report no coefficient.** With two judges and ten texts, a chance-corrected statistic implies a precision the design cannot support and invites the reader to treat it as a property of the rubric. Raw agreement plus the list of disagreeing criteria is more useful and more honest.

**When judges differ by two points or more.**

1. Re-read the specific passage, independently, with the anchor open. Not the whole text — the passage, because the disagreement is almost always about which evidence counts.
2. State the evidence each judge used, in writing, one sentence each. Most large gaps resolve here, and the resolution is usually that one judge scored a different criterion (originality scored as strangeness, depth scored as likeability).
3. If the scores still differ, record both and mark the cell as disagreed. Do not average. An average of 2 and 4 is a 3 that no judge gave and that describes neither reading.
4. If a third judge is available, score the passage blind, and report their profile as a third reading rather than folding it into the other two.
5. Report the disagreement in the results file by criterion, with the reason. The pattern is a finding: judges who systematically split on originality are telling you the anchor is doing two jobs.

**What low agreement licenses.** Below roughly half the cells identical, treat the anchors as underspecified for this text type and fix the rubric before interpreting any condition difference. That threshold is a working convention, not a measurement, and it should be revised after the first session rather than defended.

## What this rubric cannot do

- **It cannot rank texts by literary merit.** It scores twelve properties chosen because one skill claims to move them, and a profile is not a verdict on whether a story is worth reading. Using the numbers to order texts is a misuse of the instrument, and it will order them by how much they resemble the skill's aesthetic.
- **It cannot see what a text does not attempt.** Comedy of voice, pastiche, the deliberately minor, the experimental work whose virtue is in a mode the criteria do not model — all of these score low and may be excellent. The `NA` mechanism covers a missing component, not an unmodelled ambition.
- **It cannot substitute for readers.** Two judges are a proxy for an audience, and a poor one: judges read for defects because that is their instruction, which is not how anyone reads for pleasure. Nothing here measures whether a text is remembered, quoted or reread.
- **It cannot support structural claims from short texts.** Per `../references/literary-critique.md`, a scene can show a symptom of a structural defect but not the structure. Coherence, structural integrity and temporal complexity are unreliable below roughly a full short story, and scores there should be reported as provisional.
- **It cannot be written to.** A text produced to satisfy these twelve descriptions is the checklist artifact the skill exists to prevent: every criterion present, every one inert. The rubric is an after-the-fact instrument; the moment it becomes a target, its scores rise and its meaning falls.

# Chapter 3

The clock said eleven minutes past. It had said eleven minutes past when she came up the stairs, and it would still be saying it when she went down. The key for it hung on a nail beside the door. The nail had worn a groove in the paint, so the key had hung there a long while, and nobody had ever taken it off.

Brandt kept one hand flat on the ledger, as though the book might get up.

Tessa came out from under the desk. She turned her head the long way round to bring her good eye onto Maren's boots, then lay down again with her chin on the floorboards and the bad eye running its usual water.

'Sit down, Maren.'

'I'll stand.'

He opened the ledger and turned it to her.

She knew the book by its shape before she knew it by its pages. Ships he had refused: name, flag, draught, and the reason, which he wrote out in full sentences, like a man drafting a letter to a magistrate. Two inks. Black for the refusal. Pencil in the last column, small, under the words he ruled at the top of every page in his own hand.

She put a finger on a line from the spring. Sixteen foot nine. *Over her marks for the berth.* Under it, in pencil, *04 07*.

The line below: *04 18*. The line below that: *03 52*. She went down the page with her finger. Every pencil figure sat eleven minutes before the black one beside it, and the black ones were the ones copied off the clock on the wall.

She turned back a page. Eleven minutes. She turned back four pages, to a month she remembered for rain, and it was eleven minutes there too.

'How far does it go back?'

'Eleven years,' he said. 'Not the book. The clock.'

The stove ticked. Out on the window ledge a gull came down and stayed, and Maren did not look at it, and Brandt did.

'You were new,' he said. 'You'd come off the bar at three in the morning with your hands shaking and you stood where you're standing and you asked me to leave it wrong. Eleven minutes. You said the tide tables and the office clock would never agree and you were tired of arguing with men who could read a table and not a bar.'

'I remember.'

'You said eleven because eleven was the most I could give you without the board noticing.'

She remembered the number, not the reason. The reason had been smaller than the number. It had been that she needed a margin she could hold in her head without arithmetic, and eleven was a number she could subtract in the dark.

She said, 'The tide doesn't read the clock.'

'No,' Brandt said. 'It doesn't.'

He turned the pages forward. October. The third, in black, in his full-sentence hand: *Sivash, of Odessa, refused. Draught on the marks and no margin at the top. Declined.* And under it in the last column, where the pencil went, nothing.

Nothing on the ninth either. The ninth had an entry, all right. Name, flag, draught, berth. Under the three ruled words, blank.

'You've left it empty.'

'I can't put a number in it. I wasn't on the quay.'

Maren looked at the blank place a while. It was a small place. In a book that size it was the size of a thumbnail, and it had the whole of her in it.

'She was down to her marks,' Brandt said. 'Sixteen foot ten at the harbour limit and the bar giving sixteen foot eight. I refused her on arithmetic. Then she came in anyway, and she didn't touch, and the only way she doesn't touch is that somebody took her over at the top of the water and counted the minutes by that clock.' He nodded at the wall without turning his head. 'So. What time did you take her over the bar?'

'Four.'

'Four exactly.'

'Four exactly.'

He took the pencil from behind his ear, licked it, and wrote *04 00* in the column. It looked wrong on the page. Every other figure in the book was eleven minutes out; this one was a bare hour, and it sat there like a stone in a bowl of eggs.

Then he said, 'What was in four?'

'Cotton.'

'Cotton doesn't put a ship down to her marks.'

'It does if there's enough of it.' She took her hand off the page. 'The manifest said cotton. The mate said cotton. I didn't open the hold. I'm not paid to open holds.'

Brandt looked at her for a moment longer than the answer deserved, and let it go, which was worse than pressing.

'Who paid you?'

The gull shifted on the ledge, one step, and settled.

'It came folded in the manifest,' she said. 'Envelope. No name on it. I didn't count it till I was home.'

'How much?'

She told him. He did the sum in his head, and she watched him do it, and watched him arrive at the same place she had arrived at in her kitchen with the money on the table.

'That's a roof,' she said. 'Near enough. Hers, at any rate.'

'That's not a roof,' he said.

'It's half a roof. The rest I had.'

He did not ask where the rest came from. He put the pencil down and squared the ledger with the edge of the table, and the sound of it was the sound of a man deciding not to say a thing.

'Sign it.'

She took the pen. Her left hand would hold a pen but not close on it, and the nib went where the hand went rather than where she wanted, so the signature came out as a shape rather than a name, the same shape it had been since the accident. It took her three strokes and a pause.

When she had finished she stayed bent over the book, and read what she had made. The date. The berth. *04 00*, in his hand. Her mark under it.

'Now it's a confession,' she said.

'Now it's a record.'

'It's the same page.'

'It's the same page,' he agreed, and closed the book, and did not lock it, and put it in the drawer under the tide tables where it always went.

She got her coat off the hook. The cold came up through the floor and into the hand first, the way it always did, and she put it in her pocket.

'Three of my mother's letters came this week,' she said. 'In her hand.'

Brandt waited.

'The fourth was typed.'

He took his glasses off and folded them and held them, which he did when he was thinking about a thing he did not like the shape of. 'Is she worse?'

'She doesn't type,' Maren said.

The stove ticked. Tessa sighed and turned over, and the bad eye caught the lamp and shone like a coin.

'Go home,' Brandt said. 'Go home and answer it.'

She went down the stairs with her right hand on the rail and her left in her pocket, and at the bottom she stopped, because the gull had come off the ledge and gone up onto the roof of the weighbridge shed, where it sat against the sky in the last of the light. She had gone the long way round that bird since she was a girl. She walked under it.

Behind her, up in the office, the pen started again.

## 3

The ledger lay open at the Sivash entry. Brandt had not turned to a blank page but to her page — the third of October, his hand, the refusal, three words: *draught not declared*. Below that, a gap where the ninth should have been, since he wrote only what he refused, and the ninth had not been refused.

She put her right hand flat on the table. The left stayed in her lap, where the cold got at it worst.

"You want me to write in your book," she said.

"I want you to write in it or leave it. Either one is a fact. It's the standing here I can't have."

The office smelled of stove, wet wool, and dog. Tessa lay under the desk with her head on Brandt's boot, one eye shut and one open, the open one turned up at nothing. When Maren shifted her chair the dog's ear moved before the sound, as though she heard the intention.

The clock above the door said twenty past four. The tide table in the window said nine minutes past.

Eleven minutes. She had known the number for years the way you know a loose board in your own stair. She had never once asked him to fix it. He had never once offered.

"Say it again," she said.

"That you came to me in the summer and asked me to put the office clock forward. You had a reason. You gave me one. I've had four months to decide whether I believed it."

"What reason did I give?"

"That the harbour was docking you for lateness — the clerks stamp your boarding sheets by my clock, your own watch runs true, and you were losing eleven minutes a trip."

"Was it costing me?"

"I never checked. That's the point of a favour. You don't audit it."

She looked at the gap between the two dates. Rain came at the window sideways, in the slow way it does when it has all night. A gull stood on the bollard outside, head down, doing nothing, and she made herself look back at the book. A gull on a bollard in the rain meant nothing. A gull on a windowsill meant something. She had never been able to say what, only that she had been right about it twice.

"Where did you get the entry for the third?"

"Wrote it the same day. I write them the same day."

"Draught not declared."

"Because it wasn't."

"And you'd have taken her if it had been."

He considered it without hurrying, which was the most irritating thing about him and also why the pilots trusted him. "No," he said. "I'd have written a different three words."

She laughed once, no air in it, and turned the ledger round with her right hand. The column above held eleven refusals since August, each in the same slant. She had always imagined the book as a kind of grudge. Now she saw it was a kind of memory, which was worse.

Her left hand had gone white at the knuckles in her lap. On the ninth she had come down the Sivash's ladder with her right hand only, the left hooked in her coat, and the rungs had been cold enough to take the skin off a palm that gripped too long.

"Who else knows?" she said.

"About the clock? Everyone. Nobody looks."

"About the ninth."

He tapped the gap with one finger. "A man came from the company on the eleventh, about the coal boat that went aground in the roads that morning. Insurance. He wanted the tide established, and my tide figure comes off my clock, so I gave him five foot nine. His master says five foot four, and there's a mark on the hull that agrees with the master."

"So either the tide lied or your clock did."

"My clock did," he said. "The tide has never once lied to me."

The stove ticked. Down the quay a winch started and stopped, somebody learning it.

"But I've had twelve days of being a man who signed a lie on a Monday and could still taste it on a Wednesday," he said. "I'd rather be a man who asks."

"So you waited until the book had a gap in it."

"So I waited until I could put it in front of you. That's not the same thing, but it's close, and I'll take close."

She picked up the pen. It was the office pen, the nib blunt, and her right hand did all the work while the left lay in her lap like something she had carried in for a stranger.

"Do I write my name or a statement?"

"Your name. The book doesn't take statements."

She wrote it — *M. Voss*, leaning away from the line because she was writing across the book instead of down it — and pushed it back.

Brandt read it, closed the ledger, and did not put it away. He left it on the table between them, which she understood was a courtesy and also a threat, the way a man leaves a gun where you can see it.

"Now ask me what you want to know," he said.

"I'm not going to ask you anything."

"You're going to ask me why I didn't take it to the board."

"All right. Why didn't you?"

"Because the board would have asked why my clock was fast, and I'd have had to say a woman asked me. Then they'd have asked what else I'd done for her, and I'd have sat down with forty years of my own book and wondered."

"That's not loyalty. That's bookkeeping."

"At my age it's the same thing."

She stood. Her knee did what it did in weather, and she put her right hand on the chair back, not the table edge, because the chair back was warm.

She took the letter out of her coat. It had come on the Thursday in a batch of four, all in one envelope. Three were in Ada's hand — the looping, pressed-hard hand that had written every letter of Maren's life — and the fourth was typewritten. Clean. Even margins. No signature, only *A.* at the foot, which Ada had never once done in sixty years.

She laid it on the ledger.

"Read the fourth one."

Brandt turned it right way up and read it the way he read everything, without moving his lips and without any expression she could use.

"It's about the garden. She's put in a row of something. She wants to know whether you'll come in the spring and help her lift the tubers." He turned it over. "There's nothing else on it."

"That's what's wrong with it." She took it back and folded it along its own creases, which were machine creases, straight, all four alike. "She's written me about that garden eleven times. She's had the same row of potatoes coming up since 1968. And she has never in her life sent a letter without a thumbprint of something on it — a smear, a tea ring, a word scratched out and put back. This one has nothing. Not a smudge. Whoever typed it didn't have her hands."

Brandt was quiet. Tessa got up from under the desk, stiff in the back end, and came and put her head against Maren's knee, and Maren gave her the back of her right hand to smell, because the dog could not see it.

"You think she's dead," he said. Not a question, and not unkind.

"I think somebody's answering her post."

"Then go inland and look."

"Not till the Sivash's business is done."

"The Sivash's business is a gap in a book."

She looked at the clock. It said twenty past five. On the water it was nine minutes past, and the light was going the way it went in November, all at once, like water down a drain. She thought about hold 4, which she had not opened, and had been careful not to smell, and which had made the ship sit an inch low at the head as she took her through the gate. She thought about the tin in her kitchen she had not counted and had not spent.

"Fix it," she said.

"What?"

"The clock. Tomorrow. Take the eleven minutes off it."

He looked at her a long moment, working out what she was asking: to make the ninth impossible again, and leave a ship in the log that could not have been there.

"I can't," he said.

"Why not."

"Because the first thing a man does when a clock gets mended is go back and check it against the book. Any man. The board, the dues clerk, the man from the company with his insurance. They'd have the ninth inside a week." He put one hand flat on the ledger, the way she had. "You asked me to make it wrong. That was the whole of it. Now it's wrong and there's a name in it, and the only thing saving you is that nobody's looking. So we don't fix it. We let it run."

She stood in the doorway with the letter in her coat and the cold coming up through the boards. The gull was still on the bollard. It turned its head and looked at her, one eye, then the other, and she went out before it could decide anything.

Behind her, Brandt said, to the dog or to the room, "Eleven minutes. Eleven minutes and a woman's whole life in it."

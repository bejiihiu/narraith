"Sixty-three's empty," he said. "If you want to turn round in it."

She had one end of the box on the boot sill and the other against her hip. "The seats are down."

"I can see the seats are down."

"The man in the shop said it goes in a hatchback."

"The man in the shop was selling wardrobes."

He set his trolley against the kerb and took the far corner of the box and lifted. She let him. They got it level with the opening, and then it stopped.

"The parcel shelf," she said.

"That comes out."

"I know it comes out."

He took it out. The box went another hand's width and stopped again. His forearm was against hers through the cardboard, and neither of them moved it away.

"Front seats forward," he said. "Right forward. Then it drops down behind."

She went round and did it. He held the box. When she came back the boot was full and the tailgate wouldn't close by four inches.

"That's a strap job," he said.

"I've got a strap."

"Have you."

"In the wheel well. Under the jack."

He nodded at the trolley. Two bags in it, and a four-pack of the lager she used to have on a Friday.

"Have you far to go?"

"Twenty minutes. The ring road."

"Right."

A car came past looking for a space, went the length of the row, and came back, and went out again. He turned his key over in his hand.

"Number forty-one's got a skip outside it," he said. "Whole front's coming out. They've taken the hedge down."

"That was a good hedge."

"It was."

"It was a good hedge," she said again.

He looked at the trolley.

"Do you want a hand with the strap?"

"I can do a strap."

"I know."

She got the strap out and fed it through the anchor and pulled it and it held. He stood with his hands on the trolley handle. Then he took the four-pack out and put it on the passenger seat of her car.

"That's not—"

"It's not cold. Put it in the sink when you get in."

She got in. He pushed the trolley away toward the store, and stopped, and came back, and knocked on the glass, and she wound it down.

"The end one," he said. "Under the laurel. There's a length of the old fence still in there. We did that run in a weekend. You had the wrong nails."

"They were galvanised."

"They were the wrong ones."

"Right," she said.

"I'm not digging it out."

"No."

"Right," he said. "Well."

He went off with the trolley. She sat with the engine running and the four-pack on the seat beside her, and then she put her hand flat on the top of it, and drove home with the strap humming.

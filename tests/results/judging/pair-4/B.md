"Your wheel's gone."

He looked up. She was standing at the end of the next bay with a basket on her hip instead of a trolley, which was new.

"Gone how."

"Sideways." She pointed with her chin. "Look at it. It's going one way and you're going the other."

Terry crouched. The front left caster sat at an angle, like it had been thinking about leaving for a while.

"Third one," he said.

"They make them like that now."

"They don't make them like that."

"I'm not saying it's a conspiracy. I'm saying it's a known thing."

A car crawled past, considered the space he wasn't taking, and moved on.

"You're in the far corner," he said.

"Shade."

"There's no shade in the far corner. There's a hedge."

"I like the hedge."

He put the last bag in the boot and closed it with his elbow, the way he did when his hands were full, and she watched him do it, and he watched her watch him.

"Six bags," he said.

"Freezer."

"For one."

"It's a large freezer." She shifted the basket. The sleeve of her coat pulled up, and there it was, white plastic on her wrist, a printed band with her name on it and a barcode, the corner already curling. She pulled the sleeve back down without looking at him.

He looked at the middle distance.

"You want to get that off before it itches," he said.

"It's fine."

"Tape goes funny. Leaves a mark."

"I'll take it off in the car."

"Right."

Two women came out with a trolley between them. The doors breathed and shut.

He nodded at her basket. "One of everything."

"Small shop."

"Ready meal and a bottle of the Rioja."

"I'm not cooking for anyone."

He was quiet a moment. "Two steaks in mine."

"Two."

"One's for Thursday."

"It's Tuesday."

"It keeps."

She looked at his trolley, at the front where he'd stacked things, and he turned it slightly so the yoghurts went round the side. Too late. She'd seen them. Her yoghurts, the four-pack she'd eaten every morning for eleven years and which he'd said tasted like a hedge.

"Those'll go off," she said.

"Thursday."

"You'll be eating them Thursday with a fork. Standing up. Over the sink."

"That's the plan."

A trolley boy went past collecting, and they both waited him out, and the wind moved a crisp packet along the kerb between them.

"Your mother's chair's still in the garage," he said.

"I know."

"It's a good chair."

"It's a chair."

"You could have it collected."

"I could."

Neither of them moved.

She stepped back. The trolley rolled a slow foot away from him on its bad wheel and stopped by itself.

"Take care of the—" she said, and stopped, and put her hand flat on the handle.

"Take care of yourself," she said.

"You too."

She reversed out badly. Three goes at it, the way she always had, and he stood with his keys in his fist until the car was just a shape at the lights. Then he got in, and sat with the engine off, and the shopping going warm in the back.

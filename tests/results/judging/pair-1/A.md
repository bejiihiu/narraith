# A Good Lock

Three weeks of post lay on the mat inside the door. Ray could see the corner of it in the gap, white gone grey along the fold.

The smell coming out of the letterbox was not the smell he had come prepared for. Cold, dust, something older than dust. Nothing else. He set his bag down and straightened up.

"You'll want to stand back," he said.

The agent had his folder open against his chest, the way they do, as though the paperwork were a coat. "It's straightforward. The tenancy's in arrears, the landlord's had no contact since the ninth, and we've a viewing Friday."

"Friday."

"The flat has to be fit to show."

Ray looked at the door. A Union, five-lever, older than the frame it sat in, the paint over the escutcheon gone amber with age. "That's a good lock," he said. "Twenty year in that door and nobody's had a go at it."

The woman came up the last four steps and stood at the head of the stairs, and the folder came down an inch.

"You're not going in there."

"Ms Aldridge." The agent had the voice for it already, the one they keep for the ones who won't leave. "We've been through it. You're not the tenant."

"She's my sister."

"How long?" Ray said.

"Twenty-two days."

He got the pick gun out of the bag and turned it over in his hand. Twenty-two days.

It was the usual. Their voices went on above him, and he had heard it on other landings, in other buildings, with the same tune in it and the same worn step where everybody stopped. He took his time with the tension wrench. There was no hurry in him and no argument either.

"You can't just change the locks on her things."

"There are procedures. If you want access, apply for it. I'm not the court."

"And what if she's in there?"

The agent did not answer that. Ray noticed.

"She's not in there," Ray said. "You'd know. You'd smell it from the street." He set the pick gun and the barrel gave, and he turned it with two fingers and the door came off the latch and stood a half inch open, and the flat breathed out onto the landing, cold and dry and old. "There. That's your door."

He stepped back. He did that every time and had never once had to explain it. Some of them took it for superstition. It wasn't. A man who goes into other people's rooms for a living starts carrying them about, and Ray had enough of that.

"Go on, then," he said to the agent.

"I'm not going in first."

"You're the one with the folder."

The agent looked at the folder, and then at the door, and then, briefly and without meaning to, at the woman.

"Five minutes," Ray said. "I'll give you five minutes and then I'm changing the lock, in or out." He stood back from the doorway and held the door with two fingers so it would not swing.

She went past him without looking at him. The hall was narrow and dim, with a strip of light at the far end where the kitchen was, and he heard her stop. He heard her stop and not start again, and the quiet went on long enough that he took one step over the threshold, and then another, and looked down the length of the hall.

The kitchen was tidy. The table under the window had two mugs on it. One was full, or had been, the tea gone to a grey skin, the skin cracked dry at the rim. The other was upside down on the draining board, washed and put away, dry as a bone.

The woman stood with her back to him, looking at them.

"She never washed up," she said. "Not once. She'd leave it and say she'd do it in the morning."

Ray looked at the two cups. He looked at the chair pulled out and the chair pushed in. Then at the window, shut, and the catch turned.

"Two cups," he said.

She turned round. "What?"

"That's what I see. Two cups."

"You saw it. You saw the same as me."

"I see a lot of things," Ray said. "I'm a locksmith."

He went back out onto the landing and got the new barrel from the bag and took the old one down. It came out sweet, no burring on the follower, and he laid it on the mat with the screws in a row. The brass went home first time.

"It's a good lock," he said, and turned the key, and the bolt went across as smooth as anything he had ever fitted.

He gave the agent the keys, two on a ring and a spare in a paper sleeve. The agent put them in his coat pocket. "I'll have the clearance people in Monday," he said, to the folder.

The woman came out of the flat and stood at the head of the stairs with her hands at her sides.

"Will you say something?" she said. "To someone. Anyone."

Ray zipped his bag and said, "Mind how you go," and went down.

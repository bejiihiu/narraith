The building had four floors and a stairwell that smelled of other people's breakfasts. Hollis carried his case up past a bicycle chained to the rail and a door with a strip of tape across the letterbox, and on the fourth floor a woman was waiting in a good coat with the collar turned up, holding a plastic bag of post against her chest as though it might get away from her.

"Mr Hollis. Thank you for coming."

"Miss Deane."

"Yes." She had the look of someone who had rehearsed being calm. "It's this one."

The door was the ordinary kind, painted the same green as the others, with a brass number that had lost most of its brass. He set the case down and looked at the lock the way he looked at any lock, which was the way other men looked at a face.

"Mortice deadlock," he said. "Five levers. It's a decent one."

"My sister was careful about locks."

"She was right to be."

He knelt. The sister — Miss Deane, he corrected himself; she had not said Mrs — stood back against the wall and watched him take out the pick and the tension wrench, and he could feel her watching, so he worked slower than he needed to, the way he always did when someone was watching, because slowness looked like care and care was most of the trade.

It took him four minutes. Near the end the levers fell into line and he felt it in his fingers before he heard it, and then the click, small and final, like a held breath let go.

"There," he said, and stood, and turned the handle.

The door did not move.

He tried again. It gave a quarter inch and stopped. He put his eye to the gap and looked, and then he stood back and looked at the door for a while.

"There's a bolt," he said. "Thrown from the inside."

The sister did not say anything.

"A mortice bolt. It isn't part of the lock, it goes into the frame." He heard himself explaining it too carefully. "I can't pick that. It has to be drilled."

"Drilled."

"It'll be a mess. I'd rather not, but there's no other way in."

She looked at the door. Then she said, "Do it," and he heard that she'd had to find the word.

He got the drill out of the case. He laid a cloth down to catch the swarf, because he was that kind of man, and put the bit against the frame beside the bolt housing and started, and the noise went down the stairwell and into the other flats, and he thought about the people behind those other doors hearing it and deciding not to come out.

It took eleven minutes. The housing split and the bolt fell back and the door swung in an inch, and he caught it with his knee. He stood a moment with his hand flat on the paint.

The flat was cold. That was the first thing. The heating had been off long enough for the cold to get into the walls, and the air that came out at him was still and dry and smelled of dust and old lemon and nothing else. No smell at all, which after three weeks was something.

The post was on the mat. A lot of it. He stepped over it and she stepped over it after him, and he heard her breath go in and stay there.

He walked the flat the way he walked any flat, without touching anything. Hallway: coats on the hooks, one coat, a summer thing, hung straight. Kitchen: a plate on the counter with a knife and fork crossed on it, clean and dry. A mug with a dark ring inside, the tea gone to a tide-line and set. A calendar still turned to October, and a bin bag tied at the neck and set by the door, waiting to go out.

The bedroom. The bed was made. A glass of water on the nightstand with a skin on it. The wardrobe hung with clothes and a suitcase on the floor of it, empty, unzipped, waiting too.

The bathroom. A towel folded over the radiator, gone stiff. A toothbrush standing in a cup, dry as a bone. The mirror with a smudge on it at the height of a face.

He came back out. The sister was standing in the kitchen with her bag of post still against her chest, looking at the mug.

"Anything?" she said.

"No," he said. "It's just a flat."

She put the bag down on the counter, very carefully, as if it were the last thing she had. Then she pulled out the chair and sat down in it, and he understood that the chair had been pulled out like that for three weeks, and that she was sitting in it now because there was nowhere else to sit and nothing else to do.

"She'd have taken the bin down," she said.

"Yes," Hollis said.

He said, "I'll need to fit a new cylinder. The old keys—"

"Yes."

"I'll do it now, if you want. It's not much."

"Do it now."

So he did. He took the old cylinder out and put a new one in, and he was careful with the screws, and it was good work, and while he worked she sat with her hands flat on the table. When he was done he gave her two keys on a ring. She took them and held them in her fist and did not put them down.

He packed the case. At the door he said, "I'm sorry," which was not something he usually said, and she said, "Thank you," and then, when he had the door open, "Mr Hollis."

"Yes."

"You won't say about the bolt."

He looked at her.

"No," he said.

He went out onto the landing. The door closed behind him and he heard the new lock turn, and then a pause, and then the bolt, the new bolt, going home into the frame, and the sound of it was exactly the same.

He stood there longer than he needed to. Then he went down the stairs, past the bicycle and the tape on the letterbox and the smell of somebody's toast, and out into the cold, and sat in the van with his hands on the wheel and did not start it.

In the book on the seat beside him he wrote the address and the date, and in the column for the work he wrote: opened.
